## HOOK (0:00 – ~1:15)

[ARCHIVE: dark painting of a battlefield at dusk, bodies, Roman standards in the mud — slow push-in]
On a summer afternoon in the year two hundred and sixteen B.C., on a dusty plain in southern Italy, the Roman Republic lost somewhere between fifty and seventy thousand men. In a single afternoon. It remains one of the bloodiest days of fighting in the history of the world.

[ARCHIVE: Roman senators in the Curia, 19th-century painting]
Rome had brought the largest army it had ever put into the field. Eight legions. Around eighty-six thousand men. Its commanders were certain that sheer weight of numbers would finally crush the invader who had been humiliating them for two years.

[ARCHIVE: marble bust of Hannibal (Naples) — slow rotate / Ken Burns]
The invader was a thirty-one-year-old Carthaginian named Hannibal Barca. He was outnumbered almost two to one, fighting in enemy territory, a thousand miles from home, with no reinforcements coming.

[ARCHIVE: 'Hannibal swearing enmity to Rome' painting — boy at the altar]
As a boy of nine, he had sworn an oath to his father never to be a friend of Rome. As a man, he became the most dangerous enemy the Romans would ever face. Centuries later, military thinkers would call him the Father of Strategy.

[MAP: hook-march | Western Mediterranean: Carthage, Rome, New Carthage in Spain; red line of Hannibal's march over the Pyrenees and the Alps into Italy]
In two hundred and eighteen B.C., he did what no one in Rome believed possible. He marched an army out of Spain, over the Pyrenees, across the Rhône, and straight over the Alps in the approach to winter, with thirty-seven war elephants, and came down into Italy from the north.

[ARCHIVE: Turner, 'Snow Storm: Hannibal and his Army Crossing the Alps' (1812)]
He arrived with perhaps twenty thousand infantry and six thousand cavalry. Rome could raise ten times that. And yet, over the next two years, he would destroy three Roman armies, one after another. But what exactly set him apart from every other general of his age? Let's take a closer look at Hannibal's three greatest tactical moves.

## MOVE 1 — THE TREBIA: THE TRAP IN THE STREAM BED (~1:15 – ~5:45)

[MAP: trebia-1 | Northern Italy, Po valley. Title card MOVE 1 · THE TREBIA · DECEMBER 218 BC. Labels: PLACENTIA, R. PO, R. TREBIA. Carthaginian camp west of the river, Roman camp east]
December, two hundred and eighteen B.C. The plain of the river Po, in northern Italy. Hannibal's exhausted army has survived the Alps, but only just. His men are frozen, underfed, and far from home. Across the river Trebia, a Roman consular army is camped near the town of Placentia.

[MAP: trebia-1b | Small inset: skirmish at the river Ticinus a few weeks earlier; Roman cavalry arrow repulsed; consul Scipio's plaque with a 'WOUNDED' tag]
Only weeks earlier, Hannibal's cavalry had already given Rome a warning. In a clash near the river Ticinus, his Numidian horsemen had routed the Roman cavalry, and the other consul, Publius Cornelius Scipio, had been badly wounded. Scipio now urged caution. Wait for spring, he said. Let the Carthaginians starve through the winter. His colleague had no intention of waiting.

[MAP: trebia-2 | Roman counters stack up: 36,000 INFANTRY, 4,000 CAVALRY. Consul plaque: TIBERIUS SEMPRONIUS LONGUS. Carthaginian counters: ~20,000 INFANTRY, ~10,000 CAVALRY, ELEPHANTS]
The Roman commander is the consul Tiberius Sempronius Longus. He has around thirty-six thousand infantry and four thousand cavalry, fresh, well supplied, and eager for battle. His term in office is running out, and he wants the glory of beating Hannibal before his successor arrives.

[MAP: trebia-3 | Camera holds on the Roman camp; a speech bubble from Sempronius: "ONE BATTLE WILL END IT"]
Sempronius believed the equation was simple. The Carthaginians were tired, they were few, and Roman legions had beaten every army in Italy. All he needed was a fair fight on open ground. Line against line, the legion would win.

[MAP: trebia-4 | Camera pushes in on a narrow stream bed between the two camps; it highlights in white, then Mago's plaque drops in: MAGO · 1,000 INFANTRY + 1,000 CAVALRY; the counters slide into the stream and fade to half-hidden]
But Hannibal saw something different. Between the two camps, the flat plain was cut by a small watercourse with steep banks, overgrown with brambles and thorns. From the Roman side it looked like nothing at all. Hannibal saw a hiding place. The night before the battle, he gave his younger brother Mago two thousand picked men, a thousand foot and a thousand horse, and ordered them to lie in that stream bed and wait.

[MAP: trebia-5 | Dawn. Snow particles on screen. Numidian cavalry arrow crosses the Trebia toward the Roman camp, then turns back; Roman arrows follow across the river; caption: SLEET · ICY RIVER · NO BREAKFAST]
At dawn, in freezing sleet, Hannibal sent his Numidian light cavalry across the river to throw javelins into the Roman camp. It was an insult Sempronius could not ignore. He ordered his entire army out, before the men had eaten, and sent them wading through the Trebia, swollen with winter rain and running chest-deep and ice-cold.

[MAP: trebia-6 | Carthaginian camp: warm glow, caption: FED · WARM · OILED]
On the other side, Hannibal's men had eaten a hot meal by their fires and rubbed their bodies with oil against the cold. When the Romans climbed out of the river, soaked and shaking and hungry, the Carthaginian army was waiting for them, rested and in formation.

[MAP: trebia-7 | Lines form: Roman infantry center vs Carthaginian infantry center; Carthaginian cavalry and elephants on both wings; wing arrows push the Roman cavalry back]
The two infantry lines crashed together in the center, and for a while the Roman legions held. But on the wings, Hannibal's cavalry outnumbered the Romans more than two to one. They drove the Roman horse from the field, and the elephants and light troops began to press the flanks of the exposed legions.

[MAP: trebia-8 | Mago's counters burst out of the stream bed; red-blue arrow hits the Roman rear; the Roman block turns grey at the edges]
And then Mago rose out of the stream bed. Two thousand fresh men struck the Roman army in the rear. Attacked from the front, both flanks, and now from behind, the Roman formation began to come apart.

[MAP: trebia-9 | A single Roman column punches through the Carthaginian center and escapes north to Placentia; the rest of the Roman counters fade to grey; stats card: ~10,000 ESCAPE · 15,000+ KILLED · THOUSANDS CAPTURED]
About ten thousand legionaries in the center kept their discipline, cut straight through the Carthaginian line, and escaped to Placentia. Almost everyone else was killed, drowned in the river, or captured, at least fifteen thousand dead by most estimates. Hannibal's own losses were light, most of them among his Gallic allies.

[MAP: trebia-10 | Method card appears over the map: CHOOSE THE GROUND · CHOOSE THE MOMENT · TURN THE ENEMY'S STRENGTH AGAINST HIM]
This was Hannibal's method in miniature. He chose the ground: a flat plain with one hidden fold in it. He chose the moment: dawn, in sleet, on an empty stomach. And he turned the enemy's strength against him: Roman aggression, the very thing that made the legions so formidable, dragged them through an icy river and into a trap.

[ARCHIVE: Roman coin / engraving of a Roman consul — transition]
A full Roman consular army had been shattered. But Rome still had more armies, and new consuls eager to succeed where Sempronius had failed. There's a great deal more of this kind of tactical breakdown for anyone who subscribes, because just six months later, Hannibal would pull off what may be the largest ambush in military history.

## MOVE 2 — LAKE TRASIMENE: AN ARMY SWALLOWED BY FOG (~5:45 – ~10:30)

[MAP: trasimene-1 | Central Italy. Title card MOVE 2 · LAKE TRASIMENE · JUNE 217 BC. Hannibal's route through the Arno marshes; Flaminius at Arretium]
Spring, two hundred and seventeen B.C. Rome has elected new consuls, and one of them, Gaius Flaminius, is waiting at Arretium to block the road south. Hannibal does not take the road. He marches through the flooded marshes of the Arno instead, four days and three nights through water, losing most of his remaining elephants and, from infection, the sight of one eye.

[MAP: trasimene-2 | Hannibal's army appears behind Flaminius, burning farms; Flaminius's counters swing around to follow]
Suddenly, the Carthaginian army is behind Flaminius, burning the farms of central Italy under his nose. Flaminius is a proud and popular politician. He cannot stand by while his countrymen's land is looted. He sets off in pursuit, determined to catch Hannibal and force a battle.

[MAP: trasimene-3 | Lake Trasimene: narrow strip of flat shore between the lake and a ring of hills; a single road along the shore]
Flaminius believed he was the hunter. His scouts told him the Carthaginians were marching ahead along the northern shore of Lake Trasimene. The road there runs along a narrow strip of flat land, with the lake on one side and a curving line of hills on the other.

[MAP: trasimene-4 | Carthaginian counters placed along the hills, hidden (faded); heavy infantry on the rise at the far exit; cavalry at the entrance]
But Hannibal saw something different. That narrow shore was not a road. It was a corridor, and a corridor has walls. He placed his heavy infantry on a rise blocking the far end. He hid his light troops and his Gauls along the wooded hills above the shore. And he sent his cavalry around behind the entrance, out of sight, to close the door once the Romans were inside.

[MAP: trasimene-5 | Morning fog overlay rolls in from the lake; the Roman column (long thin red line) marches into the corridor]
On the morning of the twenty-first of June, a thick fog rose off the lake and filled the valley. Flaminius marched his army into the corridor in a long column, without proper scouting, unable to see more than a few paces in front of him.

[MAP: trasimene-6 | Signal: all Carthaginian counters snap to full opacity; arrows pour down from the hills onto the column from the flank, front and rear]
When the whole Roman column was inside, Hannibal gave the signal. From the hills above, out of the fog, his army charged down on the Romans from the front, the flank, and the rear, all at once. The legions never had time to form a battle line. Many men were cut down still in marching order.

[MAP: trasimene-7 | The Roman column fragments; some counters are pushed into the lake and fade; Flaminius's plaque falls; stats card: ~15,000 KILLED · ~15,000 CAPTURED · CONSUL FLAMINIUS KILLED]
Flaminius himself was killed in the fighting. Thousands of Romans were driven into the lake and drowned under the weight of their armor. In about three hours, roughly fifteen thousand Romans were dead and another fifteen thousand taken prisoner. Hannibal lost somewhere between fifteen hundred and twenty-five hundred men.

[ARCHIVE: Roman Forum crowd scene, 19th-century engraving]
In Rome, crowds gathered in the Forum waiting for news. At last a magistrate climbed the speaker's platform and said only this: "We have been defeated in a great battle." According to Livy, there was nothing more to add.

[MAP: trasimene-7b | Captured allied counters change color from red to neutral and walk off the map toward their home cities]
Then Hannibal did something just as calculated as the ambush itself. He kept the Roman citizens as prisoners, but he released the Italian allies without ransom, telling them he had come to fight Rome, not them. Rome's power rested on its network of allied cities. Hannibal was trying to break it, one act of mercy at a time.

[MAP: trasimene-8 | Method card: CHOOSE THE GROUND · CHOOSE THE MOMENT · TURN THE ENEMY'S STRENGTH AGAINST HIM]
Once again, the same method. He chose the ground: a corridor between a lake and a line of hills. He chose the moment: a fog that blinded the enemy and hid his own men. And he turned the enemy's strength against him: Flaminius's pride and his eagerness to fight led him straight into the trap.

[ARCHIVE: engraving of Quintus Fabius Maximus / Roman senate in crisis]
Rome was stunned. The Senate appointed a dictator, Fabius Maximus, who refused to fight Hannibal in open battle at all. But the Roman people hated waiting. The following summer, they sent out the largest army in their history to end the war in a single day. It would become Hannibal's masterpiece.

## MOVE 3 — CANNAE: THE PERFECT ENCIRCLEMENT (~10:30 – ~15:45)

[MAP: cannae-1 | Apulia, southern Italy. Title card MOVE 3 · CANNAE · 2 AUGUST 216 BC. River Aufidus, town of Cannae; the two armies on the plain]
The second of August, two hundred and sixteen B.C. The plain beside the river Aufidus, near the village of Cannae in southern Italy. Facing Hannibal are the consuls Lucius Aemilius Paullus and Gaius Terentius Varro, with eight Roman legions and their allies.

[MAP: cannae-2 | Counters: ROME ~80,000 INFANTRY, ~6,000 CAVALRY vs CARTHAGE ~40,000 INFANTRY, ~10,000 CAVALRY]
Around eighty thousand Roman infantry and six thousand cavalry face roughly forty thousand infantry and ten thousand cavalry. The Romans outnumber Hannibal by almost two to one.

[MAP: cannae-3 | The Roman infantry forms an unusually deep, narrow block in the center; arrow shows its intended punch through the middle]
The Romans believed they had learned from their defeats. This time there would be no ambush, no fog, no hidden stream bed, just a wide open plain. And they would use their numbers the way Romans always had. They packed their infantry into an unusually deep and narrow formation, a human battering ram designed to smash straight through the Carthaginian center.

[MAP: cannae-4 | Hannibal's line: Gauls and Spaniards bulge forward in a crescent in the center; Libyan heavy infantry held back on both ends; Hasdrubal's heavy cavalry on the left wing, Numidians on the right]
But Hannibal saw something different. He placed his least reliable troops, the Gauls and Spaniards, in the center, and pushed them forward in a crescent that bulged toward the Roman line. On both ends of that crescent, held back and out of the way, he placed his best soldiers, the veteran African infantry. And on the wings he placed his cavalry, led on the left by his officer Hasdrubal.

[MAP: cannae-4b | Wind arrows (VOLTURNUS) blow across the plain from behind the Carthaginian lines into the Roman faces; sun position marked; Hannibal and Mago plaques placed in the center of the crescent]
He even thought about the weather. The ancient sources say a hot, dusty wind called the Volturnus was blowing across the plain that day, straight into the faces of the Romans. And Hannibal did not command from a safe distance. He and his brother Mago took their place in the center, with the Gauls and Spaniards, exactly where the Roman blow would land hardest, because that was the part of the line that could not be allowed to break.

[MAP: cannae-5 | Hasdrubal's cavalry arrow smashes the Roman cavalry on the river side, then sweeps behind the Roman army toward the other wing]
The battle began on the wings. Hasdrubal's heavy cavalry crushed the Roman horsemen pinned against the river, then rode right around the back of the Roman army and fell on the allied cavalry on the other flank, which broke and fled.

[MAP: cannae-6 | The Roman block pushes forward; the Carthaginian crescent bends back slowly into a concave arc; the Roman mass follows it inward]
In the center, the Roman battering ram struck the Carthaginian crescent. And the crescent gave way, slowly, step by step, just as Hannibal had planned. It bent backwards, from a bulge into a hollow. The Romans surged forward into the gap, sure that the center was breaking, packing themselves tighter and tighter.

[MAP: cannae-7 | The African infantry on both ends turns inward and hits both Roman flanks; Hasdrubal's cavalry closes the rear; the circle is complete; the Roman block compresses]
Then Hannibal sprang the trap. The African veterans on both ends of the line turned inward and struck the Roman flanks. And Hasdrubal's cavalry, returning from its ride around the battlefield, slammed into the Roman rear. The Roman army was surrounded, on all four sides, by a force half its size.

[MAP: cannae-7b | Close-up on the bent center: the crescent holds, thin but unbroken; Hannibal's plaque in the middle]
Everything depended on that bending center not snapping. If the Gauls and Spaniards had broken and run, the Roman column would have split Hannibal's army in two, and Cannae would be remembered as his last battle. Instead they gave ground slowly and stayed together, with their general among them, until the moment the trap was ready to close.

[MAP: cannae-8 | The Roman block turns grey from the outside in; stats card: 48,000–70,000 ROMANS KILLED · CONSUL AEMILIUS PAULLUS KILLED · ~6,000 CARTHAGINIAN LOSSES]
Packed so tightly that many could not even raise their weapons, the Romans were cut down from the outside in. By nightfall, somewhere between forty-eight thousand and seventy thousand Romans lay dead, among them the consul Aemilius Paullus and some eighty men of senatorial rank. Hannibal lost around six thousand.

[MAP: cannae-9 | Method card: CHOOSE THE GROUND · CHOOSE THE MOMENT · TURN THE ENEMY'S STRENGTH AGAINST HIM]
It was the purest form of the method. He chose the ground: a plain narrow enough, between a river and the hills, that the Romans could not use their numbers to outflank him. He chose the moment: he waited until the Roman center was committed and packed tight. And he turned the enemy's strength against him: the more the Roman mass pushed forward, the deeper it drove itself into the trap.

## ENDING (~15:45 – ~17:30)

[ARCHIVE: painting of the aftermath of Cannae / Roman mothers mourning]
Remember that summer afternoon we began with? The one where Rome lost more men in a single day than many nations lose in an entire war. This was it. Cannae. For centuries afterwards, it would be the battle every general wanted to fight.

[ARCHIVE: Maharbal and Hannibal engraving; then a map of Italy with Rome unconquered]
And yet, Hannibal never took Rome. After Cannae, his cavalry commander Maharbal is said to have told him, "You know how to win a victory, Hannibal, but you do not know how to use one." Rome refused to surrender, refused to negotiate, and simply raised new armies.

[ARCHIVE: bust of Scipio Africanus; painting of the Battle of Zama]
In the end, it was a Roman who had survived Cannae, Publius Cornelius Scipio, who studied Hannibal's methods, carried the war to Africa, and finally defeated him at Zama in two hundred and two B.C. Hannibal's greatest student was the man who beat him.

[ARCHIVE: engraving of Hannibal in exile / his death by poison]
Hannibal spent the rest of his life in exile, advising foreign kings against Rome, always one step ahead of Roman agents. Around one hundred and eighty-three B.C., with Roman envoys demanding that he be handed over, the old general took poison rather than be captured. He was in his sixties, and he had never stopped being Rome's enemy.

[ARCHIVE: 20th-century generals studying maps; photo of Eisenhower (public domain, US Army)]
But his influence long outlived Carthage. Two thousand years later, German planners still studied Cannae. And a remark attributed to Dwight Eisenhower sums up its legacy: every ground commander seeks the battle of annihilation, and tries to duplicate, as far as conditions permit, the classic example of Cannae.
