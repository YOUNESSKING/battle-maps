## Hook

[MAP: hook-1 | Nijmegen close map, first frame: 26 blue boat counters on the south bank of the Waal, red guns + MG nests on the north bank and the bridges; odds card 260 MEN · 26 CANVAS BOATS vs GUNS ON THE FAR BANK; boats push off, tracers, impacts in the water (shake)] Twenty-six canvas boats. Two hundred and sixty men. A river three hundred metres wide, in broad daylight, with German guns waiting on the far bank. There were not enough paddles, so some of the men rowed with the butts of their rifles.

[MAP: hook-2 | white flash-forward (the one zoom sound): north end of the road bridge turns blue, British tank counters roll across the bridge, stamp BEFORE DARK: THE BRIDGE WAS THEIRS] The general who sent them knew exactly what he was asking. Before dark that evening, British tanks were rolling across the great bridge at Nijmegen, the bridge his division had been fighting for since the day they landed.

[MAP: hook-3 | Europe map: four parachute marks pop in one after another: SICILY JUL 1943, SALERNO SEP 1943, NORMANDY JUN 1944, HOLLAND SEP 1944; Gavin badge; caption 4 COMBAT JUMPS IN 14 MONTHS; then the three move titles] His name was James Gavin. In fourteen months he jumped into battle four times: Sicily, Salerno, Normandy and Holland. No other American general made four combat jumps in the war. He carried an M1 rifle, like a private. And three times, when a battle was slipping away, he turned it around. These are James Gavin's three greatest tactical moves.

## Who he was

[ARCHIVE: bio-1 | Gavin portraits (public domain US Army): James_M._Gavin.jpg, Gavin 1945] He was born in Brooklyn in 1907 and spent his first years in an orphanage, until a coal-mining family in Pennsylvania adopted him. At seventeen he enlisted in the army as a private, a skinny kid from a mining town with little schooling. He studied at night, passed the exams, and won a place at West Point, graduating in 1929.

[ARCHIVE: bio-2 | US paratroopers training / C-47 jump photos 1942-43 (public domain)] When America began building its first parachute units in 1941, Gavin volunteered at once. He threw himself into this new way of war, studied the German parachute attacks of the war so far, and wrote the army's first manual on how airborne troops should fight. His men called him Slim Jim, and later, Jumpin' Jim. By the summer of 1943, at just thirty-six, Colonel Gavin commanded the five hundred and fifth Parachute Infantry Regiment, about three thousand of the toughest volunteers in the army.

## Move 1

[MAP: move1-1 | Sicily wide map: title card MOVE 1 · BIAZZA RIDGE · 11 JULY 1943; invasion beaches (Gela, Scoglitti), 505th drop zone arrow onto the high ground behind Gela (Piano Lupo); transport plane icons fly in at night] Move one: Biazza Ridge. July 1943. The Allies were about to invade Sicily, their first step back into Europe. A fleet of thousands of ships would put American, British and Canadian armies ashore on the southern beaches. Gavin's regiment, more than three thousand paratroopers, would go in first, jumping in the dark the night before the landings. Their task was to seize the high ground behind the beaches at Gela and stop German tanks from reaching the shore.

[MAP: move1-2 | night; transport planes scatter, parachute marks spread all over south-east Sicily; caption HIGH WINDS · LOST PILOTS · SCATTERED DROP; Gavin badge with a handful of men far from the drop zone] It went wrong from the start. High winds and lost pilots scattered the drop across southern Sicily, some men dozens of miles from their targets. Gavin himself came down miles from where he should have been, with only a handful of troopers. For a whole day his little group moved through the hills toward the sound of the guns, fighting small skirmishes, with no idea where the rest of his regiment had gone.

[MAP: move1-3 | Hermann Göring Division counters + Tiger company north of the beaches; red arrows south toward the coast; speech bubble THROW THEM BACK INTO THE SEA (no quote marks); landing ships offshore] Waiting inland was the Hermann Goering Division, one of the strongest German formations on the island, with a company of Tiger tanks. The Tiger was the most feared tank in the world: more than fifty tons of armour, with an eighty-eight millimetre gun. The German plan was simple: drive south, smash into the landing beaches, and throw the Americans back into the sea before they could organise. Against lightly armed paratroopers, scattered across the countryside, it should have been easy.

[MAP: move1-4 | Biazza close map (test scene look): Route 115, Ponte Dirillo, the ridge pulses; caption THE HIGH GROUND ABOVE THE BEACHES; Gavin badge] Gavin saw something different. His scattered men could never stop tanks in open country. But the road to the American beaches ran past one piece of high ground: Biazza Ridge. It overlooked the bridge at Ponte Dirillo and the road the German tanks needed. Whoever held it could see, and shoot, for miles. If the Germans took it, the way to the beaches was open.

[MAP: move1-5 | dawn: Gavin's force gathers (~250 men from a tomato field + engineers + gunners), 08:30 arrow up to the ridge; target ring] At dawn on the eleventh of July he scraped together every man he could find: paratroopers, engineers, gunners, a few lost infantrymen. About two hundred and fifty of his own paratroopers he found resting in a tomato field, and he got them moving at once. At half past eight, led by a platoon of engineers, they stormed the ridge and drove the German outposts off the crest.

[MAP: move1-6 | afternoon: HG infantry (700+) + Tigers attack from the valley; two-colour front line; Tiger fire hits the ridge (boom + shake); bazooka shots bounce off; 2 pack howitzers fire over open sights] That afternoon, the Hermann Goering Division struck back with more than seven hundred infantry and Tiger tanks. Gavin later wrote that a Tiger was an awesome thing to encounter in combat. The paratroopers' bazookas left holes no bigger than a little finger in its front armour. Two small pack howitzers were dragged forward and fired at the tanks over open sights. Men were falling all along the crest. Gavin's order was simple: they were staying on this ridge, no matter what.

[MAP: move1-7 | navy ensign with radio; ships in the Gulf of Gela fire (K.gun -> arc -> impact, shake on every shell); 18:00 six Shermans come up Route 115; counterattack with everyone; Germans flee; front moves north] Then a young navy ensign with a radio reached the ridge. He called the warships offshore for a single trial round. It landed right where a Tiger had just pulled back. Then he called for everything they had, and the heavy shells began crashing down among the German tanks. From then on, Gavin wrote, the battle seemed to change. At six in the evening, six Sherman tanks rolled up. Gavin did not wait for the Germans to try again. He threw in everyone he had, even the cooks, the clerks and the truck drivers, anyone who could carry a rifle. They overran the German machine guns, captured a dozen heavy mortars, and the Germans fled from the field.

[MAP: move1-8 | casualty card US ~50 killed / 100+ wounded, German unknown; THE RIDGE HELD; method card line 1 lit: LEAD FROM THE FRONT] The ridge held. It cost about fifty dead and more than a hundred wounded, but that German column never reached the beaches. Gavin's first lesson: lead from the front. He was on that ridge, rifle in hand, from the first shot to the last, and he was awarded the Distinguished Service Cross for it.

## Move 2

[MAP: move2-1 | Normandy wide map: title card MOVE 2 · LA FIÈRE CAUSEWAY · 6-9 JUNE 1944; Utah Beach, Sainte-Mère-Église, the Merderet; 82nd drop zones; arrow from Utah west across the Merderet toward the west coast (cut the peninsula)] Move two: the La Fière causeway. June 1944. Gavin, now a brigadier general and second in command of the 82nd Airborne Division, jumped into Normandy in the first hours of D-Day. He came down west of the flooded Merderet River, waded through the marshes, and gathered paratroopers from every regiment as he went. Many others were not so lucky: weighed down by their equipment, some men drowned in water barely deep enough to cover them. The division's job was to hold the crossings over the Merderet River, west of Sainte-Mère-Église, so the troops coming ashore at Utah Beach could push west and cut the Cotentin Peninsula in two.

[MAP: move2-2 | La Fière close map: flooded Merderet (blue water overlay), the causeway as a thin line ~500 yards, the stone bridge and the manor at the east end, Cauquigny chapel at the west end; measurement bar ~500 YARDS] The Germans had flooded the Merderet valley. Across the water ran one narrow raised road, about five hundred yards long, with a stone bridge and a farm at its eastern end: the manor of La Fière.

[MAP: move2-3 | blue counters take the manor on D-Day morning; red infantry + captured French tank counters attack down the causeway again and again; bazooka shots, tanks knocked out (boom + shake); date 6-8 JUNE] Paratroopers seized the manor on the morning of D-Day, and for three days they held it. German infantry and captured French tanks came down the causeway again and again, and bazooka teams knocked out the tanks on the road. When someone asked Lieutenant John Dolan whether his company should pull back, he sent back word that he knew of no better place to die. They stayed.

[MAP: move2-4 | red MG nests on the west bank fan their fire lines across the causeway; caption NO COVER · WATER ON BOTH SIDES; speech bubble NOBODY CAN CROSS THAT ROAD (no quote marks)] On the far side, German machine guns covered every yard of the causeway. Any attack would have to run straight down a bare road with water on both sides and nowhere to hide. To the defenders, it looked impossible.

[MAP: move2-5 | Gavin badge; caption SPEED IS THE ONLY COVER; the causeway highlights] Gavin saw something different. The causeway was a death trap only for men who stopped on it. Speed was the only cover. Hit the far bank with everything at once, keep moving, and the machine guns could not stop them all. By now he had the tools: a battalion of glider infantry that had landed the day before, and heavy guns brought up from Utah Beach.

[MAP: move2-6 | 9 JUNE · 10:45: artillery impacts on the west bank (boom + shake), thin smoke; glider infantry battalion arrow down the causeway, stops halfway; red MG tracers; some counters turn back] On the ninth of June, the heavy guns opened fire on the far bank, fifteen minutes of shelling, and at a quarter to eleven a battalion of glider infantry started down the causeway. But the smoke screen was thin, German fire swept the road, and the leading men went to ground. Some turned back. The attack was dying on the causeway.

[MAP: move2-7 | Gavin badge + Captain Rae's company counter at the bridge; quote card "All right, you've got to go."; Rae's counter charges; Ridgway and Gavin markers out on the road] Gavin turned to Captain Robert Rae, whose company of paratroopers was waiting by the bridge. "All right, you've got to go." Rae's ninety men charged down the road, through the smoke, and Gavin and his commander, Matthew Ridgway, were out on the causeway themselves, under fire, pushing the attack forward.

[MAP: move2-8 | blue counters reach Cauquigny, fan out on the west bank; red counters grey out; front line moves west; territory] Rae's paratroopers reached the far bank and kept going, and the stalled glider infantry got up and followed them. Around the little chapel at Cauquigny and out into the hedgerows, one after another the German positions fell. By the end of the day the causeway was open, and fresh American troops were crossing it.

[MAP: move2-9 | casualty card 82nd ~60 killed / 500+ wounded, captured or missing (La Fière 6-9 June), German unknown; arrow onward to the west coast; method card line 2 lit: TAKE THE GROUND THAT DECIDES THE BATTLE] The fight for La Fière cost the 82nd about sixty men killed and more than five hundred wounded, captured or missing. But the road west was open, and less than two weeks later American troops had cut the peninsula in two. Lesson two: find the ground that decides the battle, take it, and never let it go.

[ARCHIVE: arch-2 | La Fière causeway / Merderet flooded fields 1944, knocked-out tank on the causeway (public domain US Army Signal Corps)] A ridge in Sicily. A causeway in Normandy. Three months later, Gavin would face the hardest problem of his war.

## Move 3

[MAP: move3-1 | Holland wide map: title card MOVE 3 · THE WAAL CROSSING · 20 SEPTEMBER 1944; the corridor: Eindhoven (101st), Nijmegen (82nd), Arnhem (British 1st Airborne); the single road; XXX Corps arrow north] Move three: the Waal crossing. September 1944, Operation Market Garden. Three airborne divisions would grab a chain of bridges across Holland, and British tanks would race up a single road to the last bridge, at Arnhem, more than sixty miles behind the German lines. If it worked, the Allies could be across the Rhine and the war might be over by Christmas. Gavin's division landed on the seventeenth of September, south of Nijmegen.

[MAP: move3-2 | zoom to Nijmegen: Gavin badge (MAJ. GEN., age 37, commands the 82nd); the bridges around Nijmegen; the Waal road bridge pulses] Gavin, just thirty-seven, now commanded the whole 82nd Airborne. His division had to take the bridges around Nijmegen, the biggest of them the great road bridge over the river Waal.

[MAP: move3-3 | Nijmegen close map: blue attacks on the south end of the road bridge repulsed by SS defenders (red, dug in around the south end); British tanks waiting in the town; clock caption ARNHEM IS RUNNING OUT OF TIME] For three days his men fought for it. On the first night, a battalion pushed through the dark streets toward the bridge and was stopped. Two days later, British tanks and American paratroopers attacked together through the parks at the southern end, and were stopped again. German SS troops dug in around the bridge threw back every attack, while the British tanks waited in Nijmegen and the paratroopers at Arnhem fought on alone, running out of time.

[MAP: move3-4 | red guns on the north bank; the river highlights ~300 M OF OPEN WATER; speech bubble THEY WILL COME FROM THE SOUTH (no quote marks)] The Germans expected the attacks to keep coming from the south. The river guarded the northern end. Three hundred metres of open water, in daylight, under their guns. No one would try to cross that.

[MAP: move3-5 | Gavin badge; dotted blue arrow: cross downstream (west), land on the north bank, swing east to the bridges; caption TAKE IT FROM BOTH ENDS] Gavin saw something different. If he could not take the bridge from the front, he would take it from both ends. His paratroopers would cross the river downstream, land on the north bank, and storm the bridge from behind.

[MAP: move3-6 | 26 boat counters arrive late at the power station; 3/504 (Maj. Julian Cook) counter; date 20 SEPT · 15:00] The boats were British canvas assault boats, and they arrived at the last minute. Twenty-six of them, flimsy things of plywood and canvas, with three paddles each. None of the paratroopers had ever used one. Each boat would carry about ten paratroopers and three engineers to paddle it. At about three in the afternoon on the twentieth of September, the third battalion of the five hundred and fourth, led by Major Julian Cook, carried them down to the water.

[MAP: move3-7 | tanks + artillery fire from the south bank (boom + shake on every impact), smoke drifts away; red MG/flak tracers; impacts among the boats (shake); some boats sink (grey)] Tanks and artillery fired from the south bank and laid smoke, but the wind blew the smoke away. The Germans opened up with machine guns, mortars and flak guns. Boats were hit, and men fell into the river. Cook prayed aloud as he paddled: Hail Mary, full of grace, over and over.

[MAP: move3-8 | about half the boats reach the north bank; blue counters charge the dike, take the fort, swing east along the river; red counters grey out] Only about half the boats made it across. The survivors scrambled up the dike, charged inland, then turned east along the river toward the bridges. British officers watching from a power-station roof could hardly believe what they were seeing.

[MAP: move3-9 | blue counters reach the north end of the railway bridge, then the road bridge; 19:00: British tank counters cross the road bridge (the flash-forward image again); front moves north] The engineers paddled the surviving boats back again and again, bringing more men across under fire. On the north bank, the paratroopers fought their way to the north end of the railway bridge, then the road bridge. At about seven in the evening, British tanks drove across the Waal bridge, and it stayed standing.

[MAP: move3-10 | casualty card 48 killed in the crossing; then wide: the road to Arnhem ten miles north, red closes around Arnhem; caption MARKET GARDEN FAILED; method card line 3 lit: ATTACK WHERE THE ENEMY IS SURE YOU CANNOT] Forty-eight of the paratroopers who crossed were killed. It was one of the bravest attacks of the war, and it took the bridge. But Arnhem, ten miles further north, was never reached, and Market Garden failed. Lesson three: when the front door is shut, go around it, even across a river. Attack where the enemy is sure you cannot.

## Ending

[ARCHIVE: end-1 | Gavin later photos (public domain): Gavin 1945, Gavin as ambassador to France 1961 (if PD)] After the war, Gavin led the 82nd down Fifth Avenue in the victory parade, rose to lieutenant general, and kept thinking ahead. In 1954 he wrote an article called Cavalry, and I Don't Mean Horses, imagining soldiers carried into battle by helicopter. He left the army in 1958 after clashing with his superiors over the future of the forces, and in the early 1960s served as America's ambassador to France. He died in 1990, and he is buried at West Point. Today a bronze paratrooper stands beside the bridge at La Fière. And in Nijmegen, a footbridge now crosses the Waal where the boats went over, lit every evening by forty-eight pairs of lights, one for each man who died in the crossing.

[MAP: end-2 | Europe map: the three moves replay (ridge, causeway, river); method card, all three lines lit] A ridge in Sicily, a causeway in Normandy, a river in Holland. Three times the plan fell apart, and three times Gavin was there at the front, with a rifle in his hands. Lead from the front. Take the ground that decides the battle. And attack where the enemy is sure you cannot.
