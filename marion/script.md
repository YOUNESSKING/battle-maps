# Francis Marion's Top 3 Legendary Tactical Moves — Script v1 (for owner approval)

Format: standard Top 3 (hook + background + 3 moves + short ending), target ~15-16 min (~2,450 spoken words; Kokoro am_michael ~155 wpm).
Facts: `marion/research/FACT_NOTES.md` (every number and quote below is logged there with its label).
Hook (1a + 1c): stakes of THIS story first (Britain's one supply road over the Santee), ONE continuous zoom ending on NELSON'S FERRY, the number + the result (promise) by ~15 s, back into the danger, the general enters as the turn (~0:50) with ONE human detail (the window jump), name slam + 3 snap cuts. No hero name before the slam.
Script devices (1d): each move opens on the enemy plan + "in other words"; the twist as "But... instead"; cliffhanger at each move end; day-by-day dates in move 1 and move 3.
**Set-up -> pay-off (replaces Gemini's "boast", which does not exist):** Cornwallis's own plan, 20 Aug 1780, "to awe the disaffected" (move 1, "Remember that word") -> his own admission, 3 Dec 1780, "scarce an inhabitant between the Santee and Pedee that was not in arms against us" (end of move 2). Second, small one: Marion's band laughed at in Gates's camp (background) -> Congress thanks Marion by name (end of move 3).
Engagement (1b): subscribe bridge after move 1 (D2 pattern); ONE "What would you do?" at Richardson's plantation (move 2); final 1-2-3 question + "which commander next" (18th century: no family line). No next-video pitch.
Sides: Americans BLUE; British + Loyalists RED. Flags: Marion = Moultrie flag (indigo, white crescent; drawn by us, representative symbol); Greene/Lee (move 3) = US 13-star; British = Union Flag 1606. Banners on poles (2f): Marion (blue, Moultrie pennant; posthumous portrait), Greene + Lee (blue, 13-star), Cornwallis, Tarleton, Wemyss (no portrait: initials), Rawdon (red, Union pennant). Each only on/after its spoken name.
Weather: none in move 1/2 (dark nights only); bright scorching sun at Fort Motte (12 May); heat at Parker's Ferry and Eutaw. Nothing else.
Method card ("THE SWAMP FOX METHOD"): **1. STRIKE IN THE DARK · 2. LET THE SWAMP FIGHT · 3. HIT THE CHAIN WITH THE ARMY**.
Labels on screen: "BY TRADITION" for the "old fox" quote; "PAINTED LONG AFTER HIS DEATH" on every Marion picture.

## MOVE 1 — STRIKE IN THE DARK (~1:20 – ~5:30)

[MAP: m1-1 | `lowcountry` wide: title card MOVE 1 · STRIKE IN THE DARK; date scroll 20 AUG 1780 on "twentieth of August"; Cornwallis banner (red) at CAMDEN after his name; red post boxes drop into place from the Pee Dee to the Savannah on "a chain of posts" (each with a small fort mark), a thin sepia trace joins them; quote card on the verbatim words "TO AWE THE DISAFFECTED"; on "between the Pee Dee and the Black River" the ground between them gets a soft focus wash; PEE DEE and BLACK RIVER carved river names on their words]
The British plan was simple. On the twentieth of August, four days after Camden, Lord Cornwallis wrote to London that he was fixing a chain of posts across South Carolina, from the Pee Dee River to the Savannah, to awe the disaffected. Remember that word. Awe. And he knew where the trouble was. The whole country between the Pee Dee and the Black River, he wrote, openly avowed rebellion.

[MAP: m1-2 | `lowcountry` medium on the rivers: in other words: the red posts + garrisons glow; on "boats" the ferry flatboats along the SANTEE (carved name on the word) vanish one by one from the upper river to the coast (small splash marks, ticks only); caption on the spoken words "ALL THE BOATS"]
In other words, the British meant to hold every road and every river crossing, until the countryside gave up. But Marion did not try to raise an army to fight them. Instead, he went for the crossings themselves. In his own words, he destroyed all the boats at the ferries, from Murray's to the mouth of the Santee. The British could still march. But now every river was a wall, and only Marion knew where the gaps were.

[MAP: m1-3 | `santee` close, NIGHT (dark, no moon): date scroll 25 AUG 1780 on "twenty-fifth of August"; GREAT SAVANNAH painted house by the road above NELSON'S FERRY (label on the word); the red guard box + grey prisoners box at the house; muskets stacked by the door; blue boxes come out of the swamp: a small group (16) peels off to the road at HORSE CREEK (label on the word, Hugh Horry tag on his spoken name); the main blue group circles behind the house; one musket flash from the sentry on "fired" (one mg-style pop, no artillery); blue rushes the house on "rushed"; the stacked muskets go blue on "seized"; battle mark on the house]
On the twenty-fifth of August, his scouts found a British guard resting at a plantation house at the Great Savannah, six miles above Nelson's Ferry, with a hundred and fifty prisoners from Camden. A little before daylight, Marion sent Colonel Hugh Horry with sixteen men to block the road at a swampy pass called Horse Creek, and led the rest around behind the house. A sentry heard them and fired. Marion's men rushed the house. The British muskets were piled in front of the door, and Marion's men got to them first.

[MAP: m1-4 | `santee` close, dawn light: casualty card (both sides spoken): BRITISH 2 KILLED · 5 WOUNDED · 20 CAPTURED / MARION 1 KILLED; the grey prisoners box turns blue (150), then on "only three stayed" most of it fades and walks off north (three small blue dots stay); Marion banner stays by the house]
Two of the guard were killed, five wounded, and twenty taken. Marion lost one man. But the freed prisoners were Continental regulars, beaten at Camden, and they had seen enough of war. Marion knew it. They will desert, to a man, he wrote. Of a hundred and fifty, only three stayed with him.

[MAP: m1-5 | `lowcountry` wide: date scroll SEP 1780; Wemyss banner (red, initials JW) on his name at CAMDEN -> a red column (cavalry slash + infantry X boxes) sweeps from KINGSTREE (label on the word) east toward the PEE DEE; small fire marks bloom one after another along its path on "burned" (visual only, no booms; a counter card on the spoken number "ABOUT 50 HOUSES"); the blue band shrinks to a single small box that retreats north across the Pee Dee into NORTH CAROLINA (label on the word)]
Cornwallis struck back. In September he sent Major James Wemyss with the Sixty-third Regiment and Loyalist militia to sweep the country from Kingstree to the Pee Dee. Wemyss reported that he had burned and laid waste about fifty houses and plantations. Marion's band melted down to about sixty men, and he retreated across the border into North Carolina. It looked like the end.

[MAP: m1-6 | `lowcountry` wide: unfortunately for Wemyss: on "burned houses" small grey smoke rises over Williamsburg, then blue dots gather toward Marion's box on "came back"; the blue box rides south fast (dust) on "sixty miles" (caption 60 MILES)]
Unfortunately for Wemyss, the burned houses belonged to the men who had followed Marion. Instead of giving up, they came back to him, angrier than before. Within days he was riding south again, about sixty miles at a forced march.

[MAP: m1-7 | `blackriver` close, NIGHT (dark, no moon): day-by-day clock: date scroll 28 SEP on "twenty-eighth of September"; BLACK MINGO creek (carved name on the word); a plank bridge over the creek a mile above SHEPHERD'S FERRY (label on the word); blue column on the bridge; on "alarm gun" one red musket flash at the Loyalist camp; the red box forms up in an open old field (no farms/fields art: a plain clearing in the woods); a red volley flash pushes the front blue box back; a second blue box swings round the flank on "the side"; red breaks into the swamp (fast move, fade 0.4 s); battle mark on "broke"]
Then came the nights. On the twenty-eighth of September, near midnight, Marion's men crossed a plank bridge over Black Mingo Creek, a mile above a Loyalist camp. Someone in the camp heard them and fired an alarm gun. The Loyalists were waiting in an open field, and their first volley drove Marion's front back. But a second party came in from the side, and the Loyalists broke and ran into the swamp. Marion took the Loyalist commander's horse for his own.

[MAP: m1-8 | `blackriver` close, NIGHT: date scroll 25 OCT on "twenty-fifth of October"; TEARCOAT SWAMP label on the word, in the fork of the Black River (carved river name); a red Loyalist box (~cards) at a small clearing; three blue arrows (solid, thin) converge from left, centre and right on "three columns"; one pistol flash on "a pistol shot"; all three hit at once (battle mark, hit); red scatters into the swamp; small grey box of captured horses + muskets turns blue on "horses"]
On the twenty-fifth of October, he found more Loyalists camped at Tearcoat Swamp, in the fork of the Black River. After midnight he split his men into three columns. A single pistol shot was the signal, and all three charged at once out of the dark.

[MAP: m1-9 | `blackriver` close: casualty card: LOYALISTS 6 KILLED · 14 WOUNDED · 23 CAPTURED / MARION: NONE LOST; then on "joined Marion" several red dots turn blue and walk to Marion's box; result + method card: THE SWAMP FOX METHOD · 1. STRIKE IN THE DARK]
Six Loyalists were killed, fourteen wounded, and twenty-three captured, along with their horses, muskets and baggage. Marion did not lose a man. And soon afterwards, most of the men he had beaten that night came over and joined him. That was the first part of his method. Never meet the enemy's strength in daylight. Strike in the dark, where he sleeps.

## MOVE 2 — LET THE SWAMP FIGHT (~5:50 – ~10:00)

[MAP: m2-1 | `lowcountry` wide -> `blackriver`: title card MOVE 2 · LET THE SWAMP FIGHT; date scroll NOV 1780; Tarleton banner (red, Reynolds portrait) on his name; two quick red stamps on their words: WAXHAWS, FISHING CREEK (past wins, small marks on the map in the north); red Legion boxes (cavalry slash + infantry X + two small kit gun boxes) ride south from Camden toward the Santee; quote card on the verbatim words "CAUTIOUS AND VIGILANT"]
The British plan this time was a hunt. Lieutenant Colonel Banastre Tarleton commanded the British Legion, a fast force of cavalry and light infantry, with two light guns. He had already destroyed one American force at the Waxhaws and another at Fishing Creek. Cornwallis sent him south to get at Marion, and warned him what he was up against. Marion is cautious and vigilant, he wrote.

[MAP: m2-2 | `blackriver` wide: in other words: Marion's small blue box in the woods west of the BLACK RIVER; a red ring tightens around it lightly on "one fight in the open" (no big card); the swamps (cypress swamp texture) glow faintly]
In other words, Tarleton did not need to win a long campaign. He needed Marion to stand and fight in the open, just once. Against horsemen like these, in open country, a band of militia would be cut to pieces.

[MAP: m2-3 | `blackriver` close, NIGHT 7 NOV (date scroll on "seventh of November"): Marion's blue box halts in a wood; to the west a big orange glow rises at GENERAL RICHARDSON'S plantation (painted house + glow, label RICHARDSON'S on the word); a single blue rider comes in from the dark on "a rider"; card on the spoken words: TWICE HIS NUMBERS · TWO GUNS]
On the night of the seventh of November, Marion was moving up the road to strike at Tarleton when he saw a great light in the sky ahead, at the plantation of the late General Richardson. Then a rider came in out of the dark. It was the general's son. The British, he said, were waiting at the plantation, at least twice Marion's numbers, with two field guns. And one of Marion's own men had deserted to guide them.

[MAP: m2-4 | `blackriver` close, NIGHT, dim: E.decide on "put yourself in Marion's place": A ATTACK IN THE DARK / B VANISH INTO THE SWAMP, TELL US IN THE COMMENTS (3 ticks); camera keeps a slow push on the glow; reveal B on m2-5 start]
Now stop for a moment, and put yourself in Marion's place. You have just won three night attacks in a row. The enemy is somewhere around that fire, just ahead of you. Do you hit him in the dark, the way you always have? Or do you turn around and vanish into the swamp? Tell us in the comments what you would have done. And then listen to what Marion did.

[MAP: m2-5 | `blackriver` close, NIGHT, "profound darkness": reveal B (hit); the blue box turns east and goes into the WOODYARD swamp (label on the word; cypress swamp texture; no trail drawn behind it); it crosses past a small mill dam at JACK'S CREEK (label on the word); caption on spoken words "NOW WE ARE SAFE!" (verbatim, ~3 s); at dawn the red Legion boxes reach the empty wood; the Richardson house burns (fire mark, smoke column) on "burned"]
He did not attack. Instead, he turned his men around and led them into the Woodyard swamp, in what one of his men later called the most profound darkness. They did not stop until they had crossed a mill dam on Jack's Creek, six miles away. Now we are safe, Marion said. When Tarleton's trap was sprung, it closed on nothing. In his anger, Tarleton burned the Richardson house to the ground.

[MAP: m2-6 | `blackriver` wide, day 8 NOV (date scroll on "eighth"): the red Legion picks up the trail; a long red arrow drawn on, chasing the blue box east and south through swamp after swamp (cypress texture, water streaks); a clock card on the verbatim words "SEVEN HOURS"; cards on spoken numbers: TARLETON ~25 MILES · MARION 35 MILES; the blue box's route goes off-road through bog (no road under it), the red route bends around each swamp (detours drawn); dust behind the red column only]
The next morning, Tarleton found the trail, and the chase began. For seven hours, as Tarleton himself wrote, he followed Marion through swamps and defiles, some twenty-five miles. Marion's men rode thirty-five, through woods and bogs where there was no road at all. Where they rode straight through a swamp, Tarleton's column had to go around it.

[MAP: m2-7 | `blackriver` close: the blue box reaches BENBOW'S FERRY on the Black River (label on the word; ferry landing + flatboat); on "felled trees" little fallen-log marks block the road (by one veteran's account: small caption on spoken words "BY ONE VETERAN'S ACCOUNT"); the red Legion halts at the edge of OX SWAMP (label on the word; dark cypress swamp band); caption BY TRADITION + quote card (verbatim): "AS FOR THIS DAMNED OLD FOX, THE DEVIL HIMSELF COULD NOT CATCH HIM"]
At Benbow's Ferry on the Black River, by one veteran's account, Marion's men felled trees across the road and waited to fight on ground of their choosing. They never had to. Tarleton halted at the edge of Ox Swamp. By tradition, he turned his men around and said: let us go back, and we will soon find the Gamecock. But as for this damned old fox, the devil himself could not catch him.

[MAP: m2-8 | `blackriver` wide: honest hedge: a small red rider (express) gallops in from Camden to Tarleton's box on "a messenger"; the red Legion turns north-west and rides away (fast, trail fades)]
Tarleton told it differently. In his own history, a messenger from Cornwallis called him back, because the other partisan leader, Thomas Sumter, the Gamecock, was threatening a British post in the west. Either way, the result was the same. Marion was still free.

[MAP: m2-9 | `lowcountry` wide: date scroll 3 DEC 1780 on "third of December"; Cornwallis banner at WINNSBOROUGH (label on the word, or Camden area if off-map); pay-off: the band between SANTEE and PEE DEE fills with blue FORM 1 hatching (~3 s); quote card (verbatim, 3 lines max): "SCARCE AN INHABITANT BETWEEN THE SANTEE AND PEDEE THAT WAS NOT IN ARMS AGAINST US"; second short quote card on the spoken words "TERROR TO THE GATES OF CHARLESTOWN" with a small blue arrow crossing the Santee toward CHARLESTON]
On the third of December, Cornwallis had to explain it all to his commander in New York. Remember the plan to awe the country? Colonel Marion, he wrote, had so wrought on the minds of the people that there was scarce an inhabitant between the Santee and Pedee that was not in arms against us. Some parties, he added, had even carried terror to the gates of Charlestown. He claimed that Tarleton's raid had checked it, for now.

[MAP: m2-10 | `lowcountry` medium: SNOW'S ISLAND (label on the word) in the fork of the PEE DEE and LYNCHES CREEK (carved names on their words): a ring of dark cypress swamp and water around a small blue box; result card: TARLETON'S CHASE · 7 HOURS · MARION ESCAPED; then the method card adds 2. LET THE SWAMP FIGHT; small caption on "eighteen forties" not used (spoken only)]
Marion made his base on Snow's Island, a patch of high ground ringed by rivers and swamp, where only his own men knew the way in. The nickname came later. The Swamp Fox appears in print only in the eighteen forties. But it fit the second part of his method. Never fight cavalry on its ground. Make the enemy fight the swamp first, and let it break his horses before he ever finds you.

[MAP: m2-11 | `lowcountry` wide, cliffhanger: date scroll DEC 1780; a blue box enters from the north edge (North Carolina) on "a new American general"; the "?" banner beside it]
And that December, a new American general rode into the Carolinas, with a plan that needed Marion more than ever.

## MOVE 3 — HIT THE CHAIN WITH THE ARMY (~10:00 – ~15:00)

[MAP: m3-1 | `lowcountry` wide: title card MOVE 3 · HIT THE CHAIN; date scroll APR 1781; Greene banner (blue, 13-star pennant, Peale portrait) reveals on "Nathanael Greene"; the red chain of posts lights up on their spoken names: CAMDEN, FORT WATSON, FORT MOTTE (painted forts); a thin red supply line runs from CHARLESTON up through them (ink trace); Rawdon banner (red) at Camden on "Lord Rawdon"]
That general was Nathanael Greene. By the spring of seventeen eighty-one, the British plan in South Carolina rested on a chain of forts. Camden in the middle, held by Lord Rawdon, and below it a string of smaller posts guarding the road to Charleston, like Fort Watson and Fort Motte. Every barrel of powder and every sack of flour for Camden came up that chain.

[MAP: m3-2 | `lowcountry` wide: in other words: the chain glows; on "cut" a blue arrow from Greene's army toward Camden, and Marion's box + a second blue box (Lee) toward Fort Watson; ink trace on the chain snaps on "starve"]
In other words, cut the chain, and Camden would starve. So Greene marched his army toward Camden, and he sent Marion, with Lieutenant Colonel Henry Lee and his Legion, to break the chain below it.

[MAP: m3-3 | `santee` close: date scroll 15 APR 1781 on "fifteenth of April"; FORT WATSON (painted stockade on a mound by SCOTT'S LAKE, label on the word); Lee banner (blue, 13-star) reveals on "Lee"; blue boxes take post between the fort and the lake (on "water"); a small blue drop mark inside the fort appears on "well" (the garrison's well); red musket flashes from the loopholes; no artillery on either side]
On the evening of the fifteenth of April, Marion and Lee surrounded Fort Watson, a log stockade on top of a tall old Indian mound beside the Santee. It looked down on cleared ground on every side. They had no cannon. So they cut the garrison off from its water at the lake. But within three days, the British had dug a well inside the walls.

[MAP: m3-4 | `santee` close: the "But... instead" beat: Maham name tag on his spoken name; date scroll 22 APR on "twenty-second"; NIGHT; blue engineer boxes (white GEAR) fetch logs from the woods edge (small log marks moving); an oblong tower of logs rises beside the stockade, crib by crib, a log floor on top (animated growth, ~6 s); dawn light on "morning"]
They could not starve the fort, and they could not batter it. But a militia officer named Hezekiah Maham had an idea. Instead of going through the walls, they would go over them. On the night of the twenty-second of April, Marion's men cut logs, borrowed axes from the farms around, and stacked them crisscross into a tall oblong pen, with a floor of logs on top. By morning, a wooden tower stood next to the fort, taller than its walls.

[MAP: m3-5 | `santee` close, day 23 APR: blue riflemen boxes appear on top of the tower; their fire flashes down into the stockade (visible, small); a blue party pulls the abatis apart at the foot (small sharp-stake marks lifted away); a white flag rises on the stockade (hit); FORM 1 blue hatching on the mound ~3 s; casualty card: 114 CAPTURED · AMERICANS 2 KILLED, 6 WOUNDED]
At dawn, riflemen climbed the tower and fired straight down into the fort, sweeping the ground inside the walls. While they kept the defenders down, a party crept up and tore away the sharpened stakes around the walls. The British commander raised a white flag. A hundred and fourteen men surrendered. The Americans lost two killed and six wounded. Not one cannon had been fired.

[MAP: m3-6 | `lowcountry` medium: Greene banner at CAMDEN; quote card (verbatim): "HISTORY AFFORDS NO INSTANCE..."; second quote card (verbatim): "TO FIGHT ... UNDER THE CONSTANT IMPRESSION OF A DEFEAT"]
The next day, before he had even heard the news, Greene wrote to Marion. History affords no instance, he wrote, wherein an officer has kept possession of a country under so many disadvantages as you have. To fight the enemy bravely with the prospect of victory is nothing, but to fight with intrepidity under the constant impression of a defeat, and inspire irregular troops to do it, is a talent peculiar to yourself.

[MAP: m3-7 | `congaree` close: date scroll 8 MAY 1781 on "eighth of May"; FORT MOTTE (label on the word) = the painted mansion on a hill with a trench and a parapet; Rebecca Motte name tag on her spoken name at the small farmhouse on the hill opposite; blue boxes around it, the six-pounder (kit gun box) on Marion's side; date scroll 10 MAY: Rawdon's red column leaves CAMDEN (from the edge), heading south; NIGHT 11 MAY: little red campfire glows appear on the hills across the river on "campfires"]
Two weeks later, they came to Fort Motte, where the British had turned a big new mansion on a hill into a fortress, with a trench and a high wall of earth around it. The owner, the widow Rebecca Motte, had been sent to live in an old farmhouse nearby. This time Greene had sent a six-pounder, but there was no time for a siege. Lord Rawdon had abandoned Camden and was marching to the rescue. On the night of the eleventh of May, the defenders could see his campfires across the river.

[MAP: m3-8 | `congaree` close, bright noon 12 MAY (warm sun light: FACT_NOTES "scorching sun"): Lee banner beside the farmhouse; on "a bow" a small bow icon; three thin arrow arcs fly onto the mansion roof (visual only) on "three arrows"; the roof catches fire (fire + smoke); red figures on the roof; the six-pounder fires (K.gun, visible, impact on the gable) on "the six-pounder"; white flag (hit); caption BY LEE'S ACCOUNT on the spoken words "by Lee's account"]
So the Americans did something hard. Lee went to Mrs. Motte and told her that her house had to burn. By Lee's account, she not only agreed, she brought him a bow and arrows to do it with. At about noon on the twelfth of May, under a scorching sun, the dry shingle roof caught fire. When British soldiers climbed up to tear the burning shingles away, the six-pounder drove them off the roof. Soon, a white flag went up.

[MAP: m3-9 | `congaree` close: FORM 1 blue hatching on the hill; casualty-free result card: ~150 PRISONERS · NO BLOOD SHED (spoken: "about a hundred and fifty", "not a drop of blood"); the red Loyalist dot that the blue crowd closes on is shielded by Marion's banner moving next to it on "Marion stepped in"]
About a hundred and fifty men surrendered, and not a drop of blood was shed. Mrs. Motte then served dinner to the officers of both sides. When Marion's own militia wanted to hang one of the Loyalist prisoners, a man accused of burning his neighbors' houses, Marion stepped in and kept him safe.

[MAP: m3-10 | `lowcountry` wide: day-by-day clock: date scroll 10 MAY CAMDEN (the red post goes grey + blue hatching ~3 s on "Camden"), 12 MAY FORT MOTTE, 7 JUN GEORGETOWN (label on the word, red box leaves by sea on "sailed away"); the chain dissolves link by link]
Without its chain, the inland country fell apart for the British. Rawdon had already given up Camden. In June, the British garrison at Georgetown, on the coast, sailed away without a fight.

[MAP: m3-11 | `edisto` close (or `lowcountry` inset): date scroll 30 AUG 1781 on "thirtieth of August"; the EDISTO river (carved name on the word) + PARKER'S FERRY (label on the word, ferry landing + flatboat) at sunset (warm dusk light, "very hot": heat shimmer NOT drawn, light only); a raised causeway through the swamp; blue boxes hidden in the thick wood beside it (only small blue edges visible); a few blue decoy riders gallop down the road; red cavalry (Fraser tag on his spoken name) charges after them along the causeway]
By late summer, the British were holding on near the coast. On the thirtieth of August, near Parker's Ferry on the Edisto River, Marion hid his men in a thick wood, within forty yards of a causeway that ran through the swamp. A few of his riders showed themselves, and then galloped away. The Loyalist cavalry of Major Thomas Fraser charged after them, straight down the causeway.

[MAP: m3-12 | `edisto` close: the ambush fires all along the wood at once (a line of flashes, mg/musket sound only, no artillery); the red cavalry box jams on the causeway and breaks (battle mark, fast fade); casualty card (Marion's estimate, spoken): ~20 KILLED · ~80 WOUNDED / MARION 1 KILLED, 3 WOUNDED; a red infantry column with a kit gun box comes up from the ferry on "a bigger column"; the blue boxes slip back into the woods]
The riders were packed so tight on the narrow road that every shot found a target, and Marion's men were firing buckshot. By Marion's own count, about twenty of the enemy were killed on the spot and some eighty wounded. He lost one man killed and three wounded. When a bigger British column came up with a field gun, his hungry men slipped away into the woods.

[MAP: m3-13 | `santee` wide (EUTAW SPRINGS, label on the word; the brick house + springs): date scroll 8 SEP 1781 on "eighth of September"; warm hot-afternoon light (FACT_NOTES: extremely sultry); Greene's army in two lines: the first line militia, Marion banner on the right with Pickens tag, NC militia on the left; second line Continentals; red line opposite; first line advances firing (musket flashes, visible); card on spoken words "FIFTEEN TO TWENTY ROUNDS"; the militia line falls back, the Continentals charge through; the British camp is overrun; the brick house holds (red); Greene withdraws to water]
Nine days later, at Eutaw Springs, Marion stood in a real battle line. Greene put his militia in front, and Marion and Andrew Pickens led the South Carolina men on the right. On a day so hot the men begged for water, they stood in the open against British regulars and fired fifteen to twenty rounds each before they fell back, and Greene's Continentals charged through them. The British camp was overrun, but a brick house held out, and both sides ended the day exhausted.

HIT THE CHAIN WITH THE ARMY; small FORM 2 stripes (~2.5 s) on "Charleston": red only around Charleston, blue across the rest of the state]
The British lost six hundred and ninety-three men killed, wounded or missing. The Americans lost five hundred and twenty-two. Neither side could call it a clean win. But the next evening, the British army left Eutaw Springs and fell back toward Charleston. Congress thanked Marion by name. That was the third part of his method. Alone, a partisan can only raid. Together with a real army, he can cut the enemy's chain one link at a time, until there is nothing left to hold.
