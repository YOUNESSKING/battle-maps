### 2. Flags Actually Used on the Maps (Historical Verification)
- **British Army & Loyalist Militia:** the 1606/1707 King's Colours (Union Flag) alongside regimental colours [S8]. [VERIFIED] [S8]
  - It combines the red Cross of St George and the white Saltire of St Andrew on a blue field.
  - No red Saltire of St Patrick: that was only added in 1801.
- **Francis Marion's Partisan Brigade (what is documented):**
  - **No physical flag on raids** [S1, p. 78; S3, p. 45]:
    - Marion's partisan militia operated on horseback through dense cypress swamps and thorny bogs at night.
    - No contemporary document records them carrying a cloth flag into guerrilla ambushes.
  - **Cockades instead** [S1, p. 22, 78; S6, p. 38]:
    - Loyalist militia wore green sprigs in their hats.
    - To tell themselves apart, Marion's men wore the silver crescent cockade of his old Continental regiment (2nd South Carolina Regiment) on their leather caps or buckled hats, inscribed "LIBERTY" (or "Ultima Ratio").
    - They also wore white paper or bucktail cockades.
  - **The Moultrie Flag (indigo blue with a white crescent)** [S1, p. 22–28; S8]. [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 22–28; S8]
    - Designed in 1775 by Col. William Moultrie for the 2nd South Carolina Regiment.
    - Indigo-blue field, white crescent in the dexter canton, sometimes bearing the word LIBERTY.
    - Marion served in that regiment as Major at Fort Sullivan in 1776 and as Lieutenant Colonel Commander in 1778–1780, and remained its commissioned Lieutenant Colonel throughout the war.
    - This makes it the most historically accurate symbol to represent Marion on animated maps.
  - **US 13-Star Flag & Continental Colors** [S8]. [VERIFIED] [S8]
    - Appropriate only for Nathanael Greene's main Continental Army and Light-Horse Harry Lee's Legion in Move 3 (Fort Watson, Fort Motte and Eutaw Springs, 1781).

### 3. Portrait Authenticity Warning (Mandatory Visual Check)
- **CRITICAL FACT: no authentic life portrait of Francis Marion exists** [S1, p. 312; S2, p. ix].
  - Marion never sat for a painter during his lifetime (1732–1795).
  - Every depiction was made after his death by artists relying on verbal descriptions from his family and veterans [S1, p. 55, 312; S6, p. 118]:
    - tiny: around 5 feet 2 inches and 110 pounds;
    - a swarthy complexion, a hooked nose, a high forehead and piercing dark eyes;
    - a permanent limp from a badly healed 1780 ankle fracture.

## 1. Hook material
### 45–60 Second Continuous Crisis Action Story (Three Candidates)

**Candidate 1: 25 August 1780, the midnight raid at Great Savannah / Nelson's Ferry** (best sourced and most dramatic opening for Move 1). [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 52, 65–68; S6, p. 48–49]
- **The action and the turn:**
  - Nine days after Camden wiped out the last American army in the South, a British guard of the 63rd Regiment and Prince of Wales Loyalists marches 150 captured Continental prisoners (starving, barefoot, bound in ropes) down the muddy stagecoach road toward the prison ships of Charleston [S1, p. 65–67].
  - It is an hour before dawn at Nelson's Ferry on the Santee River. The British officers are asleep inside Sumter's abandoned house at Great Savannah, their muskets stacked outside in piles, convinced there is not a single rebel force left within two hundred miles [S1, p. 66–67; S6, p. 48].
  - Out of the pitch-black cypress swamp, fifty-two ragged horsemen charge straight between the sentries and the stacked muskets. They carry sawed-off farm saws for swords and shotguns loaded with buckshot.
  - Their leader is a 48-year-old, 110-pound, crippled Huguenot planter who escaped the fall of Charleston only because he broke his ankle jumping out of a second-story window to flee a drunken dinner party: Francis Marion [S1, p. 52, 66–68; S2, p. 78–82].
- **The stakes** [S1, p. 60–64]:
  - After the twin catastrophes of Charleston (12 May) and Camden (16 August), the Continental Army had ceased to exist in South Carolina.
  - If Cornwallis subdued the backcountry between the Santee and Pee Dee Rivers unopposed, he would march straight through North Carolina and Virginia and end the war before Washington could send another army south.
- **Hard number:** 52 militia partisans freed 150 Continental prisoners and killed or captured 24 British guards, at a cost of 1 American killed and 1 wounded [S1, p. 67; S6, p. 49].

**Candidate 2: 10 November 1780, the 26-mile chase into Ox Swamp** (best for the nickname hook). [VERIFIED chase; TRADITION/1821 JAMES MEMOIR for the exact "damned old fox" quote] [S1, p. 122–126; S4, p. 171–172; S6, p. 66–67]
- **The action and the turn:**
  - In November 1780, Lt. Col. Banastre Tarleton ("Bloody Ban"), set on wiping out the last rebel partisan band in South Carolina, lures his quarry toward the Richardson plantation.
  - He then launches a relentless seven-hour, 26-mile cavalry pursuit across wood-choked creeks and bottomless bogs [S1, p. 122–126; S4, p. 171].
  - At Ox Swamp his thoroughbred dragoon horses sink to their bellies in the mud, facing a trackless wall of cypress knees and hanging vines.
  - Tarleton reins in his exhausted troopers at the edge of the mire, glares into the dark water, and reportedly swears: "Come my boys! Let us go back, and we will soon find the Gamecock [Sumter], but as for this damned old fox, the Devil himself could not catch him!" That christened the man watching from the trees: Francis Marion, the Swamp Fox [S1, p. 126; S6, p. 67].
- **The stakes** [S1, p. 120–127]:
  - Tarleton had crushed Buford at the Waxhaws and Sumter at Fishing Creek.
  - If he caught and annihilated Marion's ~150–200 partisans in November 1780, the last organized resistance in eastern South Carolina would vanish weeks before Nathanael Greene arrived to rebuild the Southern Army.
- **Hard number:** 26 miles of continuous pursuit over 7 hours before Tarleton's ~300–400 British Legion dragoons gave up at the edge of Ox Swamp [S1, p. 125–126; S6, p. 66–67].

**Candidate 3: 15–23 April 1781, the log tower in the night at Fort Watson.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 198–203; S7, p. 278]
- **The action and the turn:**
  - A British garrison sits inside the wooden palisades of Fort Watson, atop a thirty-foot ancient Native American burial mound beside the Santee River. It is surrounded by cleared fields, and the attackers have no cannon and no siege equipment [S1, p. 198–200].
  - At dawn on 23 April 1781, the British look out of their loopholes and find that during the night the American partisans have cut pine logs in the swamp with axes borrowed from local farms.
  - The partisans have stacked them into a forty-foot rectangular wooden tower at the edge of the fort. Riflemen on top fire straight down onto the defenders, cutting Cornwallis's lifeline to Charleston [S1, p. 201–203; S7, p. 278].
- **Hard number:** 114 British regulars and Loyalists captured without a single cannon shot, at a cost of 2 Americans killed and 6 wounded [S1, p. 203].

### The Stakes (Channel Rule Since 2026-10-28)
**What was about to be lost or won:** the entire Southern Theater of the War of Independence (South Carolina, North Carolina and Georgia).
- **No American regulars left:**
  - 12 May 1780: the British captured Charleston, where Maj. Gen. Benjamin Lincoln surrendered 5,266 Continental soldiers, the largest American surrender of the war.
  - 16 August 1780: Cornwallis crushed Maj. Gen. Horatio Gates at Camden: ~2,000 American casualties and the complete destruction of the Southern Continental Army.
  - Not a single regular American unit remained in South Carolina [S1, p. 48–62].
- **Britain's "Southern Strategy"** [S1, p. 58–61]:
  - pacify South Carolina with a network of interior forts (Camden, Ninety Six, Georgetown, Fort Motte, Fort Watson, Augusta);
  - recruit thousands of Loyalist militia;
  - roll north through North Carolina and Virginia.
- **Marion's role, August–December 1780** [S1, p. 63–65, 132]:
  - His brigade, operating between the Santee and Pee Dee Rivers out of Snow's Island, was the main force keeping the war alive in the Lowcountry.
  - It tied down thousands of British regulars, cut Cornwallis's main river supply line from Charleston to Camden, and stopped the Loyalist militia from organizing.

**The enemy's run of success before it** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 38–61]:
- **29 Dec 1778 and 9 Oct 1779:** the British capture Savannah, Georgia, and beat back the Franco-American siege of Savannah, where Marion fought with the 2nd SC Regiment [S1, p. 38–44].
- **12 May 1780:** Sir Henry Clinton captures Charleston and 5,266 American troops [S1, p. 48–53].
- **29 May 1780:** at the Battle of the Waxhaws, Tarleton slaughters Col. Abraham Buford's retreating Virginia Continentals (113 killed, 150 mutilated or wounded), giving rise to the cry "Tarleton's Quarter" [S1, p. 56].
- **16 August 1780:** Cornwallis routs Horatio Gates at Camden [S1, p. 60].
- **18 August 1780:** Tarleton surprises and routs Thomas Sumter's partisan camp at Fishing Creek, capturing ~300 men and freeing 100 British prisoners [S1, p. 61].

### Post-Hook Background (Max 20 Seconds — Move 1 Setup Only)
How did the commander of the 2nd South Carolina Regiment avoid capture when Sir Henry Clinton trapped 5,200 Americans inside Charleston in May 1780? [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 51–64; S6, p. 24]
- **The dinner party:** in March 1780, at a dinner party during the siege, the host Alexander McQueen locked the second-story dining-room doors to force every officer to drink toasts of wine [S1, p. 51–52; S6, p. 24].
- **The ankle:** Marion, who hated heavy drinking, jumped out of a second-story window to escape the party and badly fractured his ankle.
- **The evacuation:** as a result, Gen. Benjamin Lincoln ordered all "supernumerary and unfit officers" evacuated to the countryside, days before the British sealed the trap [S1, p. 52–53; S6, p. 24].
- **Four months later:** still limping so badly he had to be lifted onto his horse Ball, Marion took command of the ragtag Williamsburg militia between the Santee and Pee Dee Rivers [S1, p. 55–64].

## 3. The General's Method
Marion's 3-part method:
1. **Strike the tether between midnight and dawn** [S1, p. 66, 90, 104]:
   - never attack a superior field army head-on in daylight;
   - travel silently by night and divide into three converging prongs;
   - strike isolated supply convoys, river ferries and Tory camps an hour before dawn, while their muskets are stacked.
2. **Weaponize swamp geography to defeat cavalry** [S1, p. 122–126, 138, 248]:
   - use impenetrable river islands (Snow's Island) as sanctuaries;
   - sink or hide every ferry boat along the rivers;
   - when pursued by superior regular dragoons, cut across flooded cypress bogs to exhaust their horses and lure them into narrow causeway kill-zones.
3. **Hybrid guerrilla–regular synergy** [S1, p. 195–213, 258]:
   - pair partisan intelligence and frontier ingenuity (night log towers, flaming arrows, buckshot ambushes) with a regular Continental army as the operational anvil;
   - this forces the enemy either to disperse to guard his supply posts, where the guerrillas eat them alive, or to concentrate to chase the guerrillas, abandoning the countryside to the regulars.

Alternative phrasings:
- Alternative phrasing 1: Own the night and the river crossings, turn the cypress swamps into both a shield and a trap, and starve the enemy's main army by severing its supply forts one by one.
- Alternative phrasing 2: Hit the enemy where he sleeps before dawn, let the mud break his cavalry charges, and combine backwoods irregulars with Continental steel to reconquer the land.

## 4. Short Factual Section: Controversies & Brutality of the Partisan War (The Honest Ledger)
**Cornwallis's accusations of "terror", and militia parole executions (1780–1781).** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 83, 132–135, 225, 290–295]
- **The war's nature:** the partisan war in South Carolina between Whigs (Patriots) and Tories (Loyalists) was a vicious neighbour-against-neighbour civil war [S1, p. 78–85].
- **The accusation:** on 3 Dec 1780, Cornwallis accused Marion of ruling by "the terror of his threats and cruelty of his punishments" [S5, Vol. 1, p. 298].
- **The comparison:** historians John Oller and Hugh Rankin note that Marion was remarkably humane and disciplined compared with:
  - Thomas Sumter, who paid his troops through systematic looting of Tory estates under "Sumter's Law";
  - Banastre Tarleton and James Wemyss, who burned 50+ homes and hanged prisoners [S1, p. 83, 132–135].
- **Marion's record** [S1, p. 225, 290–295]:
  - he strictly forbade plundering private homes, and threatened to shoot any of his own men who abused civilians;
  - he negotiated the June 1781 truce with the Loyalist Maj. Micajah Ganey, pardoning hundreds of Pee Dee Loyalists;
  - after the war, he fought in the South Carolina Senate to protect former Loyalists from having their property confiscated.
- **The harder side** [S1, p. 133–134]:
  - he did threaten to confiscate the property of, or shoot, militiamen who deserted to the British after taking the Patriot oath;
  - in late 1780–1781 his pickets occasionally executed notorious Tory guides and plunderers caught red-handed.

**Enslaved men in Marion's camp (Oscar Marion) vs. plantation ownership.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 15, 76–77, 152, 298]
- **Slaveholder:** like other Lowcountry planters, Marion was a slaveholder before and after the war; he owned Pond Bluff plantation, with ~20–70 enslaved workers [S1, p. 15, 298].
- **Black men in his camp:** during the war, several free and enslaved Black men served in Marion's partisan camp as scouts, boatmen, cooks and grooms [S1, p. 76–77].
- **Oscar Marion:** the best known is Marion's devoted personal assistant, trumpeter and bodyservant. He served alongside Marion for seven years and was officially recognized as a Patriot soldier by Presidential proclamation in 2006.
- **Not an abolitionist:** unlike in *The Patriot* movie, Marion did not fight to abolish slavery [S1, p. 76, 152].
- **Bounties:** under South Carolina law, enslaved people were occasionally used as bounty or labour by state authorities. Marion personally disliked the practice and refused to adopt it, unlike Sumter.

## 7. Common Myths and Errors to Avoid
**Myth 1: *The Patriot* movie (2000) portrayal ("Benjamin Martin").** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 1–4, 255, 296]
The 2000 Mel Gibson film loosely modelled its fictional hero "Benjamin Martin" on a composite of Francis Marion, Thomas Sumter, Andrew Pickens and Daniel Morgan [S1, p. 1–4]. Almost every biographical detail in it is false for Marion:
1. **Family:** the film's hero is a widower with seven children whose sons are killed by the Tarleton character. Marion was a lifelong bachelor throughout the war (48 in 1780) with no biological children. He did not marry his cousin, Mary Esther Videau, until April 1786, after the war, when he was 54 and she was 49 [S1, p. 3, 296].
2. **Battles:** Marion never fought at Cowpens or at Guilford Courthouse, where the movie's climactic battle is set; his major conventional battle in 1781 was Eutaw Springs [S1, p. 255].
3. **The church burning:** in the film, the Tarleton character ("Col. Tavington") locks townspeople inside a church and burns them alive. That never happened anywhere in the American Revolution; it was borrowed from the 1944 Nazi war crime at Oradour-sur-Glane [S1, p. 3].

**Myth 2: the roasted sweet-potato dinner story.**
- **The story:** in early 1781, a young British officer from Georgetown was led blindfolded into Marion's camp at Snow's Island to negotiate a prisoner exchange [S1, p. 168–170].
  - After the talks, Marion invited him to dinner: only roasted sweet potatoes served on a piece of pine bark, and water mixed with vinegar ("country punch").
  - The officer supposedly went back to Georgetown and resigned his commission, because "a people who could live on roots could never be conquered".
- **What is true:** Marion's veteran William Dobein James (1821, p. 93–94) explicitly confirms that the blindfolded officer's visit and the sweet-potato dinner on bark happened at Snow's Island. Sweet potatoes and lean beef without salt really were Marion's daily diet in winter 1780–81 [S6, p. 93–94; S1, p. 169].
- **What is myth:** James also notes that Parson Weems (1809) wildly embellished the speeches and invented the officer's resignation [S1, p. 169–170; S6, p. 94].
- **Labels:** the sweet-potato meal itself [VERIFIED BY EYEWITNESS JAMES 1821]; the officer resigning his commission [TRADITION/WEEMS INVENTION 1809] [S1, p. 168–170; S6, p. 93–94].

**Myth 3: the origin of the "Swamp Fox" nickname.** [TRADITION / 1821 PARTICIPANT MEMOIR] [S1, p. 126–127; S6, p. 67]
- **During the war:** letters by Cornwallis, Greene and Rawdon call him simply "Colonel Marion" or "General Marion" [S1, p. 126].
- **The source:** the nickname traces to Tarleton's exasperated curse when he gave up his 26-mile pursuit at Ox Swamp on 10 November 1780: "as for this damned old fox, the Devil himself could not catch him".
- **How it survived:** it was preserved orally among the Williamsburg veterans and first published in 1821 by Marion's militiaman William Dobein James (*A Sketch of the Life of Brig. Gen. Francis Marion*, p. 67).
- **How it spread:** William Gilmore Simms (1844) and Henry Wadsworth Longfellow then popularized it nationwide.

**Myth 4: Marion was an untrained backwoods amateur who only knew guerrilla fighting.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 18–45, 64]
- Before taking to the swamps in August 1780, he had five years of elite conventional service as a commissioned officer in the 2nd South Carolina Continental Regiment (1775–1780) [S1, p. 18–45]:
  - he commanded that regiment at the defense of Fort Sullivan (1776) and the Siege of Savannah (1779);
  - he had learned woodland scouting fighting the Cherokee as a lieutenant in 1759–1761.
- It was precisely his Continental discipline that made him a far more effective guerrilla commander than rivals like Thomas Sumter [S1, p. 64].

## Fact-Check List (15 Core Script Claims)
1. **Charleston escape.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 51–53; S6, p. 24]
   - In March 1780, Lt. Col. Francis Marion fractured his ankle jumping out of a second-story window to escape a locked drinking party hosted by Alexander McQueen.
   - Because of it, Gen. Benjamin Lincoln evacuated him as an unfit officer before the city was sealed, so he avoided capture with the 5,266-man American garrison.
2. **Set-up boast.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 62; S5, Aug 20, 1780]
   - On 20 August 1780, four days after routing Horatio Gates at Camden, Lord Cornwallis wrote to Lord George Germain: "The rebel forces being at present dispersed, the internal commotions and insurrections in the province will now subside."
3. **Pay-off admission.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 132; S5, Vol. 1, p. 298]
   - On 3 December 1780, Cornwallis wrote to Sir Henry Clinton: "Col. Marion had so wrought on the minds of the people... that there was scarcely an inhabitant between the Santee and Pedee, that was not in arms against us. Some parties had even crossed the Santee, and carried terror to the gates of Charles Town."
4. **Great Savannah raid.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 65–68; S6, p. 48–49]
   - Before dawn on 25 August 1780, at Great Savannah near Nelson's Ferry, Marion and 52 partisans seized the stacked muskets of the British escort.
   - They freed 150 captured Maryland and Delaware Continental soldiers and captured 22–24 British guards, at a cost of 1 American killed and 1 wounded.
5. **Black Mingo and Tearcoat Swamp.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 78–106; S6, p. 56–63]
   - Maj. James Wemyss burned 50 plantations in a 70-mile scorched-earth sweep.
   - Marion rode 60 miles south from North Carolina and routed Col. John Coming Ball's Loyalists at Black Mingo Creek (28–29 Sep 1780).
   - At midnight on 25 October 1780, he ambushed Lt. Col. Samuel Tynes's 200 sleeping Loyalists at Tearcoat Swamp: 6 killed, 14 wounded, and 23 men, 80 horses and 80 new muskets captured, with zero American casualties.
6. **Cornwallis sends Tarleton.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 119, 126; S5, Nov 5 & 10, 1780]
   - On 5 and 10 November 1780, Cornwallis ordered Lt. Col. Banastre Tarleton (~300–400 British Legion dragoons and the 63rd Foot) to "go down to Black River to try to get at Mr. Marion", because "I would rather have you strike a blow at Marion than anything else."
7. **The chase.** [VERIFIED chase / TRADITION 1821 for the exact quote] [S1, p. 121–126; S4, p. 170–172; S6, p. 65–67]
   - On the night of 8 November 1780, the widow Dorcas Richardson warned Marion of Tarleton's ambush at Richardson's Plantation.
   - On 9–10 November, Marion led Tarleton on a 7-hour, 26-mile chase across Woodyard and Pocotaligo Swamps into Ox Swamp, toward a prepared ambush at Benbow's Ferry.
   - There Tarleton gave up the chase and, according to the veteran William Dobein James (1821), declared: "Come my boys! Let us go back, and we will soon find the Gamecock, but as for this damned old fox, the Devil himself could not catch him!"
8. **Snow's Island and the cockade.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 22, 78, 138; S8]
   - Marion operated out of his secret base on Snow's Island (33.8189° N, 79.3275° W), at the confluence of the Pee Dee River, Lynches Creek and Clark's Creek.
   - His men wore the silver crescent cockade of the 2nd South Carolina Regiment (LIBERTY) on their caps, shown visually by the indigo-and-white Moultrie Flag.
9. **Fort Watson.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 198–203; S7, p. 277–278]
   - At the Siege of Fort Watson (15–23 April 1781), Marion had no artillery against a British stockade atop a 30-foot Indian mound.
   - On the night of 22–23 April, his officer Maj. Hezekiah Maham directed the overnight construction of a 40-foot rectangular log tower (the "Maham Tower").
   - At dawn riflemen fired down into the fort from it, forcing 114 British and Loyalist defenders to surrender.
10. **Greene's tribute.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 204; S9, Vol. 8, p. 142]
    - On 24 April 1781, Maj. Gen. Nathanael Greene wrote to Marion: "History will afford no instance of a partisan officer doing so much with such little means."
11. **Fort Motte.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 206–212; S7, p. 285–288]
    - At the Siege of Fort Motte (8–12 May 1781), with Lord Rawdon's relief column approaching, Marion and Light-Horse Harry Lee got the widow Rebecca Motte's permission, at hot, sunny noon on 12 May, to shoot flaming arrows onto the dry shingle roof of her mansion.
    - Meanwhile Marion's 6-pounder fired grapeshot at the roof.
    - Lt. Donald McPherson and 140–184 British/Hessian troops surrendered at 1:00 PM.
12. **Parker's Ferry.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 246–250; S6, p. 136–138]
    - On 30 August 1781, at Parker's Ferry on the Edisto River, Marion hid ~200–400 marksmen 40 yards from a narrow causeway.
    - He lured Maj. Thomas Fraser's Loyalist cavalry into a buckshot crossfire: ~100–125 British casualties and 27 horses killed, at a cost of 1 American wounded.
13. **Eutaw Springs.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 255–264; S6, p. 144]
    - Nine days later (8 September 1781), at the Battle of Eutaw Springs, Marion commanded the Carolina militia in the front line under Nathanael Greene.
    - In stifling heat his men fired 17 rounds each against British regulars, helping to inflict 692 to 866 British casualties and forcing the British to retreat to Charleston.
14. **Myth corrections.** [VERIFIED] [S1, p. 1–4, 296, 312]
    - No authentic life portrait of Marion exists: all paintings, such as John Blake White's 1836 and William Ranney's 1849 works, are posthumous reconstructions.
    - Unlike in *The Patriot*, Marion was a 48-year-old childless bachelor during the war and did not marry until 1786.
15. **The sweet-potato dinner.** [VERIFIED meal / TRADITION for the resignation] [S1, p. 168–170; S6, p. 93–94]
    - Marion's veteran William Dobein James (1821) confirmed that Marion really did serve a blindfolded British truce officer a dinner of roasted sweet potatoes on pine bark at Snow's Island.
    - James also noted that Parson Weems (1809) invented the florid dialogue and the claim that the officer resigned his commission afterwards.
