Seven years earlier, that general was in a prison cell. Stalin's own secret police had arrested him. By his family's account, they knocked out his teeth, broke his ribs, and twice marched him out to a mock execution. He never signed a false confession.

## BACKGROUND (~1:25 – ~2:45)

[ARCHIVE: Soviet cavalry of the 1920s-30s / Red Army parade film (public-domain newsreel); headline card]
Rokossovsky was born in eighteen ninety-six, in the Polish lands of the Russian Empire. He was a cavalryman: a dragoon in the First World War, then a Red Army cavalry commander in the Civil War. Tall, calm and polite, he was known as an officer who listened to his men. In the summer of nineteen thirty-seven, Stalin's purge of the army reached him. He was arrested, accused of spying for Poland and Japan, and held until March nineteen forty, when he was suddenly released and given back a command.

[MAP: bg-1 | East overview, 22 June 1941 (option 1, 1941 border): three big red arrows (Army Groups North, Centre, South) drive east; blue counter "9 MECH CORPS" near Dubno is hit, its number counts 316 -> 64 tanks (tick)]
Fifteen months later, on the twenty-second of June, nineteen forty-one, Germany invaded the Soviet Union. Rokossovsky led the Ninth Mechanised Corps into the counter-attacks near Dubno. Within two weeks, his three hundred and sixteen tanks were down to sixty-four. But he had learned something the hard way: German armour could not be stopped by throwing men and tanks at it head-on. It had to be ambushed, worn down and made to bleed.

[MAP: bg-2 | East overview, October 1941 (option 1): German pincers close around Vyazma and Bryansk; huge red rings; stamp OVER HALF A MILLION ENCIRCLED; arrows point at Moscow]
By October, the Germans were driving on Moscow. In a giant double encirclement at Vyazma and Bryansk, more than half a million Soviet soldiers were cut off. The road to the capital was almost empty. Rokossovsky's headquarters escaped the trap, and he was handed a scratch force and one job: hold the Volokolamsk highway, one of the main roads into Moscow.

## MOVE 1 — BEND, DON'T BREAK: THE VOLOKOLAMSK HIGHWAY (~2:45 – ~6:50)

[MAP: move1-1 | Title card MOVE 1 · THE VOLOKOLAMSK HIGHWAY · OCTOBER-DECEMBER 1941. Moscow close-up map (option 2: two-colour glowing front lines only). Volokolamsk, Istra, Solnechnogorsk, Kryukovo, Moscow labelled small; the highway glows; blue 16th Army counters thin along the line: 316 RIFLE DIV (Panfilov), DOVATOR CAVALRY, CADETS; red 4th Panzer Group counters (Hoepner badge) mass to the west]
The Sixteenth Army was an army in name only. It had the 316th Rifle Division under General Ivan Panfilov, a cavalry group under Lev Dovator, a regiment of officer cadets from Moscow, and not much else. Against them came Erich Hoepner's Fourth Panzer Group, the same armoured force that had just helped close the trap at Vyazma.

[MAP: move1-2 | Red arrows probe east along the highway; red ring on the blue line: "THEY WILL STAND AND DIE" caption (enemy belief); faint ghost of the Vyazma ring behind]
Hoepner expected what the Germans had seen all summer. Soviet armies stood in place, were outflanked and surrounded, and died where they stood. The panzers would break the line, encircle what was left and roll down the highway to Moscow.

[MAP: move1-3 | Rokossovsky badge. Close-up: anti-tank strongpoint icons appear on every road junction and village along the highway (pop each); in the woods behind, hidden blue tank counters (Katukov 1 GDS TANK BDE) with dotted ambush arcs]
Rokossovsky saw something different. He knew his army could not win a straight fight. So he would not give the Germans one. He built his defence around the roads, because tanks needed roads in this country of forests and marshes. Every key village and junction became an anti-tank strongpoint, with guns dug in to cover the approaches. Behind them, in the woods, he kept his few tanks hidden.

[MAP: move1-4 | A red panzer column rolls along the road; blue tanks emerge from the woods on its flank, fire (K.gun), impacts on 3-4 red tanks, the blue tanks pull back into the trees; red column halts. Caption: AMBUSH, FIRE, MOVE]
Those tanks belonged to Colonel Mikhail Katukov, who had already shown near Mtsensk how a few tanks could beat many. They waited in ambush, fired on the German columns from the flank at short range, and then pulled back before the Germans could reply, only to appear again somewhere else. The Germans found themselves losing tanks to an enemy they rarely saw.

[MAP: move1-5 | Volokolamsk falls (27 Oct: red flows in, town flickers red); the blue front band MOVES back a few km, intact; red arrows stall; date 27 OCT 1941; mud overlay, clouds]
When a position could no longer hold, Rokossovsky pulled his troops back to the next one, before they could be surrounded. Volokolamsk fell at the end of October. But the Sixteenth Army did not break, and it did not disappear into a pocket. Then the autumn rains turned the roads to mud, and the German advance slowed to a crawl.

[MAP: move1-6 | 16 NOV 1941: frost overlay (snow), red arrows surge again at Dubosekovo (target ring), big red push; small text card: THE "28 PANFILOV MEN" = SOVIET PROPAGANDA (the fight was real)]
In mid-November, with the ground frozen hard, the Germans attacked again. Panfilov's division took the full weight of it, and Panfilov himself was killed two days later. Soviet newspapers later invented a story of twenty-eight of his men destroying eighteen tanks before dying to the last man. That legend was a fabrication. The real fighting was desperate enough without it.

[MAP: move1-7 | Camera on the Istra reservoir and river (water shimmer): blue proposed line drawn behind the water (dashed), arrow back; Zhukov badge appears: stamp REFUSED; Shaposhnikov card: APPROVED; then Zhukov: CANCELLED (hit on each)]
Then Rokossovsky made the boldest suggestion of the battle. Behind him lay the Istra reservoir and the Istra river, a natural water barrier. He asked to pull his army back behind it, where a small force could hold the crossings and the rest could rest and regroup. His front commander, Georgy Zhukov, refused. According to Rokossovsky's own memoirs, he then went over Zhukov's head to the Chief of the General Staff, Marshal Shaposhnikov, who approved the withdrawal. Zhukov cancelled it at once. Every man would stand where he was.

[MAP: move1-8 | 25 NOV: red pushes over the frozen reservoir, Istra and Solnechnogorsk fall; blue band falls back line by line (Istra -> Kryukovo -> Krasnaya Polyana) but stays unbroken; red recon arrow reaches Khimki: ring + label 30 KM FROM THE KREMLIN; temperature card -28 °C]
The Germans crossed the frozen reservoir anyway, and the line was pushed back to Istra, then to Kryukovo and Krasnaya Polyana. But it bent without breaking. Rokossovsky fed in every reinforcement he could find, held each village as long as it could be held, and never let the panzers get around him. By early December, German scouts were barely thirty kilometres from the Kremlin. They would get no closer. The temperature dropped to almost thirty degrees below zero, German engines froze, and the panzers were spent.

[MAP: move1-9 | 5-6 DEC 1941: counteroffensive. Fresh blue arrows burst out along the whole front; the two-colour band moves WEST; Kryukovo and Istra flip blue (flicker); red counters retreat; Hoepner badge greyed: DISMISSED 8 JAN 1942]
On the fifth and sixth of December, the Red Army struck back all around Moscow. Rokossovsky's army, which the Germans had been trying to destroy for two months, was still there to join the attack. Within days it had retaken Kryukovo, then Istra. A month later, Hitler sacked Erich Hoepner for pulling his troops back without permission.

[MAP: move1-10 | Result card: CLOSEST GERMAN APPROACH ~30 KM · 16TH ARMY NEVER ENCIRCLED · MOSCOW SAVED; method line appears: BEND, DON'T BREAK]
The Germans had come to Moscow expecting another Vyazma. Instead, they met an army that gave ground only to keep fighting, and was still alive when the counter-attack came. That was Rokossovsky's first lesson. Bend, don't break.

## MOVE 2 — READ THE BLOW BEFORE IT LANDS: KURSK, PONYRI (~6:50 – ~11:20)

[MAP: move2-1 | Title card MOVE 2 · THE NORTHERN FACE OF KURSK · JULY 1943. Kursk salient wide (option 1, Jul 43 control): the blue bulge between Orel (red) and Belgorod (red); USSR + Reich flags; Central Front (Rokossovsky) north, Voronezh Front south; counters: 711,575 vs ~335,000]
By the summer of nineteen forty-three, the front had a huge bulge around the city of Kursk. Rokossovsky now commanded the Central Front on its northern face: more than seven hundred thousand men. Facing him from the north was the German Ninth Army under Walther Model, about three hundred and thirty-five thousand men, with hundreds of tanks, including the new Tigers and the huge Ferdinand tank destroyers.

[MAP: move2-2 | Two big red ghost arrows from north and south cut off the bulge; stamp CITADEL; red belief card: "BREAK THROUGH, MEET AT KURSK"]
The German plan was called Operation Citadel. Model would strike south, another German army would strike north from Belgorod, and they would meet at Kursk, trapping everything inside the bulge. Model believed that enough heavy armour, concentrated in one place, could smash through any defence.

[MAP: move2-3 | Rokossovsky badge. His front glows: ~300 KM; the Orel-Kursk railway glows; camera pushes to Ponyri / Olkhovatka; a 40 KM bracket appears; dense blue counters + artillery silhouettes pile into it (13TH ARMY, 4TH ARTILLERY BREAKTHROUGH CORPS) while the rest of the line thins]
Rokossovsky's front was almost three hundred kilometres long. He could not be strong everywhere, so he had to guess where Model would hit. He studied the ground and the railway that ran from Orel to Kursk, and concluded that the blow would fall right there, along the railway towards Ponyri and the high ground at Olkhovatka. So he took a huge risk. He packed one army, the Thirteenth, into a sector only about forty kilometres wide, and gave it an entire artillery corps.

[MAP: move2-4 | Ponyri close-up (option 2, lines only): three defensive belts draw on as glowing blue lines in depth; minefield hatching, anti-tank strongpoints (pop), dug-in guns; label THREE BELTS · MINES · ANTI-TANK STRONGPOINTS]
Then he dug. For three months, his troops built three defensive belts in depth, one behind the other, with trenches, minefields and anti-tank strongpoints, each one a cluster of guns set to fire into the flanks of passing tanks. Even if the Germans broke one belt, there would be another behind it, and another behind that.

[MAP: move2-5 | NIGHT (K.night) 4/5 JULY: a small red sapper counter captured (ring, radio static); clock card 02:20; Soviet guns fire all along the line (K.gun salvos), shells arc onto German assembly areas, impacts (shake on each)]
On the night of the fourth of July, Soviet troops captured German sappers clearing mines in front of the line. They said the attack would come at dawn. With Zhukov's agreement, Rokossovsky gave an extraordinary order: his own artillery would fire first. In the early hours of the fifth of July, hundreds of Soviet guns opened up on the German assembly areas, hours before the German attack was due. It did less damage than hoped, but it delayed the assault and showed the Germans that they had lost surprise.

[MAP: move2-6 | DAWN 5 JULY: day returns; red barrage, then red counters + Ferdinand/Tiger silhouettes push south on a narrow front; minefield flashes (impacts), red counters thin; the first blue belt bends]
Model attacked anyway. His infantry and tanks pushed into the first belt and ran straight into the minefields. The Germans broke into the Soviet defences, but every kilometre cost them men and tanks, and behind the first belt was the second.

[MAP: move2-7 | Ponyri station target ring; red and blue flags swap repeatedly on the station (flicker), smoke columns; Ferdinands isolated in a fire sack: blue guns on both flanks fire, red silhouettes burn; caption: FIRE SACK]
The fight for Ponyri lasted almost a week. The village and its railway station changed hands again and again, and the battle has been called a little Stalingrad. German heavy armour broke into the Soviet positions, but the infantry following it was cut down by artillery. The great Ferdinands found themselves alone in fields of mines, under fire from guns on both flanks. Some were knocked out by Soviet infantry who climbed onto them.

[MAP: move2-8 | Olkhovatka heights: red arrows hit the ridge, bounce; Rokossovsky shifts reserves (blue arrows from quiet sectors, tank counters move in); red push stalls; label 10-15 KM]
At Olkhovatka, Model threw in his reserves to take the high ground. Rokossovsky was ready, shifting tanks and guns from quiet parts of his front to meet each blow. After a week of fighting, the Ninth Army had advanced barely ten to fifteen kilometres. It had not even broken through the main defences.

[MAP: move2-9 | 12 JULY: blue arrows hit the Orel bulge from north and east behind Model (Operation Kutuzov); red counters pull back north; stamp CITADEL FAILED]
On the twelfth of July, Soviet armies attacked the Orel bulge behind Model, and he had to pull his divisions back to save his own rear. In the north, Operation Citadel was over. It was the last great German offensive in the east.

[MAP: move2-10 | Casualty card (USSR flag / Reich flag): CENTRAL FRONT 15,336 killed/missing · 18,561 wounded (33,897) | GERMAN 9TH ARMY ~20,000-23,000 casualties; then the method line BEND, DON'T BREAK · READ THE BLOW BEFORE IT LANDS]
Rokossovsky's Central Front lost almost thirty-four thousand men in the defensive battle, about fifteen thousand of them killed or missing. The Ninth Army lost over twenty thousand, and its strength as an attacking force was gone for good. Rokossovsky had read where the blow would fall, and had built the defence to absorb it. Bend, don't break. Read the blow before it lands.

## MOVE 3 — STRIKE WHERE THE MAP SAYS YOU CAN'T: BAGRATION (~11:20 – ~16:00)

[MAP: move3-1 | Title card MOVE 3 · OPERATION BAGRATION · JUNE 1944. Belarus wide (option 1, Jun 44 control): the red "balcony" of Army Group Centre bulging east; Bobruisk, Minsk, Vitebsk, Mogilev; Busch badge; red armour arrows drain SOUTH to Army Group North Ukraine; Pripyat marsh texture]
A year later, the Germans still held a great bulge in Belarus, defended by Army Group Centre under Field Marshal Ernst Busch. The German high command expected the next Soviet summer offensive to come further south, in Ukraine, and moved much of its armour there. In Belarus, they trusted the terrain: dense forests, rivers and the vast Pripyat marshes.

[MAP: move3-2 | Bobruisk close-up (option 2): Rokossovsky's sector; the Berezina river splits it; marsh hatching; Rogachev (north) and Parichi (south) labelled; one big ghost arrow (Stavka: ONE MAIN BLOW) vs two arrows (Rokossovsky: TWO BREAKTHROUGHS)]
Rokossovsky's First Belorussian Front faced the German Ninth Army around Bobruisk. Soviet practice was to make one main blow, concentrating everything in one place. But Rokossovsky studied his sector and saw that the rivers and marshes split it in two. A single attack could be blocked. So he planned two breakthroughs: one in the north, from Rogachev, and one in the south, near Parichi, straight through the marshes.

[MAP: move3-3 | Dark Kremlin card (no likeness): STALIN vs ROKOSSOVSKY text duel: "ONE BLOW" / "TWO BLOWS"; door icon: "GO AND THINK IT OVER" x2 (tick each); then quote card: "YOUR CONFIDENCE SPEAKS FOR YOUR SOUND JUDGEMENT"]
According to Rokossovsky's own memoirs, Stalin did not like the plan, and told him to go into the next room and think it over. He came back with the same answer. He was sent out a second time, and again he came back with the same plan. Finally Stalin agreed: "Your confidence speaks for your sound judgement." Zhukov later remembered the meeting differently. But the two blows went ahead.

[MAP: move3-4 | Southern marsh close-up: engineers' corduroy log roads draw across the marsh (dashed brown), small raft/boat icons, blue infantry counters with "SWAMP SHOES" label; red outposts thin on dry ground only; caption: THEY THOUGHT NO ONE COULD CROSS]
For the southern blow, his men prepared to cross ground the Germans thought no army could cross. Engineers laid roads of logs over the bog. Infantry practised moving through the swamps on wide wooden swamp-shoes, with rafts for their guns and machine guns. The Germans had built their strongest defences on the dry ground and the roads. In the marshes, they kept only a thin screen.

[MAP: move3-5 | 24 JUNE: artillery barrage on both sectors (K.gun salvos, impacts with shake); northern blue arrow from Rogachev advances slowly (stalls, red holds); southern arrow (65TH ARMY, 1 GDS TANK CORPS, PLIEV) bursts out of the marsh and races north-west; c47g-style Soviet bombers (blue) fly a bombing run (K.bombRun) on German positions]
On the twenty-fourth of June, both blows went in. In the north, the attack from Rogachev ran into strong German defences and made slow progress. But in the south, the Sixty-Fifth Army came out of the marshes where the Germans least expected it, broke through the thin screen, and Soviet tanks and cavalry poured into the gap. The blow the Germans thought impossible was the one that worked.

[MAP: move3-6 | 25-27 JUNE: northern arrow (9TH TANK CORPS) breaks through too; two blue arrows race around Bobruisk on both sides of the Berezina and meet west of the city; ring closes with stamp TRAPPED (hit); red counters inside]
A day later, the northern blow broke through as well. Now the two arms raced around Bobruisk. On the twenty-seventh of June, they met, and a large part of the German Ninth Army was trapped east of the Berezina and in the city itself.

[MAP: move3-7 | Pocket: Soviet bombers (blue c47g, few, spaced) fly bombing runs over the trapped columns (K.bombRun, shake), artillery impacts, smoke columns; red counters turn grey; 29 JUNE: Bobruisk flips blue; Busch badge: RELIEVED 28 JUNE]
The trapped Germans tried to break out. Soviet bombers caught their columns on the roads and the riverbanks, and the pocket was smashed. Tens of thousands of German soldiers were killed or captured. On the twenty-eighth, Hitler sacked Busch. On the twenty-ninth, Bobruisk fell, and the same day, Rokossovsky was made a Marshal of the Soviet Union.

[MAP: move3-8 | Zoom out to Belarus (option 1): blue arrows from four fronts stream west to Minsk; red balcony collapses; Minsk encircled (3 JULY); control overlay cross-fades Jun 44 -> late Jul 44; counter ~400,000 GERMAN LOSSES (WHOLE OPERATION)]
Bobruisk was only one part of Operation Bagration. Four Soviet fronts attacked at once, and by early July, Minsk had fallen and a huge hole had been torn in the German line. By the end of the summer, Army Group Centre had lost around four hundred thousand men, and the Red Army had advanced hundreds of kilometres, to the gates of Warsaw.

[MAP: move3-9 | Casualty card Bobruisk: GERMAN ~50,000 killed · ~20,000 captured (Bobruisk) | whole operation ~400,000 German losses; method line complete: BEND, DON'T BREAK · READ THE BLOW BEFORE IT LANDS · STRIKE WHERE THE MAP SAYS YOU CAN'T]
At Bobruisk alone, some fifty thousand Germans were killed and twenty thousand captured. Rokossovsky had looked at a map that said no army could attack through the marshes, and attacked through them anyway. Bend, don't break. Read the blow before it lands. Strike where the map says you can't.

## ENDING (~16:00 – ~17:20)

[ARCHIVE: Parade of the Vanquished, Moscow 17 July 1944: German prisoners marching (public-domain newsreel if licensed; headline card "57,000 GERMAN PRISONERS MARCH THROUGH MOSCOW")]
On the seventeenth of July, nineteen forty-four, fifty-seven thousand German prisoners, most of them captured in Belarus, were marched through the streets of Moscow. Three years earlier, the German army had been at the gates of that city. Now its soldiers were walking through it as prisoners.

[ARCHIVE: Victory Parade, Red Square, 24 June 1945: Rokossovsky on a black horse (public-domain film if licensed)]
A year later, at the great Victory Parade in Red Square, Zhukov took the salute. The man who commanded the parade, riding out across the square, was Konstantin Rokossovsky, the officer Stalin's police had once beaten in a prison cell.

[MAP: end-1 | East overview: the three moves replay as rings on Moscow, Kursk and Bobruisk with their dates; final method card; Rokossovsky badge centre]
Rokossovsky never had Zhukov's fame. But in three of the greatest battles of the war, he did what few commanders could. At Moscow, he bent without breaking. At Kursk, he read the blow before it landed. And in Belarus, he struck where the map said he couldn't.
