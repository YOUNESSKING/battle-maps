## BACKGROUND (~1:00 – ~2:00)

[ARCHIVE: US Army portrait of Collins (PD, 3D depth); Guadalcanal / Pacific Signal Corps film; headline date card "6 JUNE 1944 / UTAH BEACH"]
Collins was born in New Orleans in eighteen ninety-six and graduated from West Point in nineteen seventeen. In the Pacific, he led the Twenty-Fifth Infantry Division on Guadalcanal, a division known as Tropic Lightning, and he came out of the jungle with a nickname: Lightning Joe. Eisenhower and Bradley wanted fighters for the invasion of France. So in nineteen forty-four, Collins was given the Seventh Corps, and the job of landing at Utah Beach.

[MAP: bg-1 | Western Front overview (option 1, June 1944): occupied Europe red, Britain + US flags, emblem on Germany; D-Day beaches; arrow from Utah Beach into the Cotentin; ring on Cherbourg: THE ONLY DEEP-WATER PORT; supply ships pile up off the beaches (counter of ships / tons)]
On the sixth of June, his troops came ashore at Utah Beach. But a landing is only the start. Every bullet, every shell and every gallon of fuel for the armies in France had to come across the Channel, and the Allies had no proper port. The nearest one was Cherbourg, at the northern tip of the Cotentin peninsula. And the Germans had turned it into a fortress.

## MOVE 1 — CUT THEM OFF: CHERBOURG (~2:00 – ~6:10)

[MAP: move1-1 | Title card MOVE 1 · CHERBOURG · JUNE 1944. Cotentin wide (option 1, 14 June control): US beachhead around Utah / Carentan, German-held peninsula north; VII Corps counters (4th, 9th, 79th, 90th, 82nd Airborne) vs German counters (Schlieben badge); Cherbourg fortress ring glows red]
In mid-June, the Americans held a beachhead at the base of the peninsula. North of them, the German garrison under General Karl-Wilhelm von Schlieben held the whole of the Cotentin, with Cherbourg and its ring of forts at the top. Hitler ordered Cherbourg held to the last man.

[MAP: move1-2 | Red belief card: "HOLD FOR MONTHS"; red counters fall back north into the fortress ring; ghost arrow of German reinforcements coming up the peninsula from the south]
The Germans believed the fortress could hold out for weeks, perhaps months. Every week the Allies went without a port, their armies in France would be starved of supplies. And as long as the peninsula stayed open to the south, reinforcements could still reach the garrison.

[MAP: move1-3 | Collins badge. Blue 9th Division arrow drives WEST across the neck of the peninsula to the sea at Barneville (18 JUNE stamp); the two-colour front swings into a wall across the peninsula; the ghost reinforcement arrow is cut (hit); stamp CUT OFF]
Collins saw that before he could take the port, he had to seal it off. So instead of driving straight north, he sent the Ninth Division west, straight across the neck of the peninsula. On the eighteenth of June, it reached the sea at Barneville. The Germans in the north were now cut off. No reinforcements could reach them, and none of them could escape.

[MAP: move1-4 | Camera turns north: three blue division arrows (4th, 9th, 79th) swing north and race up the peninsula side by side; red counters fall back into the fortress ring; dates tick 19 -> 21 JUNE]
Then Collins turned all three of his infantry divisions north at once. They raced up the peninsula, and by the twenty-first of June, the Germans had been driven back into the ring of forts around Cherbourg. Collins sent Schlieben an ultimatum: surrender by nine o'clock the next morning.

[MAP: move1-5 | Cherbourg close-up (option 2: glowing fronts only), 22 JUNE 12:40: glowing c47g fighter-bombers + bombers sweep in (K.bombRun x2, shake), impacts on the forts; 14:00: three blue arrows hit the perimeter at once; clock card 14:00]
The deadline passed. At twelve forty on the twenty-second of June, hundreds of Allied aircraft began bombing and strafing the German defences. At two in the afternoon, all three divisions attacked together. Collins did not want a siege. He wanted to hit the fortress everywhere at once, before the garrison could catch its breath.

[MAP: move1-6 | Fort du Roule on its ridge above the city (target ring); 25 JUNE: blue infantry climb the slope, pole-charge blasts at the bunker embrasures (impacts + shake), Medal of Honor icons for Kelly and Ogden; the fort flips blue]
For three days, the Americans fought through pillboxes and concrete strongpoints. The key was Fort du Roule, a fortress built into a rock ridge above the city. On the twenty-fifth of June, Corporal John Kelly crawled up to its bunkers again and again with pole charges, blasting the gun ports at point-blank range. Kelly, and Lieutenant Carlos Ogden, who knocked out a German gun and two machine guns despite his wounds, both received the Medal of Honor. By nightfall, the upper fort was in American hands.

[MAP: move1-7 | 26 JUNE: tunnel entrance at Saint-Sauveur glows; blue tank destroyers fire into it (impacts); a white flag; ~800 grey counters file out; Schlieben badge greyed: SURRENDERED; Collins badge: offer card "ORDER A GENERAL SURRENDER?" -> Schlieben: REFUSED (hit)]
The next day, American tank destroyers fired straight into the entrances of the tunnels where Schlieben had his headquarters. He came out with around eight hundred men and surrendered. Collins offered him the means to order the rest of the garrison to surrender too. Schlieben refused. So the Americans cleared the city strongpoint by strongpoint, using loudspeakers to talk the last defenders out of the arsenal.

[MAP: move1-8 | The harbour: red demolition blasts tick across the docks (impacts), sunken ships, mines; stamp PORT DESTROYED; date cards 1 JULY (Cap de la Hague cleared) and 16 JULY (first cargo); result card]
By the first of July, the whole peninsula was in American hands. Before they gave up, the Germans had wrecked the harbour so thoroughly that the first cargo did not come ashore until the middle of July. But the Allies had their port, and the fortress Hitler expected to hold for months had fallen in less than a week of assault.

[MAP: move1-9 | Casualty card, labelled COTENTIN CAMPAIGN 6 JUNE - 1 JULY: US 2,800 killed · 13,500 wounded · 5,700 missing | GERMAN ~39,000 captured; then the method card line 1: CUT THEM OFF, THEN NEVER LET THEM DIG IN]
The campaign for the Cotentin cost the Americans more than twenty-two thousand casualties, among them twenty-eight hundred dead. Around thirty-nine thousand Germans were taken prisoner. Collins had sealed the fortress off before he attacked it, and then gave its defenders no time to settle in. Cut them off, then never let them dig in.

## MOVE 2 — THROW THE TANKS INTO THE CHAOS: OPERATION COBRA (~6:10 – ~10:50)

[MAP: move2-1 | Title card MOVE 2 · OPERATION COBRA · JULY 1944. Normandy wide (option 1, 24 July control): the Allied lodgement, British/Canadian sector at Caen, US sector to the west; front almost static; date ticks JUNE -> JULY with a casualties counter climbing; hedgerow texture overlay; stamp STALEMATE]
By July, the Allied armies in Normandy were stuck. In the American sector, the country was divided into thousands of small fields, each one walled in by ancient hedgerows, the bocage. Every field was a fortress. The Americans were paying for every few hundred yards with thousands of casualties.

[MAP: move2-2 | Cobra close-up (option 2 glowing fronts): the Saint-Lo - Periers road glows; red Panzer Lehr counters (Bayerlein badge) dug in behind it; label ~2,200 COMBAT TROOPS · ~45 ARMOURED VEHICLES; red belief card: "BLEED THEM IN THE HEDGEROWS"]
Across the road from Saint-Lô to Périers, the German line was held by the Panzer Lehr Division under General Fritz Bayerlein. After weeks of fighting, it was down to about two thousand two hundred combat troops and some forty-five tanks and assault guns. But in this country, that was enough. The Germans believed the hedgerows would let them bleed the Americans indefinitely.

[MAP: move2-3 | Bradley + Collins badges; the plan draws on: a target box ~6,000 x 2,200 yards just south of the road (dashed rectangle), VII Corps divisions lined up behind it: 9th, 4th, 30th infantry in front, 2nd + 3rd Armored and 1st Infantry behind; arrows labelled BREAK IN / BREAK OUT]
General Omar Bradley's answer was Operation Cobra, and Collins's Seventh Corps would be its spearhead. Heavy bombers would saturate a narrow box of German positions just south of the road. Then three infantry divisions would punch a hole, and Collins would pour his armour through it.

[MAP: move2-4 | 24 JULY: bombers come in, the strike is called off mid-way; some bombs fall short on the blue line (impacts, shake), card 25 KILLED · 131 WOUNDED; stamp POSTPONED]
On the twenty-fourth of July, the bombers were called back because of bad weather. But some did not get the message, and their bombs fell on American troops, killing twenty-five men. The Germans now knew something big was coming. Bradley ordered the attack to go ahead the next day anyway.

[MAP: move2-5 | 25 JULY: the full strike: waves of glowing c47g heavy bombers (K.bombRun, many runs, spaced), the box disappears under impacts and smoke (shake on every run); counters tick 1,500 HEAVIES · 3,300 TONS, then +380 MEDIUMS +550 FIGHTER-BOMBERS · ~4,000 TONS; red Panzer Lehr counters flip over / grey]
On the morning of the twenty-fifth, more than fifteen hundred heavy bombers came over, followed by hundreds of medium bombers and fighter-bombers. Around four thousand tons of bombs fell on and around the target box. Tanks were thrown over, trenches buried, and the Panzer Lehr Division lost around a thousand men in a single morning.

[MAP: move2-6 | Short bombs again on the blue line (impacts, red flash), McNair three-star badge greyed; card 111 KILLED · 490 WOUNDED; blue infantry arrows advance slowly, stop at surviving red strongpoints (MG fire, impacts); caption THE INFANTRY STALL]
But once again, bombs fell short. A hundred and eleven Americans were killed and nearly five hundred wounded. Among the dead was Lieutenant General Lesley McNair, who had come forward to watch. Shaken, the infantry went forward into the craters, and found that some Germans had survived. By evening, they had gained only a mile or two. They had not secured the objectives the plan required.

[MAP: move2-7 | Collins badge alone, dusk; two paths drawn: WAIT (grey arrow, ticking clock, red counters reforming) vs COMMIT THE ARMOUR (blue tank counters); the blue path lights: stamp THE GAMBLE (hit)]
This was the moment Collins had to decide. The cautious choice was to wait until the infantry had cleared a clean breach. But Collins could see that the German line was shattered, not yet broken. If he waited, the survivors would reorganise and plug the gap. So on the afternoon of the twenty-fifth, he decided to send his armour in the next morning, before the breakthrough was secure. Historians have called it a gamble.

[MAP: move2-8 | 26 JULY: 2nd Armored + 1st Infantry with 3rd Armored's CCB smash into the line (tank counters, arrows, impacts, red counters grey); 27 JULY: Marigny ring flips blue, 3rd Armored joins; Bayerlein badge: stamp ANNIHILATED; the two-colour front band moves south]
On the twenty-sixth of July, the Second Armored Division and the First Infantry Division, with part of the Third Armored, drove into the wreckage. The German line gave way. On the twenty-seventh, Marigny fell, and Bayerlein reported that Panzer Lehr had been finally annihilated.

[MAP: move2-9 | Zoom out to Normandy (option 1, control 24 July -> 31 July cross-fade): blue arrows race south to Coutances (28 JULY) and Avranches (30 JULY); Patton badge appears at the gap (1 AUG THIRD ARMY), arrows fan into Brittany and east; result card: SHORT BOMBS 25 + 111 KILLED · PANZER LEHR DESTROYED · AVRANCHES IN 6 DAYS; method line 2]
Now the American tanks were out of the hedgerows and into open country. Coutances fell on the twenty-eighth, and by the thirtieth they were at Avranches, the gateway out of Normandy. On the first of August, Patton's Third Army came into action and drove through that gateway into France. The man who had opened it was Collins. Cut them off, then never let them dig in. Throw the tanks into the chaos.

## MOVE 3 — HIT THE SPEARHEAD WHEN IT RUNS DRY: CELLES (~10:50 – ~15:20)

[MAP: move3-1 | Title card MOVE 3 · CELLES · DECEMBER 1944. Western Front overview -> Ardennes (option 1, 16 Dec -> 23 Dec control): the red Bulge pushes west; Bastogne ringed; a red spearhead arrow (2ND PANZER DIV) races toward the Meuse at Dinant; UK flag on the Meuse; snow overlay]
On the sixteenth of December, Hitler launched his last great offensive in the west. German armies smashed into the thin American line in the Ardennes forest in Belgium. Their goal was to cross the River Meuse and drive on to the port of Antwerp, splitting the Allied armies in two.

[MAP: move3-2 | Ardennes close-up (option 2): 2nd Panzer spearhead (Lüttwitz badge) at Foy-Notre-Dame / Celles; ring on the Dinant bridges; bracket 4-5 MILES; red fuel gauge icon drains to EMPTY; stamp OUT OF FUEL]
By the twenty-third of December, the German Second Panzer Division had raced further west than any other German unit. Its spearhead reached Foy-Notre-Dame and Celles, just four or five miles from the bridges over the Meuse at Dinant. But it had outrun its supplies. Its tanks were almost out of fuel.

[MAP: move3-3 | Collins badge; VII Corps arrives from the north (arrows), 2nd Armored (Harmon badge) on the German flank; Montgomery badge appears (UK flag): card from First Army: "GO ON THE DEFENSIVE · YOU MAY FALL BACK" with a dashed line back to ANDENNE - HOTTON - MANHAY]
Collins's Seventh Corps had been rushed south to meet the offensive. Its Second Armored Division, under the fiery General Ernest Harmon, was now on the German flank. But on the evening of the twenty-fourth, a message reached Collins from First Army. Field Marshal Montgomery, who now commanded the northern half of the battle, and General Hodges had agreed that Seventh Corps should go on the defensive. If necessary, Collins was authorised to pull back.

[MAP: move3-4 | Split choice: FALL BACK (grey dashed arrow north) vs ATTACK (Harmon's planned arrows around Celles); Collins weighs it (clock ticks, Christmas Eve); the attack path lights: stamp ATTACK AT DAWN (hit)]
Collins and Harmon had already planned an attack. Collins looked at the situation. The panzers ahead of him were stalled and almost out of fuel. If he pulled back, they would get their fuel and reach the Meuse. If he attacked now, he could catch them before they recovered. Using the freedom his orders gave him, Collins chose to attack on Christmas morning.

[MAP: move3-5 | 25 DEC 08:00: Combat Command B arrows swing around Celles from both sides (double envelopment), British tanks (UK flag) hold Dinant and Foy-Notre-Dame; glowing c47g P-38s and Typhoons (few, spaced) strafe and bomb the stalled panzers (K.bombRun, shake); red counters grey; ring closes: stamp IN THE BAG]
At eight o'clock on Christmas morning, Harmon's Combat Command B swept around Celles from two directions. British tanks held the river line behind. And for the first time in days, the skies were clear. American P-38s and British Typhoons dived on the stranded German columns. By the afternoon, the German spearhead was surrounded. Harmon reportedly radioed his headquarters: the bastards are in the bag.

[MAP: move3-6 | 26-27 DEC: German relief attacks (red arrows) from Rochefort are beaten back (impacts); the pocket is crushed; the Bulge's tip is snapped off; zoom out: the red bulge stops short of the Meuse; stamp THE DEEPEST POINT]
Over the next two days, German attempts to break through to the trapped panzers were beaten off, and the pocket was destroyed. It was the deepest point the German offensive ever reached. From here, the Battle of the Bulge would be a German retreat.

[MAP: move3-7 | Casualty card (Cole, 2nd Armored operation 24-27 Dec): GERMAN 82 tanks · 83 guns · 441 vehicles · 1,213 prisoners | US 27 tanks · 17 killed · 26 missing · 201 wounded; method card complete: CUT THEM OFF, THEN NEVER LET THEM DIG IN · THROW THE TANKS INTO THE CHAOS · HIT THE SPEARHEAD WHEN IT RUNS DRY]
In the fighting around Celles, the Second Armored Division destroyed or captured eighty-two German tanks, eighty-three guns and over four hundred vehicles, and took more than twelve hundred prisoners. Its own losses were seventeen killed and around two hundred wounded. Cut them off, then never let them dig in. Throw the tanks into the chaos. Hit the spearhead when it runs dry.

## ENDING (~15:20 – ~16:30)

[ARCHIVE: VII Corps / 2nd Armored in the snow, knocked-out Panthers at Celles (Signal Corps PD); Collins in the field; date card "1945 / THE ELBE"]
Collins led Seventh Corps all the way to Germany and on to the Elbe River. After the war, he rose to become Chief of Staff of the United States Army.

Patton became the legend of the summer of nineteen forty-four. But it was Collins who took the port that supplied the armies, Collins who broke the German line in Normandy, and Collins who stopped the tip of Hitler's last offensive. He cut his enemies off and never let them dig in. He threw his tanks into the chaos. And he hit the spearhead when it ran dry.
