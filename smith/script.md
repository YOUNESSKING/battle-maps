## HOOK

[MAP: hook-1 | Northeast Korea in winter: Chosin Reservoir, single road (MSR) down to Hungnam; blue Marine units strung along the road; red Chinese arrows pour out of the mountains on all sides and close the ring; snow particles; caption -30°F]
On the night of November twenty-seventh, nineteen fifty, in the frozen mountains of North Korea, the temperature fell to thirty degrees below zero. Out of the darkness, bugles and whistles sounded from every ridge. Some one hundred and twenty thousand Chinese soldiers, an entire army group that American intelligence insisted was not there, came pouring down the slopes.

[MAP: hook-2 | Zoom on the reservoir: blue pockets at Yudam-ni, Hagaru-ri, Koto-ri, cut off from each other; red blocks on the road between them; "ENCIRCLED" stamp]
In their path stood about fifteen thousand men of the First Marine Division, spread along a single narrow mountain road, seventy-eight miles from the sea. Within hours, that road was cut in a dozen places. The division was surrounded, outnumbered many times over, and freezing. In Tokyo and Washington, commanders prepared themselves for the destruction of the most famous division in the United States Marine Corps.

[MAP: hook-3 | Full-length photo cut-out of O.P. Smith + bio card: OLIVER P. SMITH, Major General, USMC, born 1893, commander 1st Marine Division, "The Professor"]
They did not know the man in command. Major General Oliver Prince Smith was fifty-seven years old, a quiet, pipe-smoking Californian so thoughtful and methodical that other officers called him the Professor. He did not shout. He did not pose for cameras. But for weeks he had been quietly preparing for exactly this moment, even when it meant defying his own commanding general.

[ARCHIVE: Marines marching down the mountain road in snow, December 1950, slow push-in]
Two weeks later, his division came down out of the mountains, not as a fleeing mob, but as a fighting column, carrying its wounded, its guns, and its dead. And the Chinese army that had surrounded it would be out of the war for months. So how did one soft-spoken general turn a death trap into one of the greatest fighting withdrawals in history? Let's take a closer look at O.P. Smith's three greatest tactical moves.

## MOVE 1 — INCHON: OVER THE SEAWALL

[MAP: inchon-1 | Korean peninsula, August 1950: North Korean red arrows have pushed UN forces into the Pusan Perimeter in the southeast corner; title card MOVE 1 · INCHON · SEPTEMBER 1950]
To understand what happened at Chosin, we have to go back three months. In the summer of nineteen fifty, North Korea invaded the South. In just a few weeks, the North Korean army overran almost the entire country, and pinned the Americans and South Koreans into a small corner around the port of Pusan.

[MAP: inchon-2 | Big sweeping blue sea arrow from Japan around the peninsula to Inchon on the west coast, behind the North Korean lines; label SEOUL near Inchon; red supply lines running south through Seoul]
General Douglas MacArthur had a plan to break the deadlock. Instead of fighting his way north, he would land an army far behind the enemy lines, at the port of Inchon, right next to the capital, Seoul. Every North Korean supply line to the south ran through that city. Cut it, and the entire invading army would be trapped.

[ARCHIVE: MacArthur and staff aboard USS Mount McKinley, September 1950]
There was just one problem. Almost every naval officer who studied the plan thought Inchon was the worst possible place in Korea to land an army. And the man who would have to lead the assault was Oliver Smith, who had arrived in Japan only days before, with a division that barely existed yet.

[MAP: inchon-3 | Close-up of Inchon harbor: narrow Flying Fish Channel, wide mudflats shading in and out with a tide gauge graphic (32 FT), island of Wolmi-do connected by causeway, seawalls highlighted along the city front]
The approach was a single narrow channel, easy to mine. The tides were among the most extreme in the world, rising and falling by more than thirty feet. At low tide, the harbor turned into miles of mud where any landing craft would be stranded. There were only a few hours each day when boats could reach the shore at all. And the city itself was protected by stone seawalls, in places taller than a man standing on another man's shoulders.

[MAP: inchon-4 | North Korean side: a small red garrison in Inchon (~2,000), most red units far south at Pusan; thought bubble: "NO ONE WILL LAND HERE"]
The North Koreans looked at the same geography and drew the obvious conclusion. No sane commander would try to land there. They left only a couple of thousand troops to guard the city, and kept their main army far to the south, fighting around Pusan.

[MAP: inchon-5 | Smith portrait stake appears; counters assemble: 1st Marines from the US, 5th Marines pulled from Pusan, ships; caption 3 WEEKS TO PLAN]
Smith had barely three weeks to plan the landing. One of his regiments was still crossing the Pacific. Another had to be pulled out of the fighting at Pusan. But working with the Navy, he saw a way to turn the tides from an obstacle into a timetable. If the tide allowed only two landings a day, then the attack would come in two blows, one at dawn, one at dusk.

[MAP: inchon-6 | Dawn, September 15: blue assault arrow onto Wolmi-do (GREEN BEACH); red garrison on the island turns grey; clock 06:33; tide gauge falls, harbor turns to mud around the island]
At six thirty on the morning of September fifteenth, a single battalion of the Fifth Marines stormed the fortified island of Wolmi-do, which guarded the harbor. The Navy and Marine aircraft had pounded it for days. Within two hours the island was taken, without a single Marine killed. Then the tide went out, and that battalion sat alone on its island, surrounded by mud, for the rest of the day.

[MAP: inchon-7 | Evening: two blue arrows hit RED BEACH (north of Wolmi-do causeway) and BLUE BEACH (south of the city) at the same moment; clock 17:30; red units in the city scatter]
The North Koreans now knew exactly where the Americans were coming. But they could do nothing about it, because the same tide that trapped the Marines also kept out any reinforcements. At five thirty in the afternoon, the water came back in, and the landing craft hit two beaches at once, one on each side of the city.

[ARCHIVE: famous photo of 1st Lt. Baldomero Lopez scaling the Red Beach seawall with a ladder]
At Red Beach, there was no beach at all. There was only the seawall. The Marines climbed out of their boats and went straight up it on wooden ladders, into the fire of North Korean trenches. One of them was First Lieutenant Baldomero Lopez, photographed as he went over the wall. Minutes later, he smothered an enemy grenade with his own body to save his men. He was awarded the Medal of Honor.

[MAP: inchon-8 | Night: blue lines push inland through the city; by midnight a blue beachhead line; next panel: blue arrows toward Seoul, red supply lines south cut with X marks, red units at Pusan collapse north]
By midnight, the beachhead was secure. The cost on the first day was around twenty Marines killed and under two hundred wounded, for a landing that experts had said could not be done. Two weeks later, Seoul was back in United Nations hands. With its supply lines cut, the North Korean army in the south collapsed. Within a month, the war seemed all but won.

[MAP: inchon-9 | Method card: 1. BUILD THE LIFELINE FIRST · 2. REFUSE TO BE RUSHED · 3. KEEP THE DIVISION WHOLE, with line 3 highlighted]
MacArthur had the vision. But it was Smith's planning that got the Marines over the seawall. And already, you can see the shape of his method. Build the lifeline first. Refuse to be rushed. And keep the division whole. At Inchon, he had used the tide itself as a schedule. In the mountains of the north, he was about to face a far deadlier enemy, and this time, his own commander would be pushing him into the trap.

## MOVE 2 — HAGARU-RI: THE SLOW ADVANCE

[MAP: hagaru-1 | Korean peninsula, October–November 1950: UN arrows racing north toward the Yalu; X Corps landing on the east coast at Wonsan/Hungnam; title card MOVE 2 · HAGARU-RI · NOVEMBER 1950]
After Inchon, the United Nations armies raced north toward the Chinese border at the Yalu River. Smith's division was put ashore on the east coast of North Korea and ordered into the mountains, toward a vast frozen lake the Americans knew by its Japanese name: the Chosin Reservoir.

[MAP: hagaru-2 | Zoom: the Main Supply Route from Hungnam up through Funchilin Pass, Koto-ri, Hagaru-ri, to Yudam-ni; road drawn as a thin, twisting line; mountains in dark relief; caption ONE ROAD · 78 MILES]
Look at the ground. From the port of Hungnam, a single dirt road climbed into the mountains. It wound up a narrow pass, through the villages of Koto-ri and Hagaru-ri, and on to the west of the reservoir. There was no other way in, and no other way out. On both sides rose steep, snow-covered ridges, perfect places for an enemy to hide.

[ARCHIVE: portrait photo of Major General Edward M. Almond]
Smith's commanding officer was Major General Edward Almond, commander of Ten Corps, and one of MacArthur's favorites. Almond wanted speed. The war was almost over, and he wanted to reach the border before Christmas. He ordered the Marines to push forward as fast as possible, spread out along the road if necessary.

[MAP: hagaru-3 | Red Chinese units appear hidden in the mountains (faded, then solid) around the reservoir; label 9TH ARMY GROUP · GEN. SONG SHILUN; thought bubble from Almond's side: "THE CHINESE WON'T COME"]
But there were already Chinese soldiers in those mountains. They moved only at night, hid in villages and forests by day, and left almost no trace. The Chinese commander, General Song Shilun, was counting on exactly what Almond was demanding. He expected the Americans to race forward, stretch themselves thin along that single road, and break into small groups that could be surrounded and destroyed one by one.

[MAP: hagaru-4 | Smith portrait stake at Hagaru-ri; blue counters inch forward along the road in small steps, each step leaving a supply dump icon (Koto-ri, Hagaru-ri); caption ~1 MILE A DAY]
But Smith saw something different. He had already fought the Chinese in small clashes earlier that month, and he did not believe they had gone away. So he simply did not hurry. He advanced slowly, sometimes barely a mile a day, keeping his regiments close enough to support each other. At every stop, he stockpiled ammunition, food and fuel. He even wrote privately to the Commandant of the Marine Corps, warning that his division was being strung out along a road in the mountains in the middle of winter.

[MAP: hagaru-5 | Close-up of Hagaru-ri at the south tip of the reservoir: perimeter drawn; a rectangle outlined in white south of the village labeled AIRSTRIP · 3,000+ FT; bulldozer icons, floodlight glow]
And at Hagaru-ri, at the southern tip of the reservoir, he did something no one had asked him to do. He ordered his engineers to build an airfield, long enough for transport planes to land. The ground was frozen so hard that it broke the blades of their bulldozers, and the engineers had to blast it loose with explosives. They worked day and night under floodlights, while Almond pressed Smith again and again to stop digging and keep attacking.

[MAP: hagaru-6 | Night of Nov 27-28: red arrows hit Yudam-ni and then roads everywhere; the road between the blue pockets turns red in several places; Hagaru-ri perimeter lit up]
Then, on the night of November twenty-seventh, the trap was sprung. Chinese divisions struck the Marines at Yudam-ni, west of the reservoir, cut the road behind them, and struck the Army troops east of the reservoir. The next day, Almond flew in to the Army units and told them not to let a bunch of Chinese laundrymen stop them. Within days, those Army battalions would be almost wiped out.

[MAP: hagaru-7 | Hagaru-ri close-up: thin blue perimeter manned by cooks, clerks, engineers; red waves from the south and east; East Hill contested; supply dumps glowing inside the perimeter]
The Chinese threw an entire division at Hagaru-ri. It was defended by barely one infantry battalion, reinforced by engineers, truck drivers, cooks and clerks, fighting in the snow at thirty below. But because Smith had moved so slowly, Hagaru-ri was full of ammunition. The defenders fought through the night, lost East Hill, fought back, and held.

[MAP: hagaru-8 | The airstrip: a blue plane icon lands (DEC 1), then a loop of planes flying out south; counter: 4,300+ WOUNDED EVACUATED; ammunition para-drops fall into the perimeter]
On December first, with the airstrip less than half finished, the first transport plane landed on it. Over the next week, more than four thousand wounded and frostbitten men were flown out of the trap, men who would otherwise have had to be carried out on trucks, through the ambushes, in the cold. Planes flew in ammunition and replacements. The airfield Almond had called a waste of time had become the lifeline of the entire division.

[ARCHIVE: C-47 transport on the Hagaru-ri airstrip, wounded Marines being loaded, December 1950]
The Chinese plan had depended on catching the Marines strung out and starved. Instead, they found a division gathered around a fortress, with supplies, an airfield and a plan. The regiments at Yudam-ni were ordered to fight their way back to Hagaru-ri, and for four days and nights, they did exactly that, down fourteen miles of road, with Chinese troops on every ridge.

[MAP: hagaru-9 | Method card: 1. BUILD THE LIFELINE FIRST (highlighted) · 2. REFUSE TO BE RUSHED (highlighted) · 3. KEEP THE DIVISION WHOLE]
Build the lifeline first. Refuse to be rushed. Keep the division whole. At Hagaru-ri, the first two rules had saved his men. The Chinese had failed to break the Marines apart. But they still held the mountains, and the road to the sea was seventy miles long. ## MOVE 3 — THE BREAKOUT: ATTACKING IN ANOTHER DIRECTION

[MAP: breakout-1 | The whole road from Hagaru-ri to Hungnam: blue column massed at Hagaru-ri; red Chinese divisions on every ridge along the road; title card MOVE 3 · THE BREAKOUT · DECEMBER 1950]
By December fourth, the whole of Smith's force was gathered at Hagaru-ri. Around it, several Chinese divisions still held the mountains, and every mile of the road south ran past ridges where they were waiting. Many officers expected the Marines to be airlifted out, abandoning their heavy weapons and vehicles to the enemy.

[MAP: breakout-2 | Red side: Chinese units dig in along the road, arrows point at the Marines' vehicles and guns; thought bubble: "THEY MUST ABANDON EVERYTHING"]
The Chinese believed they had the Marines exactly where they wanted them. To get their trucks, tanks and artillery out, the Americans would have to use the road. And the Chinese could cut that road wherever they liked. Sooner or later, the Marines would have to leave their guns and their wounded behind, and a division without its guns was just a crowd of freezing men.

[ARCHIVE: O.P. Smith at Hagaru-ri, December 1950, with correspondents]
Smith saw something different. He refused to fly his fighting men out, and he refused to abandon his equipment. When war correspondents asked him about the retreat, he answered calmly. Gentlemen, we are not retreating. We are merely attacking in another direction. The newspapers turned that into a much more colorful line: Retreat, hell! But the calm version was the true one, and it described exactly what he meant to do.

[MAP: breakout-3 | Blue column leaves Hagaru-ri southward; blue arrows attack uphill onto the ridges on both sides as the column advances; Corsair icons strike red positions; caption DEC 6-7 · HAGARU-RI → KOTO-RI]
On December sixth, the column set out for Koto-ri, eleven miles to the south. It did not simply drive down the road. Infantry climbed the ridges on either side and cleared them as the vehicles moved below, while Marine and Navy fighter-bombers struck anything that moved. The column fought for every mile, but it kept moving, and it reached Koto-ri the next day.

[MAP: breakout-4 | Close-up of Funchilin Pass south of Koto-ri: the road hugs a cliff; a small bridge over the pipes of a power station flashes and breaks; gap label ~29 FT; red units on the heights above]
But just south of Koto-ri lay the most dangerous point on the whole road: the Funchilin Pass. There, the road ran along a cliff face, and crossed a narrow bridge over the pipes of a hydroelectric plant. The Chinese had blown that bridge, leaving a gap of nearly thirty feet above a sheer drop. There was no way around it. For trucks, tanks and guns, the road to the sea was simply gone.

[MAP: breakout-5 | Blue air arrows from Japan to Koto-ri; eight bridge-section icons drop by parachute into the Koto-ri perimeter; caption 8 TREADWAY SPANS · C-119 FLYING BOXCARS · DEC 7]
So Smith's engineers asked the Air Force for something that had never been tried before: to drop a bridge from the sky. On December seventh, big C-119 cargo planes flew over Koto-ri and pushed out eight steel bridge sections, each weighing about two tons, under giant parachutes. One fell into Chinese hands, and another was damaged. But there were enough.

[MAP: breakout-6 | Blue infantry arrows seize the heights above the pass (Hill 1081) in a snowstorm; engineer icons carry the spans down; the gap closes with a new bridge icon; caption DEC 9]
While Marine infantry stormed the heights above the pass in a blizzard, the engineers hauled the sections down the road and built the new bridge over the gap. It was finished on the afternoon of December ninth. It was so narrow that tanks and trucks had only inches to spare on either side, and the crossing went on through the night.

[ARCHIVE: the Treadway bridge at Funchilin Pass, vehicles crossing, December 1950]
The column wound down the mountain behind it: trucks, tanks, artillery, and the wounded. And the dead. The Marines had refused to leave them in the mountains, and many were brought out strapped to the hoods and fenders of the vehicles. It was so cold that morphine froze in its syrettes, and corpsmen carried them in their mouths to keep them liquid.

[MAP: breakout-7 | Blue column reaches Hungnam on the coast (DEC 11); ships offshore; red units left behind in the mountains fade to grey; caption 9TH ARMY GROUP · OUT OF ACTION UNTIL SPRING]
On December eleventh, the last units reached the coast at Hungnam. Behind them, in the mountains, the Chinese Ninth Army Group had been wrecked. By most estimates it had lost tens of thousands of men, killed, wounded and frozen, and it would not be able to fight again for months. The Marines had lost around a thousand dead, and thousands more wounded and frostbitten. But they had come out as a division.

[ARCHIVE: the Hungnam evacuation, ships in the harbor, the port demolition explosion of December 24, 1950]
From Hungnam, the Navy took them out by sea, together with the rest of Ten Corps and nearly a hundred thousand Korean civilians who had fled south with them. On Christmas Eve, the last ships sailed, and engineers blew up the port behind them.

[MAP: breakout-8 | Method card: all three lines highlighted: 1. BUILD THE LIFELINE FIRST · 2. REFUSE TO BE RUSHED · 3. KEEP THE DIVISION WHOLE]
Build the lifeline first. Refuse to be rushed. Keep the division whole. In the breakout, Smith put the third rule into action. By holding his column together, and bringing everything with it, he turned a retreat into an attack, and an encirclement into a disaster for the army that had set it.

## ENDING

[MAP: ending-1 | Korean peninsula zoomed out: the Chosin route glowing; arrow from Hungnam by sea to Pusan; then the front in early 1951 with Ridgway's line; small portrait stake of Ridgway]
Smith's division was soon back in the fight. Only weeks later, a new commander named Matthew Ridgway took over the battered United Nations army and began to turn the war around. He did it with divisions that had survived the winter, and the First Marine Division was among the best of them.

[ARCHIVE: portrait of O.P. Smith, later years, slow push-in]
Oliver Smith never became a household name. He was promoted to general, retired in nineteen fifty-five, and died on Christmas Day, nineteen seventy-seven, at his home in California. To the men who had walked out of the mountains with him, he was something rarer than a famous general. He was the general who had brought them home.
