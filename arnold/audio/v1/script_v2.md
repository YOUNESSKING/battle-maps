By seventeen seventy-seven, the Revolution was going badly. The British held New York and Philadelphia, the American capital, and a second British army was coming down from Canada, toward a village called Saratoga.

But on the afternoon of the seventh of October, it did not look like a victory. About fifteen hundred British and German troops had marched out of their camp, with cannon, into the fields in front of the American left. The general who had fought them hardest three weeks before had quarrelled with his commander, Horatio Gates, and been left without a command. When the guns opened, he rode out to the fighting anyway. His name was Benedict Arnold. Three years later he would try to sell West Point to the British, and his name would become another word for traitor. But before that, he had marched an army through the wilderness to Quebec. He had built a fleet out of a forest and taken on the Royal Navy on Lake Champlain. And at Saratoga, he broke a British army. These are Benedict Arnold's three greatest tactical moves.

Two and a half years earlier, the war was only weeks old. Arnold was a Connecticut merchant and sea captain who had traded by ship as far as Quebec, and in May of seventeen seventy-five he helped capture Fort Ticonderoga on Lake Champlain. That summer, outside Boston, George Washington planned an invasion of Canada from two directions, and he chose Arnold to lead one of them.

## MOVE 1 — THE MARCH TO QUEBEC (~1:30 – ~6:00)

[MAP: m1-1 | `northeast` wide, option 1 (territory, half strength): title card MOVE 1 · THE BACK DOOR · 1775; date scroll SEP 1775; one blue arrow north up Lake Champlain toward Montreal (Montgomery, badge after his name later), a second dotted blue line from the Maine coast up the Kennebec, over the mountains, down the Chaudière to QUEBEC (label on the word); then dive into `maine` with the colours dissolving]
One American army would go north up Lake Champlain toward Montreal. Arnold's would go up the Kennebec River in Maine, over the mountains, and down the Chaudière River to Quebec, the fortress city that held Canada. He set out in late September with about eleven hundred men, in some two hundred flat-bottomed boats called bateaux. The plan said about a hundred and eighty miles, in about twenty days.

[MAP: m1-2 | `maine` route map: the boats push upriver against rapids (small blue boat counters, white water marks at the falls); far to the west, beyond the frame edge, a red force faces the lake route; card on "back door": THE BACK DOOR (stamp, ~3 s)]
The British in Canada were watching the lake, where the first American army was coming. Nobody expected an army to come out of the Maine woods. That was the idea: come in through the back door, while the front door held everyone's attention.

[MAP: m1-3 | `maine` closer on the Great Carrying Place and the Dead River: men drag boats and barrels between ponds (dotted carry line, 12 MILES label on the words); night storm on "storm": rain, wind, trees falling marks, the river line swells wide (water overlay grows), boat counters flicker and drop out; date scroll 21 OCT]
The back door nearly killed them. The boats leaked, and the food inside them spoiled. At the Great Carrying Place the men had to haul boats and barrels across about twelve miles of ponds and bog. Then, on the night of the twenty-first of October, a storm hit the Dead River. Trees came down, and by morning the river had risen eight to ten feet. Boats sank, supplies washed away, and men lost their way in the flooded woods.

[MAP: m1-4 | `maine`: part of the column turns back south (three counters peel off, grey, on "turned back"); a small blue party runs fast down the Chaudière (Arnold's badge on the river) to the first farms (label SARTIGAN), cattle icons move back up the trail; Dearborn badge as initials; hunger card lines one at a time: DOG · SHAVING SOAP · LIP SALVE · SHOE LEATHER]
A few days later, three companies at the rear held a council and turned back for home, about four hundred and fifty men, and they took more than their share of the food. The rest kept going. Arnold pushed ahead with a small party, down the rapids of the Chaudière to the first French farms, and sent back cattle and flour. Behind him, one of his captains, Henry Dearborn, wrote that the men of another company had killed his dog, and eaten it. The army's surgeon wrote that men ate shaving soap, lip salve, the leather of their shoes, and their cartridge boxes.

[MAP: m1-5 | `quebec` wide (St. Lawrence, Point Lévis, the walled city on its cliff): date scroll 9 NOV; blue counters emerge on the south bank (number card ~600 on the words); a stat line 180 MILES PLANNED → ~350 MILES; night: two red warship counters on the river, canoes slip across between them to Wolfe's Cove and up onto the Plains of Abraham (label on the words)]
On the ninth of November, about six hundred men came out of the forest on the bank of the St. Lawrence, across from Quebec. They had come about three hundred and fifty miles, nearly twice the distance on the plan. Four nights later, in some thirty-five canoes and dugouts, they slipped across the river between two British warships and climbed onto the Plains of Abraham. But six hundred worn-out men could not storm the walls of Quebec. Arnold had to wait for the other army.

[MAP: m1-6 | `quebec`: date scroll DEC 1775; Montgomery's column arrives from upriver (badge on his name), ~300 label; a clock/calendar card on "end of the year"; snow begins on "blizzard"; on "Liberty or death" a paper slip card with the words in handwriting]
In early December, General Richard Montgomery arrived from Montreal with about three hundred men. Now they had a deadline. The enlistments of many of the New England men ran out at the end of the year. So they waited for a storm to hide them, and on the last night of seventeen seventy-five, in a blizzard, they attacked. Every man wore a piece of paper in his cap, with the words "Liberty or death."

[MAP: m1-7 | `quebec` close on the Lower Town, night + snow, option 2 (two glowing fronts only): Montgomery's column along the river under Cape Diamond from the south, a blockhouse cannon flash (visible) on "grapeshot", his badge greys out; Arnold's column along the waterfront from the north to the barricade at SAULT-AU-MATELOT (label on the word), a hit on "leg", Arnold's badge pulls back; Morgan's badge (initials or photo) on his name, blue over the first barricade, then stalls; a red column comes out of a gate behind them; ring closes; casualty card at the end with the spoken numbers]
The plan was to hit the Lower Town, the strip of streets below the cliffs, from both sides. Montgomery came along the river from the south, under the cliff of Cape Diamond. At the first barrier, a cannon loaded with grapeshot fired, and Montgomery was killed. His column fell back. On the north side, Arnold led his column along the waterfront to a barricade on a street called Sault-au-Matelot. A ball hit him in the leg, and he had to be helped to the rear. His rifle captain, Daniel Morgan, took over and got over the barricade. But the attack stalled in the narrow streets, the powder was wet, and British troops came out of a gate behind them. About four hundred Americans were captured, and somewhere between thirty and sixty were killed. The defenders lost fewer than twenty men.

[MAP: m1-8 | `quebec` wide, grey dawn: the blue blockade line around the city through the winter (date scroll runs to MAY 1776); a pistol card only on "pistols"; result card QUEBEC HELD · THE MARCH: ~350 MILES; method card METHOD · GO WHERE HE SAYS NO ARMY CAN GO on the spoken words]
In the hospital, Arnold had his pistols loaded and a sword laid on his bed, and told the surgeon he would kill as many as he could if the British came into the room. They never came. With a few hundred men, he kept Quebec under siege for the rest of the winter. The city had not fallen. But Arnold had already shown how he fought. Go where the enemy says no army can go. Let the ground and the weather fight for you. And lead from the front, at the point where it is decided.

[MAP: move1-sub | `champlain` wide: the Americans pull back south out of Canada (blue arrows down the lake, date scroll 1776); E.subscribe button bottom-right with cursor click on the subscribing clause; on the last sentence red ship counters gather at the north end of the lake; NEXT: MOVE 2 label]
Quebec was only the beginning. By the next summer the Americans had been driven out of Canada, and the British were building a fleet to come down Lake Champlain. Arnold had no navy at all. So how do you stop a fleet, when all you have is a forest? That's the kind of question we answer here, and subscribing is how you catch the next one. Here's how Arnold did it.

## MOVE 2 — VALCOUR ISLAND (~6:00 – ~11:00)

[MAP: m2-1 | `champlain` wide, option 1 (half strength): title card MOVE 2 · THE FLEET FROM A FOREST · 1776; at the north end (ST. JOHNS label) red ship counters multiply; one big red ship counter INFLEXIBLE · 18 GUNS assembles piece by piece on "putting her back together"; card 28 DAYS on the spoken words; then a red arrow runs south down the lake to TICONDEROGA and on toward the Hudson; New England glows on "cut New England off"]
In the summer of seventeen seventy-six, at the north end of Lake Champlain, the British were building warships. They even took one ship apart, carried her past the rapids in pieces, and put her back together. She was the Inflexible, with eighteen guns, and she sailed twenty-eight days after her keel was laid. The lake was the road south to Fort Ticonderoga and the Hudson. Whoever held it could cut New England off from the rest of the colonies.

[MAP: m2-2 | `champlain`: at the south end (SKENESBOROUGH label) blue boat counters appear one by one (gondolas, galleys); numbers card 15 VESSELS · ~700 MEN (blue) on the words; then red card ~25 ARMED VESSELS · ~700 SEAMEN; red troop-boat counters behind them]
At the south end of the lake, at Skenesborough, Arnold's carpenters built boats out of fresh-cut trees: flat-bottomed gondolas, and galleys driven by oars. By October he had fifteen vessels and about seven hundred men, most of them soldiers who had never fired a ship's cannon. Coming against them were about twenty-five armed British vessels with about seven hundred seamen, and behind them more than a thousand soldiers in boats, ready to board.

[MAP: m2-3 | dive into `valcour` (colours dissolve ~2.8 s): Valcour Island close to the western shore, the narrow channel behind it; the blue boats anchor in a curved line across the channel (crescent), hidden from the main lake; a dashed sight-line from the open lake that cannot see them]
The British expected to find the Americans out on the open lake, where their bigger ships could sail around them. Arnold had picked something else. Halfway down the lake, the island of Valcour lies close to the western shore, with a narrow channel behind it. He anchored his fleet across that channel in a curved line, out of sight of anyone sailing down the main lake. Weeks earlier, he had written to Gates that if the enemy came up the lake, they would not be able to take advantage of his position.

[MAP: m2-4 | `valcour`, option 2: date scroll 11 OCT 1776; wind arrow from the north-east; the red fleet sails south past the island on the open lake, then turns and has to beat back up toward the channel (zig-zag tacks); only the schooner CARLETON and the red gunboats come close; the big red ships stay far out; the blue schooner ROYAL SAVAGE runs onto the island's point (grey, fire later); gunfire between the lines (visible, large)]
On the morning of the eleventh of October, the British fleet came down the lake with a strong wind from the north-east behind it, and sailed right past the island before it saw him. To attack, the British had to turn and work their way back up against the wind. Only the schooner Carleton and the gunboats could get close. Their biggest ships could hardly get into the fight. The American schooner Royal Savage ran aground on the point of the island and was lost. From about noon until dusk, the two lines fought at close range.

[MAP: m2-5 | `valcour` close: Arnold's badge on the galley CONGRESS, gun flashes from it on "aimed"; quote card "I was obliged myself to Point Most of the Guns" (verbatim, small); the red CARLETON hit, swings, towed out by two gunboats on "towed"; dusk: the big red INFLEXIBLE comes up and fires five broadsides (five visible flashes); the blue gondola PHILADELPHIA sinks after the firing stops]
Arnold had too few gunners, so he aimed most of the guns on his galley, the Congress, himself. The Carleton was hit so hard that a young officer named Edward Pellew had to climb out on her bowsprit under fire to swing her round, and she was towed out of the fight. At dusk the Inflexible finally came up and fired five broadsides. The gondola Philadelphia was so full of holes that she sank about an hour after the guns stopped.

[MAP: m2-6 | `valcour` NIGHT mode: the red ships anchor in a line across the south end of the channel; blue boats trapped; card ~60 KILLED AND WOUNDED · 3/4 OF THE AMMUNITION GONE on the words; then E.decide card on "put yourself in Arnold's place": A FIGHT AT DAWN / B SLIP PAST IN THE DARK, TELL US IN THE COMMENTS with 3 ticks; reveal B on m2-7 start]
That night the British anchored in a line across the south end of the channel, to finish him at dawn. Arnold had lost about sixty men killed and wounded, and nearly three quarters of his ammunition was gone. Now stop for a moment, and put yourself in Arnold's place. You are trapped in a narrow channel, and a stronger fleet is waiting for daylight. Do you fight it out at dawn, or do you try to slip past the enemy in the dark? Tell us in the comments what you would have done. And then listen to what Arnold did.

[MAP: m2-7 | `valcour` night: decision reveal (B lights blue); the blue boats move in single file close along the western shore past the end of the red line (TRUMBULL first, CONGRESS and WASHINGTON last, labels on the words); no fire; then dawn: the channel empty, a red stamp EMPTY only if spoken (it is not: use no stamp, just the empty channel)]
At seven o'clock that evening, the galley Trumbull led off, the gondolas and small boats followed, and the Congress and the Washington brought up the rear. One behind another, in the dark, they crept along the shore past the end of the British line. His second in command, David Waterbury, wrote that they went through without being discovered. In the morning, the British found the channel empty.

[MAP: m2-8 | `champsouth` (Valcour to Crown Point): the chase south over two days (date scroll 12 OCT, 13 OCT); red ships close in; the WASHINGTON greys out on "surrendered"; Arnold's CONGRESS + four gondolas run into a bay on the Vermont shore and burn (fire + smoke columns, flags still on the hulls); a dotted blue line of men walks through the woods to CROWN POINT; a red ambush marker appears on the road behind them]
The British chased him south for two days. The battered boats could not outrun them. Around noon on the thirteenth, the Washington was caught and surrendered. Arnold fought on in the Congress with four gondolas for more than two hours, then ran them ashore in a small bay on the Vermont side and set them on fire, with their flags still flying. He and about two hundred men walked through the woods to Crown Point, and got past an ambush that was laid on the road just after they went by.

[MAP: m2-9 | `champlain` wide: casualty card (both sides spoken): US ~80 killed and wounded, ~110 captured, 10-11 of 15 vessels / British fewer than 40; then the red army at Crown Point, looks at TICONDEROGA ~9,000 (card), date scroll 2 NOV 1776, snow; red arrows turn back north to Canada; quote card for Mahan on the spoken words; method card METHOD · LET THE GROUND AND THE WEATHER FIGHT FOR YOU]
In two days he had lost ten or eleven of his fifteen vessels, about eighty men killed and wounded, and more than a hundred captured. The British lost fewer than forty. As a sea battle, it was a defeat. But the British took Crown Point, looked at Ticonderoga and its nine thousand defenders, and decided it was too late in the year. On the second of November, with the first snow, they started back to Canada. More than a century later, the naval historian Alfred Thayer Mahan wrote that Saratoga was won because of "the invaluable year of delay secured to them in seventeen seventy-six by their little navy on Lake Champlain." Arnold had chosen the place, and let the wind fight on his side.

## BRIDGE (~11:00 – ~11:25)

[ARCHIVE: ~22 s: headline card "FEBRUARY 1777 / PHILADELPHIA"; Burgoyne portrait (3D depth, label LT. GEN. JOHN BURGOYNE); Ticonderoga view again with date card "JULY 1777"; projector frame; 2.5-3 s per shot]
The next year began badly for Arnold. In February of seventeen seventy-seven, Congress promoted five generals who were junior to him over his head. Then, in the summer, a new British army under General John Burgoyne came down the lake, took Fort Ticonderoga, and marched for Albany. Arnold went north to fight it anyway.

## MOVE 3 — SARATOGA (~11:25 – ~16:10)

[MAP: m3-1 | `hudson` wide, option 1 (half strength): title card MOVE 3 · THE OPEN BACK DOOR · 1777; date scroll SEP 1777; red army moving down the Hudson toward ALBANY (label); blue line dug in on BEMIS HEIGHTS (label on the words) across the river road; dive into `saratoga` (colours dissolve); red columns swing west through the woods; date scroll 19 SEP; clearing label FREEMAN'S FARM on the words]
By September, Burgoyne's army was on the Hudson, about twenty-five miles above Albany. Gates's army was dug in on Bemis Heights, a line of bluffs above the river road. So Burgoyne went around, through the woods to the west. On the nineteenth, at a clearing called Freeman's Farm, the two armies met.

[MAP: m3-2 | `saratoga` close, option 2: Arnold's badge on the American left; blue waves go into the woods one after another (Morgan's riflemen first, badge on his name); red line in the clearing; at dusk a red German column arrives from the river side; casualty card NEARLY 600 vs 320 on the words]
Arnold commanded the American left. He pushed Gates to fight in the woods, instead of waiting behind the earthworks, and he sent in Daniel Morgan's riflemen, and then regiment after regiment, in waves, all afternoon. At dusk German reinforcements arrived, and the British kept the clearing. But they had lost nearly six hundred men, against three hundred and twenty Americans, and they were stuck.

[MAP: m3-3 | `saratoga` quiet camp view: Gates badge and Arnold badge face each other inside the American lines; Morgan's counter slides from Arnold's side to Gates's HQ on "took Morgan's riflemen"; quote card (verbatim, small) on the words "did not know"; Arnold's command lines fade out on "no command at all"]
Then the American generals turned on each other. Gates took Morgan's riflemen out of Arnold's command. The two men had a shouting match, and Arnold wrote to Gates that he had been told Gates "did not know I was a Major-general or had any command in the army." Arnold asked for a pass to leave the army. He got it, and then he stayed. When the next battle came, he had no command at all.

[MAP: m3-4 | `saratoga`: date scroll 7 OCT; red columns with guns move out into the wheat field (label BARBER'S WHEATFIELD small); blue attacks hit both ends; Morgan's riflemen on the British right; Fraser badge on his name, hit + greys on "mortally wounded"; the red line falls back into two redoubts (labels on "redoubts")]
On the seventh of October, Burgoyne tried again. About fifteen hundred men, with cannon, moved out into a wheat field to probe the American left. The Americans hit both ends of the line. On the British right, Morgan's riflemen came in, and in that fight the British general Simon Fraser, trying to hold the line together, was shot and mortally wounded. The British fell back to two big log forts, their redoubts.

[MAP: m3-5 | `saratoga`: Arnold's badge appears on the field; a split card, small: ASKED FOR MEN? / RODE OUT? (on "accounts differ"); blue regiments gather behind his badge and hit the first redoubt (label LIGHT INFANTRY REDOUBT), gun flashes from the redoubt, blue recoils on "It held"]
Arnold was in the middle of it. Accounts differ on whether Gates let him go, or whether he simply went. Either way, he took charge of whatever regiments he could reach, and threw them at the first redoubt, held by British light infantry. It held. So he looked for a weak point.

[MAP: m3-6 | `saratoga` close, option 2: the gap between the redoubts with two cabin icons and a thin red line (CANADIANS, small); the second redoubt BREYMANN (label on the name), its back drawn open; Arnold's badge gallops across the open field between the two firing lines (dotted path, musket flashes either side); blue clears the cabins; Arnold with a small group swings round the open side of the German redoubt]
Between the two redoubts was a gap, held by Canadian troops around two log cabins. Beyond it stood the second redoubt, held by German troops under Colonel Breymann, and it was open at the back. By several accounts, Arnold galloped straight across the field between the two firing lines to get there. The Americans cleared the cabins, and Arnold went in with a small group around the open side of the German redoubt.

[MAP: m3-7 | `saratoga` close: musket flash, Arnold's badge falls (hit + shake on "broke his leg"), horse icon down; the redoubt turns blue; Breymann's badge (initials) greys on "dead"; quote card for Gates's report (verbatim) on the spoken words; the British camp's flank glows open]
A ball broke his leg, the same leg that had been hit at Quebec, and his horse was killed and fell on him. But the redoubt was taken, and Breymann was dead. Gates himself reported on "the Gallant General Arnold, whose Leg was fractured by a musket ball, as he forced the Enemy's Breast Work." With the German redoubt gone, the whole British camp lay open from the side.

[MAP: m3-8 | `saratoga` → `hudson`: casualty card 7 OCT: BRITISH OVER 630 / AMERICANS ABOUT 150; red army pulls north to SARATOGA at night (date scroll 8 OCT), blue rings close around it; date scroll 17 OCT; result card SURRENDER · ABOUT 6,000; small FRANCE · 1778 card on "France"; method card METHOD · LEAD FROM THE FRONT, AT THE POINT WHERE IT IS DECIDED]
On the seventh of October the British lost more than six hundred and thirty men, the Americans about a hundred and fifty. That night Burgoyne abandoned his camp and retreated north to Saratoga. The Americans closed in around him, and on the seventeenth of October he surrendered, about six thousand men. Within months, France signed an alliance with the United States. Arnold spent the winter in a hospital bed. He had found the one open door in the British line, and gone through it himself.

## ENDING (~16:10 – ~16:50)

[MAP: end-1 | `northeast` wide, the three places light up in turn as they are named (QUEBEC, VALCOUR, SARATOGA); on "a boot" a simple boot-monument card (archive photo if licensed); last part clean]
Look at the three moves together. At Quebec, he took an army where no one believed an army could go. On Lake Champlain, he picked a channel where the wind fought on his side, and bought a whole year. And at Saratoga, with no command at all, he found the one weak point in the British line and went through it himself. Today, on the battlefield at Saratoga, there is a monument to the soldier who was wounded at that redoubt. It shows a boot. It does not carry his name.
