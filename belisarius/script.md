## HOOK

[MAP: hook-rome | Night. City of Rome from above, the Aurelian Walls glowing as a thin ring; the Tiber snaking through; Gothic camps (red) appear one after another around the ring; counter: GOTHS 150,000? (Procopius) — then a small blue counter inside: 5,000]
March, five hundred and thirty-seven A.D. The city of Rome is surrounded. Outside its walls, the king of the Ostrogoths has gathered an army so large that an eyewitness put it at a hundred and fifty thousand men. Modern historians think it was closer to twenty or thirty thousand. Either way, the defenders are hopelessly outnumbered. Inside the city, holding twelve miles of ancient walls, there are about five thousand soldiers.

[MAP: hook-rome-2 | Camera slides along the wall circuit; the aqueduct lines leading into the city are cut one by one with red X marks; caption: AQUEDUCTS CUT]
The Goths cut the great aqueducts that had fed the city for centuries. They build siege towers, battering rams and ladders. By every rule of war, Rome should fall within weeks. Its commander should surrender, or die on the walls.

[ARCHIVE: San Vitale mosaic, Ravenna — Justinian and his court; slow push-in on the bearded figure at the emperor's right, traditionally identified as Belisarius]
That commander was a man named Belisarius. He was about thirty-two years old. He had been born in the Balkans, in a small town on the border of Thrace, and joined the bodyguard of a young prince named Justinian. When Justinian became emperor, Belisarius rose on merit alone, and by his mid-twenties he commanded the armies of the Eastern Roman Empire.

[MAP: hook-empire | Mediterranean at the start of Justinian's reign: Eastern Roman Empire blue; Sassanid Persia to the east, Vandal Kingdom in North Africa, Ostrogothic Kingdom in Italy in red; three pulsing markers: DARA, CARTHAGE, ROME]
The old Western Roman Empire had collapsed half a century earlier. Italy belonged to the Goths, North Africa to the Vandals, and in the east the Persian Empire pressed on the frontier. Justinian dreamed of taking it all back, and he gave that job to Belisarius, almost always with fewer men than his enemies.

[ARCHIVE: Byzantine cataphract / armoured horse archer illustration; coin of Justinian]
Over the next ten years, Belisarius beat the Persians, destroyed the Vandal kingdom in a single campaign, and took back Italy and Rome itself. Later historians would call him the Last of the Romans. But how did a general who was nearly always outnumbered keep winning? Let's take a closer look at Belisarius's three greatest tactical moves.

## MOVE 1 — DARA: THE TRENCH THAT WAS A TRAP

[MAP: dara-1 | Title card MOVE 1 · DARA · SUMMER 530 AD. Region map, upper Mesopotamia: Roman frontier (blue) and Persia (red); fortress city of DARA on the border, NISIBIS to the east; red army arrow marches from Nisibis toward Dara]
Summer, five hundred and thirty A.D. The frontier between the Roman and Persian empires, in what is now southeastern Turkey. A Persian army marches out of the city of Nisibis toward the Roman fortress of Dara, only a few miles away. Its orders are simple: take the fortress, and break the Roman frontier wide open.

[MAP: dara-2 | Battlefield zoom at Dara. Counters: ROMANS ~25,000 (blue) vs PERSIANS 40,000 (red), then +10,000 drops in beside the red counter. Plaques: BELISARIUS (age 25) · HERMOGENES vs PEROZES]
The Persian commander, Perozes, has around forty thousand men, and ten thousand more are on their way from Nisibis. Against him stands a Roman army of about twenty-five thousand, many of them raw recruits. Its commander is Belisarius, twenty-five years old, in his first great battle as a general.

[MAP: dara-3 | Persian army deploys on the plain in the classic formation: infantry center, heavy cavalry wings; caption: CATAPHRACTS · IMMORTALS; camera shows the massive red line]
Persia's great weapon was its heavy cavalry. Armoured riders on armoured horses, the finest of them an elite guard called the Immortals. In open country, a charge of that kind could smash an infantry line in minutes. And the plain in front of Dara was open country.

[MAP: dara-4 | Perozes plaque with speech bubble: "PREPARE MY BATH IN DARA"]
Perozes believed the battle was already won. The Romans were fewer, younger, and they were not coming out to fight. They were digging. According to the historian Procopius, Perozes sent Belisarius a message telling him to have a bath ready for him in Dara the next day.

[MAP: dara-5 | Camera pushes in; a white trench line draws itself in front of the walls: straight flank sections pushed forward, center section pulled back, connecting cross-trenches; gaps left for sallies; labels: TRENCH, REFUSED CENTER]
But Belisarius saw something different. He did not want the trench as a wall to hide behind. He wanted it as a funnel. A short distance from the city, he dug a long ditch, with crossings left open. The two outer sections stood forward. The center section was pulled back. Behind the center he placed his weakest troops, his infantry. Behind the forward wings, his cavalry.

[MAP: dara-6 | Blue units appear: infantry behind center trench; cavalry behind flank trenches; small Hun cavalry units (SUNICAS · AIGAN; SIMMAS · ASCAN) placed in the two inner corners where the trenches meet; on the far left a hill, and 300 HERULI (PHARAS) slide behind it and fade to half-hidden]
And in the corners, where the forward trenches met the center, he placed small units of Hunnic horse archers, fast riders who could strike left or right. Finally, on the far left, he hid three hundred Herulian horsemen behind a low hill, out of sight. Any Persian charge would have to run between the trenches, straight into a crossfire.

[MAP: dara-7 | Small duel icon between the lines; caption: DAY ONE · CHAMPIONS; a single blue figure 'ANDREAS' defeats a red rider, twice]
On the first day, the armies only watched each other. A young Persian rider came out and challenged any Roman to single combat. The man who answered was not a soldier at all, but a wrestling trainer from the city baths named Andreas. He knocked the Persian from his horse and killed him. When a second champion came out, Andreas killed him too. The Roman army roared.

[MAP: dara-8 | Day two. Clock/sun icon moves to afternoon; caption: PERSIANS EAT LATE · ROMANS HUNGRY; red reinforcement arrow arrives from Nisibis: +10,000]
The next day, the Persian reinforcements arrived. Perozes waited until the afternoon to attack. Persians ate their main meal late in the day; Romans ate at noon. He hoped to catch the enemy hungry and tired. Then the Persian right wing charged.

[MAP: dara-9 | Red cavalry arrow crashes into the Roman left behind the forward trench; blue left cavalry pushed back; then the hidden Heruli burst over the hill into the Persian rear (arrow), and Hun horse archers from the left corner hit the Persian flank; red right wing breaks and flees]
It hit the Roman left hard and began pushing it back. Then the trap sprang. From behind the hill, the three hundred Herulians came charging into the Persian rear. At the same moment, the Huns in the corner rode out and struck the Persians in the flank. Hit from two directions, the Persian right wing broke and fled.

[MAP: dara-10 | Perozes plaque; red IMMORTALS unit shifts secretly to the Persian left; big red arrow crashes into the Roman right; blue right cavalry falls back toward the walls]
Perozes had not given up. He had quietly moved his Immortals to his left wing, and now he threw his heaviest cavalry at the Roman right. The Roman horsemen there were driven back toward the city walls. For a moment, it looked as if the battle had turned.

[MAP: dara-11 | Blue Hun units from the LEFT corner race across behind the center trench to the right corner (arrow), join the right-corner Huns and Belisarius's guard; combined blue arrow slices into the flank of the Persian cavalry, cutting it in two; red standard icon falls; caption: STANDARD-BEARER KILLED]
But the charge had taken the Persians deep into the funnel, and Belisarius was ready. He sent the Huns from his left corner racing across behind the center to join those on the right, together with his own guard. They slammed into the side of the advancing Persian column and cut it in two. The Persian standard-bearer was killed. His cavalry, separated and leaderless, collapsed.

[MAP: dara-12 | Red left wing routs; red infantry in the center abandons shields and runs; counter: PERSIAN DEAD ~5,000 on one wing; blue units stop at a dashed line: caption: NO RECKLESS PURSUIT]
The Persian infantry in the center saw both wings running and threw down their shields. Around five thousand Persians died in the rout of their left wing alone. Belisarius stopped his men from chasing too far. A victory was worth more than a massacre, if the chase could turn into a disaster.

[MAP: dara-13 | Recap diagram: trench funnel highlighted; three method lines appear one by one]
It was the first great Roman victory over Persia in a generation, and it showed Belisarius's method in miniature. He shaped the battlefield: a trench that turned an open plain into a funnel. He struck what held the enemy together: their charges, their flanks, and their standard. And he made time fight for him: he waited behind his ditch until the Persians were committed, then hit back with everything at once.

[ARCHIVE: Byzantine soldiers, manuscript illustration (e.g. Madrid Skylitzes) — slow zoom]
Dara made Belisarius famous. There is a lot more of this kind of tactical breakdown on this channel, so if you're enjoying it, consider subscribing. Because three years later, Justinian sent Belisarius across the sea, to destroy an entire kingdom with a few thousand horsemen.

## MOVE 2 — TRICAMARUM: STRIKE THE HEAD

[MAP: tricam-1 | Title card MOVE 2 · TRICAMARUM · DECEMBER 533 AD. Mediterranean map: fleet arrow from CONSTANTINOPLE via Sicily to North Africa; landing near CAPUT VADA; blue counter 15,000; Vandal Kingdom (red) around CARTHAGE]
Five hundred and thirty-three A.D. A Roman fleet of five hundred transports sails from Constantinople to North Africa. On board are about fifteen thousand soldiers. Their target is the Vandal kingdom, the Germanic people who had seized Carthage a century earlier and ruled the richest provinces of the old empire.

[MAP: tricam-2 | North Africa zoom: blue arrow from the landing up the coast to AD DECIMUM (10th milestone) and into CARTHAGE; caption: SEPTEMBER 533 · CARTHAGE TAKEN; Gelimer plaque retreats west]
Belisarius landed, marched on Carthage, and at a place called Ad Decimum, the tenth milestone from the city, he defeated the Vandal king Gelimer in a confused running battle. Carthage opened its gates. But Gelimer escaped. Over the next three months, he gathered every Vandal warrior he could find, called his brother Tzazon back from Sardinia with his troops, and marched on Carthage again.

[MAP: tricam-3 | Battlefield map at Tricamarum, about 30 km west of Carthage: a small stream (label: STREAM) crosses the plain; Vandal camp (red) on the far side; counters: VANDALS 15,000-30,000? vs ROMAN CAVALRY ~5,000; Roman infantry counter far behind, dim, with caption: STILL MARCHING]
They met near a place called Tricamarum, some twenty miles west of Carthage. The Vandals were camped behind a small stream. Their army was far larger than the Roman force; modern estimates range from fifteen to thirty thousand. Belisarius had come ahead with only his cavalry, perhaps five thousand horsemen. His infantry was still on the road, hours behind.

[MAP: tricam-4 | Plaques: GELIMER (king) and TZAZON (his brother, center); speech bubble from Gelimer: "SWORDS ONLY"]
Gelimer believed time was on his side. No sane general, he thought, would attack across a stream against a larger army without infantry to hold the line. He even ordered his men to fight with swords alone, no spears or javelins, as if the battle would be a brawl the Vandals were sure to win at close quarters. He placed his brother Tzazon, with his veterans from Sardinia, in the center.

[MAP: tricam-5 | Camera pushes on the Vandal center; red plaques GELIMER and TZAZON pulse; lines from the plaques spread out to every red unit like strings; caption: ONE FAMILY · ONE ARMY]
But Belisarius saw something different. The Vandal army was big, but it was held together by one thing: its royal family. Every order, every warrior's loyalty, ran through Gelimer and his brothers. If the center held, the army held. If the head was struck, the whole body might fall.

[MAP: tricam-6 | Blue unit JOHN THE ARMENIAN crosses the stream in a short arrow, strikes the Vandal center, bounces back; caption: CHARGE 1]
So he did not wait. Around midday, he sent his officer John the Armenian across the stream with a few picked horsemen, straight at the Vandal center. The Vandals pushed them back easily. But they did not follow them over the water.

[MAP: tricam-7 | Second, larger blue arrow crosses, strikes, recoils; caption: CHARGE 2]
John went back with more of Belisarius's own guard and charged a second time. Again the Vandals threw him back. Each time, the center held, and each time, Tzazon and his men stood firm and waited for the next.

[MAP: tricam-8 | Third charge: whole blue cavalry line, with BELISARIUS's banner, crosses at once; melee star at the center; TZAZON plaque turns grey with caption: KILLED; red center collapses]
Then John charged a third time, with almost the whole of Belisarius's guard behind him. In the fierce fighting in the center, Tzazon was cut down. At the same moment, the Roman line crossed the stream and attacked along the whole front. The Vandal center, with its commander gone, broke.

[MAP: tricam-9 | Red units flow back into their camp; Roman infantry counter arrives (late afternoon); Gelimer plaque slips out of the camp to the west with a small dotted arrow; caption: THE KING FLEES; red units scatter in all directions]
The Vandals fled to their camp. By late afternoon the Roman infantry arrived, and Belisarius advanced on the camp. Gelimer lost his nerve. He had just lost one brother at Ad Decimum, and now another. Without a word to his army, he mounted his horse and rode away. When the Vandals realized their king was gone, the whole army dissolved into the night.

[MAP: tricam-10 | Casualty counters: ROMANS fewer than 50 · VANDALS ~800; then the Roman units break formation and swarm over the red camp; caption: LOOT]
According to Procopius, fewer than fifty Romans died that day, against about eight hundred Vandals. But what followed nearly undid the victory. Belisarius's soldiers, poor men who had suddenly found the treasure of a kingdom, broke ranks and scattered to loot the Vandal camp. Procopius himself wrote that if the enemy had turned and attacked that night, the Roman army could have been destroyed.

[ARCHIVE: 19th-century engraving of Gelimer's surrender / Gelimer brought before Belisarius]
The enemy never came back. Within weeks Gelimer was trapped in the mountains, and in March of five hundred and thirty-four he surrendered. The Vandal kingdom, which had lasted a hundred years, had fallen in a single campaign. Legend says that when Gelimer was brought before Justinian in Constantinople, he could only repeat a line from the Bible: vanity of vanities, all is vanity.

[MAP: tricam-11 | Recap diagram: three arrows across the stream into the center; method lines appear]
Again, the same method. He shaped the battlefield: he did not let the stream or his missing infantry decide when the battle began. He struck what held the enemy together: not the Vandal army, but its center, and the royal brothers who commanded it. And he made time fight for him: by attacking before Gelimer expected it, he took away the Vandals' greatest advantage, their numbers, before they could use it.

## MOVE 3 — THE SIEGE OF ROME: THE TRAP WITHIN THE WALLS

[MAP: rome-1 | Title card MOVE 3 · THE SIEGE OF ROME · 537–538 AD. Italy map: blue arrow from Sicily up through Naples to ROME (536); Ostrogothic Kingdom red, capital RAVENNA; big red arrow from Ravenna toward Rome]
Two years later, Belisarius was in Italy. He took Sicily, stormed Naples, and in December of five hundred and thirty-six marched into Rome without a fight. But the Gothic king, Vitiges, gathered the full strength of his people and marched south to take the city back. Belisarius had around five thousand soldiers. It was not nearly enough to face the Goths in the open.

[MAP: rome-2 | Rome close-up: the Aurelian Walls (12 miles, 18 gates), Tiber through the city; Mausoleum of Hadrian by the river; Gothic camps (7) ring the north and east, one across the Tiber; counters: GOTHS 150,000? (Procopius) / 20,000-30,000 (modern) vs 5,000]
Instead, he shut himself inside the walls. The Aurelian Walls ran for about twelve miles and had eighteen gates, far too many for five thousand men to hold. The Goths built seven fortified camps around the city and settled in. Vitiges believed Rome would fall in one of two ways: starved into surrender, or stormed by his massive army.

[MAP: rome-3 | Aqueduct lines leading into the city are cut with red X marks; icons of mills along the aqueducts go dark; caption: NO WATER · NO MILLS · NO BREAD]
First, he cut the aqueducts. Rome's water supply was gone, but that was not the real danger. The city's grain mills were driven by the water of the aqueducts. With the mills stopped, there would be no flour, and without flour, no bread. A city of many thousands would begin to starve.

[MAP: rome-4 | Camera pushes in on the Tiber bridges; small boat-mill icons chained between pairs of boats appear below the bridge, water arrows turning the wheels; caption: FLOATING MILLS]
But Belisarius saw something different. The aqueducts had stopped, but the Tiber had not. He had pairs of boats anchored in the river, just below a bridge where the current ran fastest, with mill wheels mounted between them. The river turned the wheels, and the wheels ground the grain. When the Goths found out and threw logs and bodies into the river to smash the mills, he stretched iron chains across the water to catch them.

[MAP: rome-5 | Gothic assault, day 18: red siege towers (4) on wheels pulled by ox teams roll toward the SALARIAN GATE from the north; rams and ladders; caption: DAY 18 · THE GREAT ASSAULT]
On the eighteenth day of the siege, Vitiges launched his great assault. Toward the Salarian Gate, in the north of the city, the Goths rolled huge wooden siege towers, taller than the walls, each pulled by teams of oxen. If the towers reached the wall, Gothic warriors would pour onto the ramparts.

[MAP: rome-6 | Blue archer arrows fly from the wall; they target only the ox teams in front of the towers; ox icons drop; towers stop short of the wall with caption: STRANDED; Belisarius plaque on the gate, speech bubble: "SHOOT THE OXEN"]
The Roman defenders watched the towers come closer, and they panicked. But Belisarius laughed. He ordered his archers to hold, and then, when the towers were in range, to shoot at one target only: the oxen. The animals went down. The towers stopped dead, far short of the walls, and there was no way to move them. The greatest weapons of the Gothic army stood useless in the open, and later they were burned.

[MAP: rome-7 | Other assault points flash red: the tomb of Hadrian by the Tiber (caption: STATUES THROWN DOWN), and the VIVARIUM at the Praenestine Gate (a red breach, then a blue sally arrow bursts out of the gate and hits the Goths from behind)]
The assault failed everywhere. At the tomb of Hadrian, the defenders ran out of arrows and smashed the marble statues on the roof, hurling the pieces down onto the Goths. At the Praenestine Gate, where the Goths broke through an outer wall, Belisarius sent troops out of another gate to hit them from behind. By nightfall, thousands of Goths lay dead around the walls.

[MAP: rome-8 | Inside the walls: lock icon on each gate changes; guard units rotate positions between gates; caption: LOCKS CHANGED TWICE A MONTH]
Then the siege settled into a long war of nerves. Belisarius knew that a city is often lost not to an assault, but to a traitor who opens a gate. So he changed the locks on the gates twice every month, and constantly moved his guards from one post to another, so that no one could bribe the same men twice.

[MAP: rome-9 | Blue sally arrows shoot out of the gates, strike red foraging parties, and snap back inside; later: blue reinforcement arrow arrives from the south (1,600 cavalry, then more); red camps begin to dim with caption: FAMINE · PLAGUE]
He used his few horse archers to raid the Goths constantly, darting out to hit their foraging parties and dashing back behind the walls before the Gothic cavalry could catch them. Reinforcements slipped in from the east. And slowly, the siege turned inside out. Now it was the huge Gothic army, camped in the marshy countryside through a long, hot summer, that ran out of food. Disease spread through their camps.

[MAP: rome-10 | Blue arrow: JOHN's cavalry raid goes up the east coast into PICENUM toward RIMINI, threatening RAVENNA; red camps around Rome dissolve, red arrow retreats north; caption: MARCH 538 · SIEGE LIFTED after 1 year and 9 days]
Finally, Belisarius sent a force of cavalry deep into Gothic territory, on the Adriatic coast, threatening the Gothic capital itself. In March of five hundred and thirty-eight, after a year and nine days, Vitiges burned his camps and marched away. His army had lost huge numbers of men, to battle, hunger and disease. Five thousand defenders had held Rome against an entire nation.

[MAP: rome-11 | Recap: walls, floating mills, stranded towers, retreating red arrow; method lines appear]
It was the purest form of the method. He shaped the battlefield: he turned Rome's walls and its river into weapons. He struck what held the enemy together: not the Gothic warriors, but the oxen that pulled their siege towers and the food that kept their army alive. And he made time fight for him: a siege is supposed to starve the defender, but Belisarius made it starve the attackers instead.

## ENDING

[MAP: ending-ravenna | Italy map: blue arrows advance north to RAVENNA (540); a crown icon floats from the Goths toward the Belisarius plaque, then Belisarius hands it on to a JUSTINIAN plaque]
Two years later, in five hundred and forty, the Goths were trapped in their capital, Ravenna. In desperation they made Belisarius an extraordinary offer: betray Justinian, and become emperor of the West himself. Belisarius pretended to accept. The Goths opened their gates. He marched in, took the city, and handed it to his emperor.

[ARCHIVE: Jacques-Louis David, 'Belisarius Begging for Alms' (1781) — slow zoom]
Justinian never fully trusted him again. There is a famous story that the emperor had Belisarius blinded, and that the old general ended his days begging in the streets of Constantinople. It inspired great paintings, like this one by Jacques-Louis David. But it is a legend, written centuries later. The truth is that Belisarius fell in and out of favour, but he died a rich man, at home, in five hundred and sixty-five.

[ARCHIVE: San Vitale mosaic detail — Belisarius's face, slow fade]
He won against the Persians, against the Vandals, and against the Goths, and he almost always did it with fewer men than his enemy. He never had the numbers. He had something better: a way of thinking.
