## BACKGROUND (~0:55 – ~1:45)

[ARCHIVE: Cleburne portrait (LoC / Wikimedia, PD, 3D depth); headline date card "1828 / COUNTY CORK, IRELAND"; headline date card "1863 / CHATTANOOGA, TENNESSEE"; Bragg portrait (LoC, PD); Grant portrait (PD); never a slideshow; no battle-flag art; no Kurz & Allison prints]
Patrick Cleburne was born near Cork in eighteen twenty-eight. At eighteen he joined the British army as a private, bought his way out three years later, and sailed for America. In Helena, Arkansas, he worked as a druggist and then a lawyer. When the war came, he joined his town's volunteer company, and rose to command a division in the Army of Tennessee under General Braxton Bragg. He fought at Shiloh, and at Chickamauga in September eighteen sixty-three, where the Army of Tennessee won its biggest victory and pinned the Union army inside the city of Chattanooga. It looked as if the Confederacy might win the war in the West.

[MAP: bg-1 | `chatt` (option 1, Oct-Nov 1863): Union besieged in CHATTANOOGA, Confederates on LOOKOUT MOUNTAIN and MISSIONARY RIDGE; Grant badge arrives; stamps per word: CRACKER LINE (supplies flow, OCT), LOOKOUT MOUNTAIN · 24 NOV (red wins the mountain), 25 NOV; Thomas's red columns storm the CENTER of Missionary Ridge (arrows, impacts, shake), blue-grey center collapses and flows south; the NORTH END (Tunnel Hill) stays blue, ring pulses; method card frame appears empty: CLEBURNE'S METHOD]
Then Ulysses Grant arrived, and the siege was broken. Bragg's army sat on the high ground above the city, on Lookout Mountain and along Missionary Ridge. On the twenty-fourth of November, Union troops took Lookout Mountain. The next day, George Thomas's soldiers stormed straight up the centre of Missionary Ridge, and the Confederate line there collapsed. But at the far northern end of the ridge, on a knob called Tunnel Hill, one Confederate division never gave way. That division was Cleburne's. Three of the Union generals he was about to face, Sherman, Hooker and Howard, would meet him again before the year was out.

## MOVE 1 — LET THEM TIRE ON THE HILL, THEN STRIKE: TUNNEL HILL (~1:45 – ~6:05)

[MAP: move1-1 | Title card MOVE 1 · TUNNEL HILL · 25 NOVEMBER 1863. `tunnel` (option 2 glowing fronts), night of 23-24 NOV: Cleburne's blue division (count card ~4,000) rides back from CHICKAMAUGA STATION (train icons, a wagon) to the north end of MISSIONARY RIDGE; red SHERMAN badge (count card 16,000 in three divisions, ABT) across the TENNESSEE RIVER on BILLY GOAT HILL; the RAILROAD TUNNEL glows under TUNNEL HILL; ravine between the hills pulses]
Grant's plan for the twenty-fifth gave Sherman the main role. Sherman had crossed the Tennessee River in the dark, and was to roll up Bragg's right flank from the north, at the end of Missionary Ridge. That end was held by almost nobody, because Cleburne's division had just been ordered onto trains for Knoxville. Then Bragg changed his mind, and sent it racing back. Cleburne arrived on the twenty-fourth to face William Sherman, who had about sixteen thousand men in three divisions. Cleburne had about four thousand.

[MAP: move1-2 | Red belief card: THE END OF THE RIDGE IS EASY; Sherman's red units seize BILLY GOAT HILL (stamp WRONG HILL: dotted ravine appears between it and Tunnel Hill, red pulse); then the key move: Cleburne's blue guns climb TUNNEL HILL (K.gun), infantry sets on the crest and the railroad cut, and the ravine and open fields are the only way in (arrows trace the approaches, red pulse on each: THE SADDLE · THE WESTERN FIELD)]
Sherman believed the end of the ridge was an easy prize. On the twenty-fourth, he seized a hill, and then discovered it was the wrong one: a deep ravine lay between it and the real Tunnel Hill. Cleburne saw something different. He did not have to beat Sherman's whole army. He had to make Sherman's men cross the ground in front of him, a few at a time. In his own report, he called Tunnel Hill the highest point on my line. He put his guns on top of it, near the mouth of the railroad tunnel, and his infantry along the crest. Then he let the ravine and the open fields do the rest.

[MAP: move1-2b | Hold on the hill (option 2): slow push-in on Tunnel Hill; blue guns on the crest, the railroad tunnel below, the western fields open; caption per word: THE CREST · THE CUT · THE OPEN FIELD; red units form up on Billy Goat Hill and in the western valley (counters), a red pulse on each approach: NO COVER; Hardee badge (real photo) arrives from the south]
That was the whole idea. A hill like that does not need many defenders if the attackers must cross open ground to reach it. Every Union brigade would have to climb a steep slope, or cross a field under fire, with no cover and no way to bring its full weight to bear. And Cleburne had a general behind him, William Hardee, who could feed him fresh men if the fight lasted. It was exactly what Sherman's plan had not allowed for.

[MAP: move1-3 | Morning 25 NOV (option 2): red CORSE's brigade (~920) charges south from Billy Goat Hill up the narrow spine, two waves, blue fire, K.impact, shake; red falls back; quote card "GO BACK AND MAKE THAT CHARGE IMMEDIATELY; TIME IS EVERYTHING" - SHERMAN (small); red LOOMIS moves across the western fields toward the railroad embankment, stops short; MATTHIES crosses the flat (impacts, MG, shake), blue fire shreds it; each repulse = the red front band stays pinned at the hill's foot (never moves)]
Sherman did not attack until about eight o'clock, and then he sent his men in one brigade at a time. John Corse's brigade, fewer than a thousand men, charged along the narrow spine of the ridge and was torn apart. Sherman urged them forward again: time is everything. Another brigade, under John Loomis, crossed the open fields on the west and was stopped at the railroad embankment. Then another, under Colonel Matthies, tried the flat ground and was shredded. Each time, Cleburne's men fired from the crest, and each time the Union line broke before it reached them. Sherman had sixteen thousand men on that field. He never used more than a fraction of them at once.

[MAP: move1-4 | Afternoon (option 2): red RAUM's brigade attacks at 2 p.m. (arrows, impacts), cannot break through; Hardee's reinforcement badges: blue brigades arrive on the flanks (arrows from the south), count card grows; red column thins (counter drops); ABOUT 4 PM: blue lines leap off the crest and charge down all around the hill (arrows, MG, shake), the red survivors break across the fields, prisoners (bars icon)]
At two in the afternoon, Sherman threw in one more brigade, and it could not break through either. Meanwhile, the Confederate general Hardee sent Cleburne reinforcements, so he could bring fresh troops into the line faster than Sherman could answer. By about four o'clock, the Union soldiers were exhausted and short of ammunition. Cleburne chose that moment. His men came down off the hill all along the line, and the Union attack collapsed. Hundreds of Federals surrendered, and the rest ran back across the fields to their own lines. Colonel Putnam of the Ninety-third Illinois had been killed leading his men up the slope, and Colonel Matthies was mortally wounded.

[MAP: move1-5 | Night (K.night): 2 miles south the broken Confederate center (grey/blue streams south), Cleburne's blue division turns into the REAR GUARD: rolls back across the CHICKAMAUGA CREEK bridges (ring on bridges, they burn: smoke); CASUALTY card: TUNNEL HILL · UNION ~2,000 · CONFEDERATE ~220 (ABT); method card line 1: LET THEM TIRE ON THE HILL, THEN STRIKE]
While Cleburne held the hill, Thomas's men had broken the centre, two miles to the south, and Bragg's army was in flight. That night, Cleburne's undefeated division became its rear guard. Sherman's attack on Tunnel Hill had cost him almost two thousand men. Cleburne's division had lost a little over two hundred. Let them tire on the hill, then strike.

But Tunnel Hill was only the beginning. So how do you stop an entire pursuing army with nothing but one tired division and a narrow pass? That's the kind of question we answer here, and subscribing is how you catch the next one. Here's how Cleburne did it.

## MOVE 2 — HIDE YOUR STRENGTH UNTIL THEY ARE CLOSE: RINGGOLD GAP (~6:05 – ~10:45)

[MAP: move2-1 | Title card MOVE 2 · RINGGOLD GAP · 27 NOVEMBER 1863. Wide (`ringgold`, option 1): Bragg's wagon train and guns (icons) stuck and jammed on the road to DALTON; red HOOKER's pursuit (count card 12,000+, ABT) behind at RINGGOLD; the gap through TAYLOR'S RIDGE (400 ft walls, railroad + road + creek glow); quote card "TO HOLD THIS POSITION AT ALL HAZARDS, AND KEEP BACK THE ENEMY UNTIL THE ARTILLERY AND TRANSPORTATION OF THE ARMY ARE SECURE" - BRAGG'S ORDER; Cleburne's blue division (count card ~4,000, ABT)]
By the twenty-sixth, the army was streaming south in cold, wet weather and bad order. Its wagons and artillery were stuck on muddy roads, and Union troops were on its heels. The only good ground was a mountain called Taylor's Ridge, split by a narrow gap, with a small town named Ringgold at its mouth. Bragg ordered Cleburne to hold the gap at Ringgold, in his own words, at all hazards, and keep back the enemy until the artillery and transportation of the army were secure. Cleburne had about four thousand men and two guns. Joseph Hooker's pursuing columns, marching on the road behind him, numbered more than ten thousand.

[MAP: move2-1b | Red belief card: A BEATEN MOB; red HOOKER badge (real photo) and his columns (seven red arrows) march south on the road; the burned creek bridge slows them (small smoke); the gap ahead looks open; blue-grey stragglers and wagons pass THROUGH the gap behind Cleburne's line (arrows), then the gap closes (ring)]
The Union generals thought they were chasing a beaten mob. Hooker's columns had been delayed by a burned bridge, but they came on, seven columns strong, expecting to scoop up whatever the army had left behind. In a sense, they were right about the army. It had broken at Missionary Ridge. But they had not counted on the one division that had not.

Now stop for a moment, and put yourself in Cleburne's place. Hooker's men are coming, and your guns number only two. Do you meet them in the open, near the town and the stone railroad depot? Or do you hide your men in the gap and up on the ridges, and let the enemy walk into it? Tell us in the comments what you would have done. And then, here is what he did.

[MAP: move2-3 | Close on the gap (option 2): blue GOVAN in the notch, GRANBURY on the north ridge, POLK on the south ridge, LOWREY in reserve behind (arrows, rings); two blue guns (Lt. GOLDTHWAITE) hidden behind brush at the mouth (brush icons); Cleburne badge; captions THE NECK · THE HIDDEN GUNS · THE RESERVE]
Cleburne chose the second. He put Govan's brigade in the narrow throat of the gap, and the brigades of Granbury and Polk up on the walls of Taylor's Ridge, on either side. Lowrey's brigade stayed back in reserve. And he hid his two guns, under Lieutenant Richard Goldthwaite, behind cut brush at the mouth of the gap.

[MAP: move2-4 | About 8 a.m. (option 2): red OSTERHAUS's division marches out of the town toward the gap, no guns (red cannons stuck at the creek), skirmishers advance; red belief card: ONLY CAVALRY AHEAD; at ~150 yards Cleburne's brush icons vanish, the two guns fire (K.gun -> impact, shake), blue crossfire from Govan; red column breaks behind the railroad embankment and the depot; stamp THEY NEVER SAW IT COMING]
At about eight in the morning, Peter Osterhaus's Union division marched out of Ringgold toward the gap. Its own artillery had not caught up. Osterhaus later said he thought he faced only a few hundred cavalry. Cleburne waited until the Union column was close, and then gave the order to fire. The brush was cleared, the guns opened, and Govan's men poured fire across the mouth of the gap. Osterhaus's men fell back behind the railroad embankment.

[MAP: move2-5 | WILLIAMSON's red brigade climbs the NORTH ridge (arrows) to turn the blue right; Cleburne sees it, three of LOWREY's regiments run up the paths (blue arrows, fast) and reach the crest first; GEARY's red division assaults the ridge (arrows), blue fire from the top (K.impact, MG, shake), red falls down the slope; stamp CREIGHTON KILLED (small), CRANE KILLED (small)]
Stopped at the gap, the Union commanders tried to go over the top. A brigade climbed Taylor's Ridge to get around Cleburne's flank. He saw it coming, and sent part of Lowrey's reserve up the slope at the double, and they reached the crest first. Then John Geary's division attacked up the ridge, and Cleburne's men, waiting at the top, drove it back down the mountain. Union soldiers did reach the crest and planted a flag, but they could not hold it. Two of Geary's senior officers, Colonel William Creighton and Lieutenant Colonel Orrin Crane, were killed.

[MAP: move2-6 | Noon: courier to Cleburne (static, hit): the TRAINS ARE SAFE past TUNNEL HILL; red guns finally arrive at the creek (small red cannons, impacts); blue guns pulled out by hand, brigades fall back across the creek bridges, bridges burn (smoke); red stops at the gap; Grant badge (stamp PURSUIT ENDED); CASUALTY card: RINGGOLD GAP · UNION ~500 · CONFEDERATE 221 (CLEBURNE'S REPORT); stamp THANKS OF THE CONFEDERATE CONGRESS; method card line 2: HIDE YOUR STRENGTH UNTIL THEY ARE CLOSE]
Around noon, word came that the army's train was safe. Union artillery was only now arriving at the creek. Cleburne pulled his division back, and burned the bridges behind it. Grant reached Ringgold that day and called off the pursuit. The Union side lost about five hundred men there. Cleburne reported twenty killed, one hundred and ninety wounded and eleven missing, two hundred and twenty-one men in all. For this stand, the Confederate Congress passed a formal vote of thanks to Cleburne and his division. The army reached its winter quarters at Dalton, Georgia, intact. The price of the stand was small beside what it saved. Within days, Bragg, who had lost his army's centre, resigned. Hide your strength until they are close.

## MOVE 3 — LET THE GROUND DO THE KILLING: PICKETT'S MILL (~10:45 – ~15:20)

[MAP: move3-1 | Title card MOVE 3 · PICKETT'S MILL · 27 MAY 1864. Calendar rolls from NOV 1863 to JAN 1864 at DALTON (ticks); the memorandum card folds open: "AS BETWEEN THE LOSS OF INDEPENDENCE AND THE LOSS OF SLAVERY..." - CLEBURNE, 2 JAN 1864 (ending cut off, the rest on the next card); stamps: TO WIN THE WAR · NOT TO END SLAVERY · SUPPRESSED; badges of Davis (text card, no likeness if no photo) and Walker; Cleburne's badge: the rank stays at DIVISION; calendar jumps to MAY 1864]
In January eighteen sixty-four, in the winter camp at Dalton, Cleburne put his name to a long paper. He argued that slavery had become one of the Confederacy's chief sources of weakness, and that the South should free and arm enslaved men to win the war. His own words were: as between the loss of independence and the loss of slavery, every patriot would give up the latter. He was not trying to end slavery for its own sake. He was trying to save a government that had been built on it. Most of his fellow officers were appalled, and President Jefferson Davis ordered the paper suppressed. In May, the armies were fighting in Georgia again.

[MAP: move3-2 | Theatre `corridor` (option 1, 25-26 MAY 1864): Atlanta Campaign; Johnston's blue lines at NEW HOPE CHURCH facing Sherman's red armies; red SHERMAN badge, Howard badge (real photo); the red right-hand column slides east in the woods toward PICKETT'S MILL (dotted, hidden), 14,000 (ABT); blue cavalry (KELLY) screens the east flank (cavalry icon) and spots them; Cleburne's blue division slides east to meet them (arrows, hits), stamp THE FLANK IS NOT EMPTY]
Sherman had been stopped at New Hope Church, and looked for a way around the Confederate right flank. He ordered Oliver Howard to take a large force on a long march through the woods to the east. Howard believed he was marching around the end of Joseph Johnston's line. But the Confederate cavalry saw him coming, and Johnston moved Cleburne's division to the end of the line, in time to meet him.

[MAP: move3-2b | `pickett` wide (option 2): the dotted red march arc ends at the creek; blue lines newly arrived, men digging with bayonets and axes (log icons, half-built); red Howard badge (stamp: EMPTY FLANK?); count cards: BLUE ~4,000 (CLEBURNE) · RED WOOD ~4,500 (ABT) in front, more behind; hit]
The ground at Pickett's Mill was worse than anything Howard had expected. Cleburne's men had arrived only hours before, and had not finished their works. But the ground did not need them to. A ravine lay between the two armies, narrow and steep, and any attacker had to go down into it, and out the far side, under fire.

[MAP: move3-3 | `pickett` close (option 2): the ravine of PICKETT'S MILL CREEK glows (V-shaped, brush icons); blue GRANBURY on the ridge above, GOVAN beside, LOWREY in reserve, two guns (Key) at the bend, cavalry on the far right; red WOOD's division of Howard's column forms in a column of brigades at the top of the ravine (HAZEN, GIBSON, KNEFLER in a line, count card ~1,457 shown at the end); caption: A QUARTER-MILE UPHILL THROUGH ALMOST IMPASSABLE TANGLE (BIERCE)]
At Pickett's Mill, Cleburne found a ravine between him and the Union column: steep, choked with brush and fallen timber, with a ridge on the far side where his men stood. Wood's division, on the Union side, formed up in a column, with one brigade behind the other. Ambrose Bierce, a staff officer in the lead brigade under William Hazen, later wrote that the way ahead was a quarter of a mile uphill through an almost impassable tangle of underbrush.

[MAP: move3-4 | HAZEN's red brigade charges down into the ravine (arrows, brush slows them: the arrows fray), hit by Granbury's frontal fire and canister from the bend (K.gun -> impact, MG, shake), flank fire from the east; Cleburne sends LOWREY's reserve to the right (blue arrows, fast); red survivors stream back, stamp ONE BRIGADE AT A TIME]
Howard attacked at about half past four. He sent forward Hazen's brigade alone, without scouting, and it plunged into the ravine. The brush tore its line apart. Granbury's Texans, who had not had time to build proper works, met it at close range with rifle fire and canister, and Cleburne fed in Lowrey's reserve on the threatened flank. On the far right, Confederate cavalry slowed the Union advance, and bought Cleburne the minutes he needed. Hazen's brigade was shattered, and its survivors streamed back up the ravine.

[MAP: move3-5 | GIBSON's red brigade goes down the SAME ravine over Hazen's dead (arrows), cut down again (impacts, shake); KNEFLER's brigade goes in at dusk; the red counters drop; quote card "THERE WAS A DEAD-LINE BEYOND WHICH NO MAN ADVANCED BUT TO FALL" - BIERCE; Bierce badge (real photo), small quote card "WE WILL PUT IN HAZEN AND SEE WHAT SUCCESS HE HAS" - WOOD, AS BIERCE RECORDED IT; stamp HOWARD PLANNED IT · SHERMAN ORDERED IT (BIERCE)]
Then Wood sent in a second brigade, under William Gibson, down the same ravine, over the bodies of the first. Later came a third, under Frederick Knefler. When Wood gave the order for Hazen to go in, he said only: we will put in Hazen and see what success he has. Hazen did not say a word, and Bierce read in his face a sense of what he later called a criminal blunder. Bierce wrote that there was a dead line beyond which no man advanced but to fall. In his account, Howard planned the attack, and Sherman ordered it. Nobody had looked at the ground, and Cleburne's men were waiting for every wave.

[MAP: move3-6 | NIGHT (K.night): blue lines advance down the ravine (Granbury), prisoners (bars icon), red withdraws north; next morning: dead lie in the ravine (grey counters); CASUALTY card: PICKETT'S MILL · UNION ~1,600 · CONFEDERATE ~450 (CLEBURNE'S REPORT, ABT); stamp SHERMAN'S FLANK MARCH FAILED; method card line 3: LET THE GROUND DO THE KILLING]
After dark, Cleburne's men cleared the ravine and took prisoners. Wood's division alone had lost about fourteen hundred men, and the whole Union force about sixteen hundred. Cleburne reported about four hundred and fifty Confederate casualties. Bierce survived, and later wrote an essay called The Crime at Pickett's Mill, one of the most famous eyewitness accounts of the war. Sherman's flank march had failed, and the Confederate line stood. The Union army would have to try another way around. Let the ground do the killing.

## ENDING (~15:20 – ~16:00)

[MAP: end-1 | `chatt`/`corridor` theatre: the three moves light in turn (Tunnel Hill, Ringgold Gap, Pickett's Mill); method card full: CLEBURNE'S METHOD: LET THEM TIRE ON THE HILL, THEN STRIKE · HIDE YOUR STRENGTH UNTIL THEY ARE CLOSE · LET THE GROUND DO THE KILLING; calendar rolls to 30 NOV 1864: FRANKLIN, TENNESSEE; Cleburne's blue badge greys, stamp KILLED LEADING AN ATTACK HE OPPOSED]
Three battles, one method. Cleburne let the Union attackers wear themselves out against ground he had chosen. He hid his strength until they were close enough to hurt. And he never forgot that the ground itself, a hill, a pass, a ravine, could do most of the killing. Cleburne was killed on the thirtieth of November, eighteen sixty-four, a year and three days after Ringgold, leading a doomed assault at Franklin, Tennessee, an attack he had opposed. Later writers called him the Stonewall Jackson of the West. In three battles, with a single division, he had faced Sherman, Hooker and Howard, and beaten each of them on ground he chose. He never rose above the command of a division. Less than four months later, the Confederate Congress authorized black troops, the very idea he had been shunned for, but by then it was far too late.
