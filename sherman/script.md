## BACKGROUND (~1:00 – ~1:45)

[ARCHIVE: Sherman portrait (Brady / LoC, PD, 3D depth); headline date card "1820 / LANCASTER, OHIO"; headline date card "1861 / LOUISIANA STATE SEMINARY" (designed); Barnard / LoC 1864 photo of Sherman on horseback at Federal Fort No. 7, Atlanta (PD); never a slideshow; no battle-flag art]
Sherman was born in Ohio in eighteen twenty, and named after the great Shawnee leader Tecumseh. His family called him Cump. He went to West Point, left the army to become a banker in California, and when the war began, he was running a military school in Louisiana. He resigned when the state left the Union, and went north to fight.

[MAP: bg-1 | Theatre `south` (option 1, 1862-63 -> spring 1864 territory, the Mississippi + Tennessee shown, then the front at CHATTANOOGA); stamps per word: SHILOH 1862 (WOUNDED, small), VICKSBURG 1863, CHATTANOOGA 1863; Grant badge (real photo) beside Sherman's badge; MARCH 1864: Grant's badge slides EAST to Virginia (arrow), Sherman's badge takes the West: card MILITARY DIVISION OF THE MISSISSIPPI; method card frame appears empty: SHERMAN'S METHOD]
After Kentucky, he came back under Ulysses Grant. He was wounded at Shiloh, fought beside Grant at Vicksburg and at Chattanooga, and the two men trusted each other completely. In March eighteen sixty-four, Grant went east to take on Robert E. Lee, and handed Sherman the whole war in the West. Their plan was simple: both of them would attack at the same time, so the Confederacy could not shift troops to stop either one.

## MOVE 1 — HIT THE RAILROAD, NOT THE WALL: THE ATLANTA CAMPAIGN (~1:45 – ~7:00)

[MAP: move1-1 | Title card MOVE 1 · THE ATLANTA CAMPAIGN · MAY - SEPTEMBER 1864. Corridor `corridor` wide (option 1, May 1864 territory): CHATTANOOGA top, ATLANTA bottom; the WESTERN & ATLANTIC RAILROAD glows as one thin line between them (train icon running along it); blue: SHERMAN ~100,000 (count card, ticks) in three counters: THOMAS (Army of the Cumberland), McPHERSON (Army of the Tennessee), SCHOFIELD (Army of the Ohio), badges; red: JOHNSTON (badge) dug in at DALTON behind ROCKY FACE RIDGE (count card ~[FACT_NOTES]); ridges glow NE-SW across the path]
In May eighteen sixty-four, Sherman marched south from Chattanooga with nearly a hundred thousand men in three armies, under George Thomas, James McPherson and John Schofield. Facing him, at the town of Dalton, was the Confederate Army of Tennessee under Joseph Johnston, roughly half his strength, dug in behind a steep wall of rock called Rocky Face Ridge.

[MAP: move1-2 | Close on DALTON / ROCKY FACE RIDGE (option 2 glowing fronts): red line along the crest, guns on the gaps (MILL CREEK GAP), red belief card: "LET HIM ATTACK THE RIDGE"; then the railroad behind Johnston glows from Dalton south to RESACA (ring); blue supply line back to Chattanooga glows too (one thin line, red pulse on it: ONE TRACK)]
Everything between those two armies ran along one thin line: the Western and Atlantic Railroad, from Chattanooga to Atlanta. It fed Sherman's army. It also fed Johnston's. Johnston expected the Union army to throw itself at his ridge, and every few miles further south, the mountains and rivers offered him another wall just like it.

[MAP: move1-3 | Key move: THOMAS + SCHOFIELD press the front of the ridge (blue arrows, impacts, MG, shake: PIN); McPHERSON's counter slips away SOUTH-WEST behind the hills (dotted, hidden), through SNAKE CREEK GAP (glow, ruler ~15 MILES), out toward RESACA and the railroad (ring pulses); Sherman badge: caption THE ANVIL / THE HAMMER]
Sherman saw something different. He did not need to break the wall. He needed to get behind it, onto that railroad. So while Thomas and Schofield pressed hard against the front of Rocky Face Ridge, he sent McPherson's whole army on a hidden march to the south, through an unguarded pass called Snake Creek Gap, to come out at Resaca, on the railroad, behind Johnston's back.

[MAP: move1-4 | At RESACA: McPHERSON's arrow stops short of the railroad (red trench ring at Resaca, impacts), pulls back to the gap (stamp TOO CAUTIOUS, small); Johnston's red army slides south from Dalton to RESACA by night (K.night, arrows); quote card per FACT_NOTES ("Well, Mac, you have missed the great opportunity of your life" - as Sherman recalled it, MEMOIRS 1875); then the pattern repeats as fast steps (date tags): RESACA (blue crossing over the OOSTANAULA at LAY'S FERRY: red falls back) -> ALLATOONA (blue swings west to DALLAS / NEW HOPE CHURCH: red slides west) -> each red withdrawal = the red front band MOVES south (moveT)]
McPherson found Resaca held, and pulled back instead of cutting the line. Johnston slipped away south by night. Sherman later wrote that he told McPherson he had missed the great opportunity of his life. But the pattern was set. At Resaca, Sherman sent troops over the Oostanaula River around Johnston's flank, and Johnston fell back. At Allatoona Pass, Sherman left the railroad altogether and swung west through the woods, and Johnston had to fall back again. Ridge after ridge, Sherman never took the bait.

[MAP: move1-5 | PINE MOUNTAIN, 14 JUNE: blue guns fire -> arcs -> impact on the summit (shake); stamp POLK KILLED (Polk badge greys, small); then KENNESAW MOUNTAIN, 27 JUNE (option 2 fronts): blue lines charge straight up the slope at PIGEON HILL and CHEATHAM HILL (arrows, impacts, MG, shake), break against the red trenches; casualty strip: UNION ~3,000 · CONFEDERATE ~1,000 (NPS); Sherman badge: stamp HIS MISTAKE]
Then the rain came, and the armies ground to a halt in front of Kennesaw Mountain. On the twenty-seventh of June, Sherman lost patience and did the one thing he had avoided all campaign: he sent his men straight up the slopes, into Johnston's trenches. It was a mistake. In a few hours, about three thousand Union soldiers were killed or wounded, against roughly a thousand Confederates. Within days, Sherman was back to the method that worked, and swung around the mountain toward the Chattahoochee River.

[MAP: move1-6 | CHATTAHOOCHEE, 8-9 JULY: blue SCHOFIELD crosses upstream at SOPE CREEK (pontoon icon, surprise stamp), the river line collapses (red front moves back to ATLANTA); 17 JULY: Johnston badge greys, stamp RELIEVED; HOOD badge slams in (hit); three red attacks out of the city, each timed to its name (arrows, impacts, MG, shake): PEACHTREE CREEK 20 JULY, ATLANTA 22 JULY (stamp McPHERSON KILLED, small, his badge greys), EZRA CHURCH 28 JULY; each breaks against blue breastworks; counter red losses ticks (per FACT_NOTES)]
On the ninth of July, Schofield's men crossed the river upstream, almost unopposed, and the last natural barrier before Atlanta was gone. Jefferson Davis had seen enough. He replaced the careful Johnston with John Bell Hood, a fighter. Hood attacked at once, three times in eight days, at Peachtree Creek, east of Atlanta, and at Ezra Church. McPherson was killed in the fighting. But each time, Hood's men broke against Union breastworks, and Hood lost [FACT_NOTES: Hood's July losses] men he could not replace.

[MAP: move1-7 | AUGUST: siege look, blue lines curve around the north and east of ATLANTA, red forts ring the city; Union guns shell the city (arcs, impacts, shake); the two railroads into Atlanta from the south glow: the MACON & WESTERN (red, last open line); 25-26 AUG night (K.night): the blue counters vanish from the trenches one by one, a single blue corps left at the CHATTAHOOCHEE BRIDGE (XX CORPS); red belief card: "THE YANKEES ARE RETREATING?"; the blue mass swings in a wide arc around the WEST side of the city (big arrows, ruler ~25 MILES) toward ROUGH AND READY and JONESBORO]
By August, Hood was penned inside Atlanta's forts, too strong to storm. So Sherman did it one more time, on the biggest scale yet. On the night of the twenty-fifth of August, he pulled almost his entire army out of its trenches, left a single corps to guard the river crossing, and swung everyone else in a wide arc around the west side of the city. In Atlanta, some Confederates thought the Yankees were giving up and going home. They were heading for the last railroad into the city.

[MAP: move1-8 | JONESBORO (close, option 2 fronts), 31 AUG: red HARDEE attacks the blue line over the FLINT RIVER (impacts, MG, shake), thrown back; 1 SEPT: blue storms the red flank (arrows); blue troops tear up the MACON & WESTERN (rail-twist icons: SHERMAN'S NECKTIES caption); NIGHT 1 SEPT: zoom out: big red explosions in ATLANTA (impacts r 20+, shake, fire glow): caption HOOD BLOWS UP HIS AMMUNITION TRAINS (per FACT_NOTES: number of cars); red streams out south-east]
At Jonesboro, on the thirty-first of August, Hood's men attacked and were thrown back. The next day, the Union army broke their line and tore up the tracks, heating the rails over bonfires and twisting them around trees. Soldiers called them Sherman's neckties. That night, the sky over Atlanta lit up as Hood blew up his own ammunition trains and abandoned the city.

[MAP: move1-9 | 2 SEPT: blue counters march into ATLANTA (ring, stamp ATLANTA IS OURS); telegram card "SO ATLANTA IS OURS, AND FAIRLY WON." - SHERMAN, 3 SEPT 1864; zoom out to the theatre `south` (option 1, Sep 1864 territory): CASUALTY card: ATLANTA CAMPAIGN · UNION ~[FACT_NOTES] · CONFEDERATE ~[FACT_NOTES]; then 8 NOV: card LINCOLN RE-ELECTED · 212 ELECTORAL VOTES TO 21; method card line 1: HIT THE RAILROAD, NOT THE WALL]
On the second of September, Union troops marched into Atlanta. So Atlanta is ours, Sherman telegraphed, and fairly won. The campaign had cost each side some [FACT_NOTES] men. But in the North, the news changed everything. Two months later, Lincoln was re-elected by two hundred and twelve electoral votes to twenty-one. Sherman had not won by smashing the Confederate army. He had won by going around it, again and again, to the one thing it could not live without. Hit the railroad, not the wall.

But Atlanta was only the beginning. Breakdowns like this, move by move on the map, are exactly what subscribing keeps bringing you. Because what Sherman did next had no supply line at all.

## MOVE 2 — THREATEN TWO PLACES AT ONCE: THE MARCH TO THE SEA (~7:00 – ~12:30)

[MAP: move2-1 | Title card MOVE 2 · THE MARCH TO THE SEA · NOVEMBER - DECEMBER 1864. `corridor` wide (option 1, Oct 1864): red HOOD swings north-west around Atlanta onto the W&A RAILROAD (arrows, rails cut: red pulses), ALLATOONA 5 OCT (impacts, stamp HELD), then red turns west toward ALABAMA and TENNESSEE (big arrow); blue columns chase north (dotted), stop; quote card (FACT_NOTES verbatim): "WAR IS CRUELTY, AND YOU CANNOT REFINE IT" - SHERMAN TO THE MAYOR OF ATLANTA, 12 SEPT 1864 (small: he had ordered the civilians out of the city)]
Sherman ordered Atlanta's civilians to leave the city. When the mayor protested, he wrote back that war is cruelty, and you cannot refine it. In October, Hood struck back, not at Atlanta, but at the long railroad behind it, tearing up the track. Sherman chased him north for weeks. Then Hood turned west, toward Alabama and Tennessee, trying to drag Sherman back the way he had come.

[MAP: move2-2 | E.decide: dimmed map (theatre `south`): Sherman at ATLANTA, HOOD's red arrow toward TENNESSEE, the long blue supply line back to CHATTANOOGA flickering; WHAT WOULD YOU DO? A: CHASE HOOD BACK TO TENNESSEE · B: CUT LOOSE AND MARCH TO THE SEA; TELL US IN THE COMMENTS (3 ticks); on "He did both" B lights blue (A half-lit: THOMAS SENT TO NASHVILLE), hit; telegram card (FACT_NOTES verbatim) "...MAKE GEORGIA HOWL" - SHERMAN TO GRANT, OCT 1864]
Now stop for a moment, and put yourself in Sherman's place. Your enemy has slipped around you and is heading for Tennessee. Your army sits at the end of a railroad more than a hundred miles long, and he can cut it whenever he likes. Do you chase him all the way back to Tennessee, or do you let him go, cut loose from your supplies, and march the other way, to the sea? Tell us in the comments what you would have done. And then hear what Sherman did. He did both. He sent George Thomas back to Nashville to deal with Hood, and asked Grant to let him take the rest of the army across Georgia. He promised he could make Georgia howl.

[MAP: move2-3 | 12-15 NOV: telegraph line north of Atlanta snaps (spark, stamp WIRES CUT); the W&A railroad behind him burns (red-orange pulses, smoke); ATLANTA: depots, rail yards, shops burn (K.smoke columns, fire glow; caption: MILITARY TARGETS ORDERED · THE FIRE SPREAD); 15 NOV: blue army in four columns leaves the city south-east (counters with wagon icons): card 62,000 MEN · [FACT_NOTES] GUNS · ~20 DAYS' RATIONS]
Grant agreed. In mid-November, Sherman burned the railroad behind him and cut the telegraph to the North. His engineers wrecked Atlanta's depots, shops and rail yards, and the fires spread into much of the city. Then some sixty-two thousand men marched out, with about twenty days of food in their wagons and no way to get more, except from the land in front of them.

[MAP: move2-4 | Georgia `georgia` wide (option 1, Nov 1864): two blue wings fan out: RIGHT WING (HOWARD) south toward MACON (ring pulses, red defenders rush there), LEFT WING (SLOCUM) east toward AUGUSTA (ring pulses, red rush there); cavalry icons (KILPATRICK) feint at MACON; red belief cards over each city: "HE IS COMING HERE"; between them MILLEDGEVILLE (state capital) glows EMPTY; blue arrows converge into the middle: stamp MILLEDGEVILLE TAKEN (23 NOV); 1860 CENSUS card (small: county farm tables)]
And here was Sherman's trick. He split his army into two wings and pointed them at two different cities. The right wing headed south, toward Macon. The left wing marched east, toward Augusta. The Confederates had too few men to defend both, so they rushed what they had to each city, and waited. But Sherman wanted neither. His wings swung back into the middle and took the state capital, Milledgeville, without a fight. He had studied the census tables before he left, county by county, and he knew the farms along his route could feed his army.

[MAP: move2-5 | GRISWOLDVILLE, 22 NOV (close, option 2): red militia lines charge across an open field (arrows) at one blue brigade behind a rail barricade (MG, impacts, shake), break; casualty strip per FACT_NOTES (~650 vs ~62); small caption: OLD MEN AND BOYS (as Union soldiers described them)]
The only real battle on the way came near Macon, at Griswoldville. Georgia militia, many of them old men and teenage boys, charged a veteran Union brigade with repeating rifles behind a barricade of fence rails. They charged three times. They lost [FACT_NOTES] men; the Union brigade lost [FACT_NOTES]. Some of the Union soldiers who walked over the field afterwards wrote that they wished they had never seen it.

[MAP: move2-6 | `georgia` (option 1): the blue path widens into a band ~60 MILES WIDE (ruler), foragers fan out (small dots: BUMMERS), cotton gins and rail lines burn along it (smoke, rail-twist icons), red territory behind the band greys; a growing column of small figures follows the army (caption: THOUSANDS OF FREED PEOPLE FOLLOWED THE ARMY); EBENEZER CREEK, 9 DEC: pontoon bridge pulled up (stamp, small caption per FACT_NOTES); Lincoln badge: card (only if verified) "I KNOW WHAT HOLE HE WENT IN AT..."]
For a month, the army cut a path of destruction some sixty miles wide across Georgia. Foraging parties, the men called them bummers, stripped farms of food and animals, and burned cotton gins and railroads. Many homes were looted, against orders, and Georgians never forgot it. Thousands of enslaved people walked away from the plantations and followed the army to freedom. At Ebenezer Creek, one Union general pulled up his pontoon bridge before the refugees behind his column could cross, and many of them drowned. In the North, nobody knew where Sherman was.

[MAP: move2-7 | SAVANNAH close `mcallister` (option 2): red HARDEE ~10,000 behind flooded rice fields and causeways west of the city (water shimmer, red line); blue arrives 10 DEC; wagon icon: ration bar runs low (red); offshore: blue ship silhouettes (DAHLGREN's fleet) wait beyond the OGEECHEE mouth; FORT McALLISTER (red star fort) blocks the river (ring); Sherman badge on a RICE MILL roof across the river (eye icon)]
On the tenth of December, Sherman's army reached Savannah. Confederate general William Hardee held the city with about ten thousand men, behind flooded rice fields that could only be crossed on narrow causeways. Sherman's wagons were running low. A Union fleet with all the food he needed was waiting off the coast. But the river leading to it was blocked by an earthen fort: Fort McAllister.

[MAP: move2-8 | 13 DEC, ~4:45 PM: blue HAZEN's division (counter) storms the fort from the land side (arrows through abatis, small torpedo bursts in the ditch = impacts, shake), fort guns fire (impacts), clock ticks per FACT_NOTES (~15 MIN); stamp FORT McALLISTER TAKEN; blue ship silhouette steams up the OGEECHEE (supply line opens, glow); 20-21 DEC NIGHT (K.night): red HARDEE slips out over a pontoon bridge across the SAVANNAH RIVER into South Carolina (dotted arrows); 21 DEC: blue enters SAVANNAH (ring)]
On the thirteenth of December, Sherman watched from the roof of a rice mill across the river as one division under William Hazen charged the fort from the land. In about a quarter of an hour, it was over. That night Sherman was rowed out to the fleet, and his army had food again. A week later, Hardee slipped out of Savannah across a bridge of boats, and Union troops marched in.

[MAP: move2-9 | Theatre `south` (option 1, Dec 1864): the march line ATLANTA -> SAVANNAH glows (ruler ~[FACT_NOTES] MILES); telegram card "I BEG TO PRESENT YOU AS A CHRISTMAS GIFT THE CITY OF SAVANNAH..." (verbatim per FACT_NOTES) - SHERMAN TO LINCOLN, 22 DEC 1864; RESULT card: [FACT_NOTES: Union losses on the march] · SAVANNAH TAKEN · SHERMAN'S OWN ESTIMATE OF DAMAGE: $100,000,000; method card line 2: THREATEN TWO PLACES AT ONCE]
On the twenty-second of December, Sherman sent Lincoln a telegram presenting him with the city of Savannah as a Christmas gift. He had crossed the heart of Georgia, some [FACT_NOTES] miles, for the loss of [FACT_NOTES] men, and by his own estimate done a hundred million dollars of damage. Twice the Confederates had guessed where he was going, and twice they had guessed wrong. Threaten two places at once.

## MOVE 3 — GO WHERE THEY SAY NO ARMY CAN: THE CAROLINAS (~12:30 – ~17:00)

[MAP: move3-1 | Title card MOVE 3 · THE CAROLINAS · FEBRUARY - APRIL 1865. Carolinas `carolinas` wide (option 1, Jan 1865): SAVANNAH bottom, BENTONVILLE / RALEIGH top; rivers glow one after another across the path (SAVANNAH, SALKEHATCHIE, EDISTO, CONGAREE, PEE DEE, CAPE FEAR), flooded-swamp texture; red belief card: "NO ARMY CAN CROSS IN WINTER"; red rings at CHARLESTON and AUGUSTA; blue count card ~60,000]
In January eighteen sixty-five, Grant thought of bringing Sherman's army to Virginia by sea. Sherman asked to march there instead, by land, straight up through the Carolinas. Between him and Virginia lay river after river, and miles of swamps, flooded by the heaviest winter rains in years. The Confederates were sure that no army could cross them in winter.

[MAP: move3-2 | Two-place feint again: RIGHT WING toward CHARLESTON (ring pulses, red HARDEE stays), LEFT WING toward AUGUSTA (ring pulses, red stays); both wings swing NORTH into the swamps between them (arrows); RIVERS' BRIDGE, 3 FEB (close, option 2): blue lines wade through the SALKEHATCHIE swamp around the red flank (water ripples, impacts, MG, shake): stamp LINE BROKEN]
Sherman used the same trick again. One wing threatened Charleston, the other Augusta, and the Confederates held on to both. Then both wings turned north, into the swamps between them. At the Salkehatchie River, Union soldiers waded for hours through icy water, sometimes up to their shoulders, holding their rifles and cartridge boxes above their heads, and came out on the flank of the Confederate line. It gave way.

[MAP: move3-3 | Corduroy beat: blue columns crawl north on roads that build themselves log by log (stripe pattern laid ahead of each column, axe icons, PIONEERS caption), wagon icons follow; counter MILES PER DAY per FACT_NOTES; Johnston badge: quote card per FACT_NOTES ("...NO SUCH ARMY IN EXISTENCE SINCE THE DAYS OF JULIUS CAESAR" - JOHNSTON, AS LATER REPORTED, attribution per FACT_NOTES)]
Where the roads disappeared under water, Sherman's men built new ones. Whole regiments cut down pine trees and laid them side by side across the mud, mile after mile, so the wagons and guns could follow. Joseph Johnston, who would soon face Sherman again, reportedly said later that when he heard how fast they were moving through those swamps, he decided there had been no such army since the days of Julius Caesar.

[MAP: move3-4 | 17 FEB: COLUMBIA (state capital) ring, blue enters; NIGHT 17-18 FEB (K.night): fire spreads through the city (fire glow, smoke columns, wind arrows): caption WHO STARTED THE FIRE IS STILL DISPUTED (small: cotton bales · wind · drunken soldiers); meanwhile CHARLESTON (coast): red HARDEE pulls out (arrows north), stamp CHARLESTON EVACUATED (18 FEB) - NOT A SHOT AT ITS FORTS]
On the seventeenth of February, Sherman's army took Columbia, the capital of South Carolina. That night, a third of the city burned. Who started the fire is still argued over: burning cotton bales left in the streets, a strong wind, and drunken Union soldiers all played a part. The same day, cut off from the interior, the Confederates gave up Charleston, the city where the war had begun, without a fight.

[MAP: move3-5 | North Carolina: blue takes FAYETTEVILLE (11 MAR, arsenal burns: smoke); Johnston badge returns (stamp BACK IN COMMAND, 22 FEB); AVERASBORO 16 MAR (impacts, red delays the LEFT WING); the two blue wings drift apart on separate roads toward GOLDSBORO (gap widens, ruler, red pulse on the gap); red JOHNSTON gathers ~[FACT_NOTES] at BENTONVILLE (arrows converging, hidden in woods)]
In North Carolina, Johnston was back in command, and he saw his chance. Sherman's two wings were marching on separate roads, a day apart. If Johnston could catch one of them alone, he could destroy it before the other arrived. He gathered every man he could find, some [FACT_NOTES] of them, in the woods near a village called Bentonville.

[MAP: move3-6 | BENTONVILLE close `bentonville` (option 2 fronts), 19 MAR: red hook-shaped line in the woods; blue XIV CORPS (SLOCUM's left wing) marches into it (arrows); ~3 PM: red attack smashes the blue left (impacts, MG, shake, two blue counters fall back), blue line re-forms and holds (ring holds: attacks thrown back, count per FACT_NOTES); couriers gallop east (dotted) to Sherman; night: the RIGHT WING (HOWARD) marches through the dark to the field (K.night, arrows)]
On the nineteenth of March, Sherman's left wing walked straight into the trap. The Confederate attack drove part of the Union line back through the woods. But the veterans re-formed, and held, attack after attack, until dark. Slocum's couriers rode through the night, and Sherman turned his whole right wing around and marched it to the battlefield.

By the twenty-first, Johnston was almost surrounded, with a flooded creek and a single bridge behind him. One Union division broke through the swamp and got close to that bridge. Sherman called it back. He wanted no more big battles, with reinforcements a day's march away. He later admitted it may have been a mistake. That night, Johnston escaped.

A few weeks later, the news came that Lee had surrendered at Appomattox. On the seventeenth of April, at a small farmhouse near Durham's Station, called Bennett Place, Sherman met Johnston, and handed him a telegram: Lincoln had been assassinated. After nine days of talks, on the twenty-sixth of April, Johnston surrendered the [FACT_NOTES] soldiers left under his command in the Carolinas, Georgia and Florida, the largest surrender of the war. Go where they say no army can.

In less than a year, the man a newspaper had called insane took Atlanta, saved an election, and marched an army through the heart of the South. He fought few great battles, and lost the one where he went straight at the enemy. Southerners never forgave the destruction he left behind. Years later, he reportedly told a class of young cadets that war is all hell.
