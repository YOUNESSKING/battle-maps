Nearly a hundred thousand Union soldiers had fought toward it for four months, and the city still held. Then their general swung almost his whole army around it, onto its last railroad. Three years earlier, that same general had been in command in Kentucky. He told the Secretary of War he would need two hundred thousand men there, and the request leaked to the newspapers. He lost his command and was sent home. And one newspaper printed, for the whole country to read, that he was insane.

His name was William Tecumseh Sherman. In less than a year, he outmanoeuvred two Confederate commanders all the way to Atlanta, vanished into Georgia and came out on the sea, and then marched through swamps that looked impassable in the middle of winter. These are William T. Sherman's three greatest tactical moves.

## BACKGROUND (~0:55 – ~1:40)

[ARCHIVE: Sherman portrait (Brady / LoC, PD, 3D depth); headline date card "1820 / LANCASTER, OHIO"; headline date card "1861 / LOUISIANA" (designed); Barnard 1864 photo of Sherman on horseback at Federal Fort No. 7, Atlanta (LoC, PD); never a slideshow; no battle-flag art]
Sherman was born in Ohio in eighteen twenty, and named after the great Shawnee leader Tecumseh. His family called him Cump. He went to West Point, left the army to run a bank in San Francisco, and when the war came, he was running a military school in Louisiana. When Louisiana left the Union, he resigned, and went north to fight.

[MAP: bg-1 | Theatre `south` (option 1, spring 1864 territory, the front at CHATTANOOGA); stamps per word: SHILOH 1862 (WOUNDED, small), VICKSBURG 1863, CHATTANOOGA 1863; Grant badge (real photo) beside Sherman's badge; MARCH 1864: Grant's badge slides EAST to Virginia (arrow), Sherman's badge takes the West: card COMMAND IN THE WEST; method card frame appears empty: SHERMAN'S METHOD]
After Kentucky, he came back under Ulysses Grant. He was wounded at Shiloh, and fought beside Grant at Vicksburg and Chattanooga, and the two men came to trust each other completely. In March eighteen sixty-four, Grant went east to take on Robert E. Lee, and left Sherman in charge of the war in the West. Their plan was simple: both of them would attack at the same time, so the Confederacy could not shift troops to stop either one.

## MOVE 1 — HIT THE RAILROAD, NOT THE WALL: THE ATLANTA CAMPAIGN (~1:40 – ~6:50)

[MAP: move1-1 | Title card MOVE 1 · THE ATLANTA CAMPAIGN · MAY - SEPTEMBER 1864. Corridor `corridor` wide (option 1, May 1864): CHATTANOOGA top, ATLANTA bottom; the WESTERN & ATLANTIC RAILROAD glows as one thin line between them (train icon running along it); blue SHERMAN ~99,000 (count card, ticks) in three counters: THOMAS (Army of the Cumberland), McPHERSON (Army of the Tennessee), SCHOFIELD (Army of the Ohio), badges; red JOHNSTON (badge) dug in at DALTON behind ROCKY FACE RIDGE (count card ~54,000, ABT); ridges glow NE-SW across the path]
In May eighteen sixty-four, Sherman marched south from Chattanooga with nearly a hundred thousand men in three armies, under George Thomas, James McPherson and John Schofield. Facing him, at the town of Dalton, was the Confederate Army of Tennessee under Joseph Johnston, little more than half as strong, dug in behind a steep wall of rock called Rocky Face Ridge.

[MAP: move1-2 | Close on DALTON / ROCKY FACE RIDGE (option 2 glowing fronts): red line along the crest, guns in MILL CREEK GAP; red belief card: LET THEM ATTACK THE RIDGE; then the railroad behind Johnston glows from Dalton south to RESACA (ring); the blue supply line back to Chattanooga glows too (red pulse: ONE TRACK)]
Everything in this campaign ran along one thin line: the Western and Atlantic Railroad, from Chattanooga to Atlanta. It fed Sherman's army. It also fed Johnston's. Johnston's men waited on the ridge for the Union army to throw itself at them. And every few miles further south, the mountains and rivers offered another wall just like it.

[MAP: move1-3 | Key move: THOMAS + SCHOFIELD press the front of the ridge (blue arrows, impacts, MG, shake; caption PIN); McPHERSON's counter slips away south-west behind the hills (dotted, hidden), through SNAKE CREEK GAP (glow), out toward RESACA and the railroad (ring pulses); Sherman badge; captions THE ANVIL / THE HAMMER]
Sherman saw something different. He did not need to break the wall. He needed to get behind it, onto that railroad. So while Thomas and Schofield pressed hard against the front of Rocky Face Ridge, he sent McPherson's whole army on a hidden march to the south, through an unguarded pass called Snake Creek Gap, to come out at Resaca, on the railroad, behind Johnston's back.

[MAP: move1-4 | At RESACA, 9 MAY: McPHERSON's arrow stops short of the railroad (red trench ring at Resaca, impacts), pulls back to the gap; quote card "SUCH AN OPPORTUNITY DOES NOT OCCUR TWICE IN A SINGLE LIFE" - SHERMAN, MEMOIRS (1875); Johnston's red army slides south from Dalton to RESACA by night (K.night, arrows); then the pattern repeats as fast steps with date tags: RESACA 15 MAY (blue division crosses the OOSTANAULA downstream: red falls back) -> ALLATOONA, late MAY (blue swings west to DALLAS / NEW HOPE CHURCH: red slides west) -> each red withdrawal = the red front band MOVES south (moveT)]
McPherson found Resaca held, and pulled back instead of cutting the line. Johnston slipped away south. Sherman later wrote that such an opportunity does not occur twice in a single life, though he admitted McPherson had followed his orders. But the pattern was set. At Resaca, Sherman sent a division across the Oostanaula River, around Johnston's flank, and Johnston fell back. At Allatoona Pass, Sherman left the railroad altogether and swung west through the woods, and Johnston had to fall back again. Ridge after ridge, Sherman never took the bait.

[MAP: move1-5 | PINE MOUNTAIN, 14 JUNE: blue guns fire -> arcs -> impact on the summit (shake); stamp POLK KILLED (Polk badge greys, small); rain streaks; KENNESAW MOUNTAIN, 27 JUNE (option 2 fronts): blue lines charge straight up the slopes at PIGEON HILL and CHEATHAM HILL (arrows, impacts, MG, shake), break against the red trenches; casualty strip: UNION ~3,000 · CONFEDERATE ~1,000 (ABT); Sherman badge: stamp HIS MISTAKE; quote card "FAILURE AS IT WAS, AND FOR WHICH I ASSUME THE ENTIRE RESPONSIBILITY" - SHERMAN'S REPORT]
In June, the rain came, and the armies ground to a halt in front of Kennesaw Mountain. On the twenty-seventh, Sherman lost patience and did the one thing he had avoided all campaign: he sent his men straight up the slopes, into Johnston's trenches. It was a mistake. By noon, about three thousand Union soldiers had been killed or wounded, against roughly a thousand Confederates. Sherman took the entire responsibility in his report. Within a week, he was back to the method that worked, and swung around the mountain.

[MAP: move1-6 | Early JULY: blue SCHOFIELD crosses the CHATTAHOOCHEE upstream (pontoon icon, surprise stamp), the river line collapses (red front moves back to ATLANTA); mid-JULY: Johnston badge greys, stamp RELIEVED; HOOD badge slams in (hit); three red attacks out of the city, each timed to its name (arrows, impacts, MG, shake): PEACHTREE CREEK 20 JULY, ATLANTA 22 JULY (stamp McPHERSON KILLED, small, his badge greys), EZRA CHURCH 28 JULY; each breaks against blue breastworks; counter red losses ticks to ~11,000 (ABT)]
In early July, Union troops crossed the Chattahoochee River upstream, and the last great barrier before Atlanta was gone. Jefferson Davis had seen enough. In the middle of July, he replaced the careful Johnston with John Bell Hood, a fighter. Hood attacked at once, three times in eight days: at Peachtree Creek, east of Atlanta, and at Ezra Church. McPherson was killed in the fighting. But each time, Hood's men broke against Union breastworks, and Hood lost about eleven thousand men he could not replace.

[MAP: move1-7 | AUGUST: siege look, blue lines curve around the north and east of ATLANTA, red forts ring the city; Union guns shell the city (arcs, impacts, shake); the railroads into Atlanta from the south glow: the MACON & WESTERN (red, last open line); 25 AUG night (K.night): the blue counters vanish from the trenches one by one, a single blue corps left at the CHATTAHOOCHEE BRIDGE (XX CORPS); the blue mass swings in a wide arc around the WEST side of the city (big arrows) toward JONESBORO (ring)]
By August, Hood was penned inside Atlanta's forts, too strong to storm. So Sherman did it one more time, on the biggest scale yet. On the night of the twenty-fifth of August, he pulled almost his entire army out of its trenches, left a single corps to guard the river crossing, and swung everyone else in a wide arc around the west side of the city, heading for the last railroad into Atlanta, at a town called Jonesboro.

[MAP: move1-8 | JONESBORO (close, option 2 fronts), 31 AUG: red HARDEE attacks the blue line (impacts, MG, shake), thrown back; red belief card at ATLANTA: HOOD PULLS A CORPS BACK TO DEFEND THE CITY (arrow north); 1 SEPT: blue storms the red line (arrows); blue troops tear up the MACON & WESTERN (rail-twist icons, caption SHERMAN'S NECKTIES); NIGHT 1 SEPT: zoom out: big red explosions in ATLANTA (impacts r 20+, shake, fire glow); caption HOOD BLOWS UP HIS AMMUNITION TRAINS; red streams out south-east]
Hood guessed wrong. He thought the blow would fall on Atlanta itself, and pulled troops back toward the city. At Jonesboro, on the thirty-first of August, his men attacked and were thrown back. The next day, the Union army broke their line and tore up the tracks, heating the rails over bonfires and twisting them around trees. Soldiers called them Sherman's neckties. That night, the sky over Atlanta lit up as Hood blew up his own ammunition trains and abandoned the city.

[MAP: move1-9 | 2 SEPT: blue counters march into ATLANTA (ring, stamp ATLANTA IS OURS); telegram card "SO ATLANTA IS OURS, AND FAIRLY WON." - SHERMAN, 3 SEPT 1864; zoom out to the theatre `south` (option 1, Sep 1864): CASUALTY card: ATLANTA CAMPAIGN · UNION 31,687-37,081 · CONFEDERATE ~35,000 (SHERMAN'S RETURNS / ABT); then 8 NOV: card LINCOLN RE-ELECTED · 212 ELECTORAL VOTES TO 21; method card line 1: HIT THE RAILROAD, NOT THE WALL]
On the second of September, Union troops marched into Atlanta. So Atlanta is ours, Sherman telegraphed, and fairly won. The campaign had cost more than thirty thousand men on each side. But in the North, the news changed everything. Two months later, Lincoln was re-elected by two hundred and twelve electoral votes to twenty-one. Sherman had not won by smashing the Confederate army. He had won by going around it, again and again, to the one thing it could not live without. Hit the railroad, not the wall.

But Atlanta was only the beginning. Breakdowns like this, move by move on the map, are exactly what subscribing keeps bringing you. Because what Sherman did next, he did with no supply line at all.

## MOVE 2 — THREATEN TWO PLACES AT ONCE: THE MARCH TO THE SEA (~6:50 – ~12:20)

[MAP: move2-1 | Title card MOVE 2 · THE MARCH TO THE SEA · NOVEMBER - DECEMBER 1864. `corridor` wide (option 1, Oct 1864): civilians' column leaving Atlanta south (small grey dots); quote card "WAR IS CRUELTY, AND YOU CANNOT REFINE IT" - SHERMAN TO THE MAYOR OF ATLANTA, 12 SEPT 1864; OCTOBER: red HOOD swings north-west around Atlanta onto the W&A RAILROAD (arrows, rails cut: red pulses), ALLATOONA 5 OCT (impacts, stamp REPULSED), then red turns west toward ALABAMA and TENNESSEE (big arrow); blue columns chase north (dotted), stop]
Sherman ordered Atlanta's civilians to leave the city. When the mayor protested, Sherman wrote back that war is cruelty, and you cannot refine it. In October, Hood struck back, not at Atlanta, but at the long railroad behind it, tearing up the track. Sherman chased him north for weeks. Then Hood turned west, toward Alabama and Tennessee, trying to drag Sherman back the way he had come.

[MAP: move2-2 | E.decide: dimmed map (theatre `south`): Sherman at ATLANTA, HOOD's red arrow toward TENNESSEE, the long blue supply line back to CHATTANOOGA flickering; WHAT WOULD YOU DO? A: CHASE HOOD BACK TO TENNESSEE · B: CUT LOOSE AND MARCH TO THE SEA; TELL US IN THE COMMENTS (3 ticks); on "He did both" B lights blue and A turns half-lit with THOMAS TO NASHVILLE (hit); telegram card "I CAN MAKE THE MARCH, AND MAKE GEORGIA HOWL." - SHERMAN TO GRANT, 9 OCT 1864]
Now stop for a moment, and put yourself in Sherman's place. Your enemy has slipped around you and is heading for Tennessee. Your army sits at the end of a single railroad more than a hundred miles long, and he can cut it whenever he likes. Do you chase him all the way back to Tennessee, or do you let him go, cut loose from your supplies, and march the other way, to the sea? Tell us in the comments what you would have done. And then hear what Sherman did. He did both. He sent George Thomas back to Nashville to deal with Hood, and asked Grant to let him take the rest of the army across Georgia. I can make the march, he wired, and make Georgia howl.

[MAP: move2-3 | NOV: telegraph line north of Atlanta snaps (spark, stamp WIRES CUT); the W&A railroad behind him burns (orange pulses, smoke); ATLANTA: depots, rail yards, shops burn (K.smoke columns, fire glow; caption MILITARY TARGETS ORDERED · THE FIRES SPREAD); 15 NOV: blue army in four columns leaves the city south-east (counters with wagon icons): card 62,204 MEN · 65 GUNS · ~20 DAYS' RATIONS (MEMOIRS)]
Grant agreed. In mid-November, Sherman wrecked the railroad behind him, and the telegraph wire to the North went dead. His engineers burned Atlanta's depots, workshops and rail yards, and the fires spread far beyond them. Then some sixty-two thousand men marched out of the city, with about twenty days of food in their wagons and no way to get more, except from the land in front of them.

[MAP: move2-4 | Georgia `georgia` wide (option 1, Nov 1864): two blue wings fan out: RIGHT WING (HOWARD badge) south toward MACON (ring pulses, red defenders rush there), LEFT WING (SLOCUM badge) east toward AUGUSTA (ring pulses, red rush there); cavalry icons (KILPATRICK) toward MACON; red belief cards over each city: HE IS COMING HERE; between them MILLEDGEVILLE (state capital) glows EMPTY; blue arrows converge into the middle: stamp MILLEDGEVILLE TAKEN (23 NOV); card 1860 CENSUS · EVERY COUNTY (small, MEMOIRS)]
And here was Sherman's trick. He split his army into two wings and pointed them at two different cities. The right wing headed south, toward Macon. The left wing marched east, toward Augusta. The Confederates did not have the men to defend both, so they held on to each city, and waited. But Sherman wanted neither. His wings swung back into the middle and took the state capital, Milledgeville, without a fight. Before he left, he had studied the census tables for every county in Georgia, and he knew the farms along his route could feed his army.

[MAP: move2-5 | GRISWOLDVILLE, 22 NOV (close, option 2): red militia lines charge across an open field (arrows) at one blue brigade behind a barricade of rails and logs (MG, impacts, shake), three times, break; casualty strip: CONFEDERATE 523-1,100 · UNION 94 (SCAIFE & BRAGG / ABT)]
The only real battle on the way came near Macon, at Griswoldville. Georgia militia charged a single veteran Union brigade dug in behind a barricade of fence rails and logs. They charged three times, and three times they were cut down. The militia lost at least five hundred men. The Union brigade lost fewer than a hundred.

[MAP: move2-6 | `georgia` (option 1): the blue path widens into a band ~60 MILES WIDE (ruler), foragers fan out (small dots: BUMMERS), cotton gins and rail lines burn along it (smoke, rail-twist icons); a growing column of small figures follows the army (caption FREED PEOPLE FOLLOW THE ARMY); EBENEZER CREEK, 9 DEC: pontoon bridge pulled up (stamp, small caption HUNDREDS DROWNED · GEORGIA HISTORICAL SOCIETY); Lincoln badge: card "WE ALL KNOW WHERE HE WENT IN AT, BUT I CAN'T TELL WHERE HE WILL COME OUT AT." - LINCOLN, 6 DEC 1864]
For a month, the army cut a path some sixty miles wide across Georgia. Foraging parties, the men called them bummers, stripped the farms of food and animals, and the army burned cotton gins and railroads. Many homes were looted, against orders, and Georgians never forgot it. Thousands of enslaved people walked away from the plantations and followed the army to freedom. At Ebenezer Creek, one Union general pulled up his pontoon bridge before the refugees behind his column could cross, and hundreds of them drowned. In the North, nobody knew where Sherman was. Lincoln told a crowd he knew where Sherman went in, but could not tell where he would come out.

[MAP: move2-7 | SAVANNAH close `mcallister` (option 2): red HARDEE ~10,000 behind flooded rice fields and causeways west of the city (water shimmer, red line); blue arrives 10 DEC; wagon icon: ration bar runs low (red); offshore: blue ship silhouettes (DAHLGREN's fleet) beyond the mouth of the OGEECHEE; FORT McALLISTER (red earthwork) blocks the river (ring)]
On the tenth of December, Sherman's army reached Savannah. The Confederate general William Hardee held the city with about ten thousand men, behind flooded rice fields that could only be crossed on narrow causeways. Sherman's wagons were running low. A Union fleet with all the food he needed was waiting off the coast. But the river that led to it was blocked by an earthen fort: Fort McAllister.

[MAP: move2-8 | 13 DEC, late afternoon: Sherman badge on the roof of a RICE MILL across the river (eye icon, ~3 MILES ruler); blue HAZEN's division (~4,000) storms the fort from the land side (arrows through abatis, small mine bursts in the sand = impacts, shake), fort guns fire (impacts), clock ticks 0 -> 15 MIN; stamp FORT McALLISTER TAKEN; a blue ship silhouette steams up the OGEECHEE (supply line opens, glow); 20 DEC NIGHT (K.night): red HARDEE slips out over a pontoon bridge across the SAVANNAH RIVER into South Carolina (dotted arrows); 21 DEC: blue enters SAVANNAH (ring)]
On the thirteenth of December, Sherman watched from the roof of a rice mill across the river as one division under William Hazen charged the fort from the land side, through buried mines and felled trees. It was over in about fifteen minutes. That night Sherman was rowed out to the fleet, and his army had food again. A week later, Hardee slipped out of Savannah across a bridge of boats, and Union troops marched in.

[MAP: move2-9 | Theatre `south` (option 1, Dec 1864): the march line ATLANTA -> SAVANNAH glows; telegram card "I BEG TO PRESENT YOU, AS A CHRISTMAS GIFT, THE CITY OF SAVANNAH, WITH 150 HEAVY GUNS ... AND ALSO ABOUT 25,000 BALES OF COTTON." - SHERMAN TO LINCOLN, 22 DEC 1864; RESULT card: SAVANNAH TAKEN · SHERMAN'S OWN ESTIMATE OF THE DAMAGE TO GEORGIA: $100,000,000; method card line 2: THREATEN TWO PLACES AT ONCE]
On the twenty-second of December, Sherman wired Lincoln, presenting him with the city of Savannah as a Christmas gift. In his report, he estimated the damage his army had done to Georgia at a hundred million dollars, and called most of it simple waste and destruction. Twice the Confederates had guessed where he was going, and twice they had guessed wrong. Threaten two places at once.

## MOVE 3 — GO WHERE THEY SAY NO ARMY CAN: THE CAROLINAS (~12:20 – ~16:50)

[MAP: move3-1 | Title card MOVE 3 · THE CAROLINAS · FEBRUARY - APRIL 1865. Carolinas `carolinas` wide (option 1, Jan 1865): SAVANNAH bottom, BENTONVILLE / RALEIGH top; rivers glow one after another across the path (SAVANNAH, SALKEHATCHIE, EDISTO, CONGAREE, PEE DEE, CAPE FEAR), flooded-swamp texture, caption A MILE WIDE (Salkehatchie); red rings at CHARLESTON and AUGUSTA; blue count card 60,079 MEN · 68 GUNS]
At first, Grant wanted Sherman's army brought to Virginia by sea. Sherman asked to go there by land instead, straight up through the Carolinas, with some sixty thousand men. Between him and Virginia lay river after river and miles of swamp, swollen by winter rain. At one crossing, Sherman found the river spread a full mile wide over its banks. He called it impassable.

[MAP: move3-2 | Two-place feint again: RIGHT WING toward CHARLESTON (ring pulses, red HARDEE stays), LEFT WING toward AUGUSTA (ring pulses, red stays); red belief cards: HE IS COMING HERE (x2); both wings swing NORTH into the swamps between them (arrows); RIVERS' BRIDGE, 3 FEB (close, option 2): blue lines wade through the SALKEHATCHIE swamp around the red flank (water ripples, impacts, MG, shake): stamp LINE BROKEN]
So Sherman played the same trick again. One wing threatened Charleston, the other Augusta, and the Confederates held on to both. Then both wings turned north, into the swamps between them. At Rivers' Bridge, on the Salkehatchie River, two Union divisions waded through the flooded swamp, with the water up to their shoulders, and came out on the flank of the Confederate line. It gave way.

[MAP: move3-3 | Corduroy beat: blue columns crawl north on roads that build themselves log by log (stripe pattern laid ahead of each column, axe icons, caption CORDUROY ROADS), wagon icons follow; Johnston badge: small caption NO SUCH ARMY SINCE THE DAYS OF JULIUS CAESAR - ATTRIBUTED TO J. E. JOHNSTON (no quote marks)]
Where the roads vanished under water, Sherman's men built new ones. Whole regiments cut down trees and split fence rails, and laid them side by side across the mud, mile after mile, so the wagons and the guns could follow. Sherman wrote that nearly every mile had to be paved this way. Joseph Johnston, who would soon face him again, is credited with saying there had been no such army since the days of Julius Caesar.

[MAP: move3-4 | 17 FEB: COLUMBIA (state capital) ring, blue enters; NIGHT 17-18 FEB (K.night): fire spreads through the city (fire glow, smoke columns, wind arrows): card ABOUT ONE-THIRD OF THE CITY BURNED (SC ENCYCLOPEDIA); caption WHO STARTED IT IS STILL DISPUTED · BURNING COTTON · HIGH WIND · DRUNKEN SOLDIERS; meanwhile CHARLESTON (coast): red HARDEE pulls out north (arrows), stamp CHARLESTON GIVEN UP · NOT A SHOT AT ITS FORTS]
On the seventeenth of February, Sherman's army took Columbia, the capital of South Carolina. That night, about a third of the city burned. Who started the fire is still argued over: cotton bales set alight in the streets, a howling wind, and drunken Union soldiers all played a part. The same night, cut off from the interior, the Confederates gave up Charleston, the city where the war had begun, without a fight.

[MAP: move3-5 | North Carolina (`carolinas`): blue takes FAYETTEVILLE (11 MAR, arsenal burns: smoke); Johnston badge returns (stamp BACK IN COMMAND); AVERASBORO 16 MAR (impacts, red delays the LEFT WING); the two blue wings drift apart on separate roads toward GOLDSBORO (gap widens, red pulse on the gap); red JOHNSTON gathers ~20,000 at BENTONVILLE (arrows converging, hidden in woods)]
In North Carolina, Johnston was back in command of what was left, and he saw his chance. Sherman's two wings were marching on separate roads, far apart. If Johnston could catch one of them alone, he might destroy it before the other came up. He gathered every man he could find, about twenty thousand of them, in the woods near a village called Bentonville.

On the nineteenth of March, Sherman's left wing walked straight into the trap. In the afternoon, the Confederate attack drove part of the Union line back through the woods. But the veterans re-formed and held, attack after attack, until dark. Slocum's couriers rode through the night, and Sherman turned his whole right wing around and marched it to the battlefield.

[MAP: move3-7 | 20-21 MAR: blue surrounds Johnston on three sides (arrows); 21 MAR, rain: MOWER's division plunges through the swamp into Johnston's rear toward the MILL CREEK BRIDGE (arrow, ring on the bridge, impacts), overruns Johnston's HQ (HQ icon flees); Sherman badge: stamp PULL BACK; Mower's arrow retreats; quote card "I THINK I MADE A MISTAKE THERE" - SHERMAN, MEMOIRS (1875); NIGHT 21 MAR: red crosses MILL CREEK and leaves (arrows); CASUALTY card: BENTONVILLE · UNION 1,527 · CONFEDERATE 2,606 (OFFICIAL RETURNS / ABT)]
By the twenty-first, Johnston was almost surrounded, with a flooded creek and a single bridge behind him. One Union division broke through the swamp, overran Johnston's headquarters, and got close to that bridge. Sherman called it back. He did not want a big battle before his reinforcements arrived. Years later, he wrote: I think I made a mistake there. That night, Johnston escaped.

[MAP: move3-8 | Theatre `south` / `carolinas` (option 1, Apr 1865): blue joins SCHOFIELD at GOLDSBORO (count card 88,948, 10 APR); 9 APR stamp in Virginia: LEE SURRENDERS AT APPOMATTOX · 28,231; 17 APR: Lincoln badge greys (stamp ASSASSINATED, small); BENNETT PLACE farmhouse icon near DURHAM'S STATION: Sherman + Johnston badges; 26 APR: stamp SURRENDER; count card 89,270 (CAROLINAS · GEORGIA · FLORIDA; NC HISTORIC SITES); method card line 3: GO WHERE THEY SAY NO ARMY CAN]
Three weeks later came the news that Lee had surrendered at Appomattox. On the seventeenth of April, at a small farmhouse near Durham's Station, called Bennett Place, Sherman met Johnston, and showed him a telegram he had kept secret all morning: Lincoln had been assassinated. Nine days later, Johnston surrendered all the troops left under his command, in the Carolinas, Georgia and Florida: eighty-nine thousand, two hundred and seventy men, more than three times as many as at Appomattox. Go where they say no army can.
