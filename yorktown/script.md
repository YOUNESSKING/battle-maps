# Yorktown 1781 — single-battle story — Script v1 (for owner approval)

Working title: **Britain Thought Yorktown Was a Safe Harbor. Washington Turned It Into a Trap.** (packaging: 3 titles + 2-3 thumbnails, PACKAGING_GUIDE 0b).
Format: the channel's first SINGLE-BATTLE story: hook + background + three parts (THE MARCH, THE SEA, THE SIEGE) + short ending. Each part = enemy plan first, "in other words", "But... instead", a cliffhanger (STYLE_LOCK 1d); the hook = 1a + 1c (stakes first, ending on YORKTOWN; number + promise by ~15 s; back into the danger; the general enters as the turn ~0:45 with ONE human detail; name slam + 3 snap cuts).

By seventeen eighty-one, the American Revolution was broke. Its army was unpaid, and close to mutiny. And it all came down to a small tobacco port on a river in Virginia. Yorktown.

[MAP: hook-2 | `peninsula` wide: red boxes dig in around the town with the river behind them, red ships in the river; BIG number card on "eight thousand": ABOUT 8,000 BRITISH (ticks); on "the harbor was a trap" blue boxes close a half circle from river to river, French ship boxes block the river mouth (hit + shake); stamp SURRENDERED on "surrendered"]
About eight thousand British soldiers dug in there, sure that the Royal Navy would keep them safe. Within three weeks of the first shot, the harbor was a trap, and they surrendered.

But that August, there was no trap. The American army was camped outside New York, watching the wrong city, more than four hundred miles to the north. To reach Virginia, it would have to slip away from the strongest British army in America without being stopped. And at sea, a French fleet would have to get there first, all the way from the Caribbean, before the Royal Navy did. The man who bet everything on it had not seen his own home in six years.

[MAP: hook-4 | Name slam GEORGE WASHINGTON (the "?" banner reveals Washington's portrait, BN.reveal, hit + shake); three snap cuts (~3 s each, whoosh + hit): THE MARCH (columns curling toward Staten Island, then wheeling south), THE SEA (two lines of ships off Cape Henry), THE SIEGE (a night assault on a redoubt); title card YORKTOWN 1781 (boom)]
His name was George Washington. To build the trap, he would have to fool the British in New York. He would need a battle at sea that he never saw. And he would end it with a night attack with unloaded muskets. This is how Yorktown was won.

The war was six years old. The British held New York, and in May seventeen eighty they had taken Charleston, with an entire American army under General Benjamin Lincoln. The British refused Lincoln's men the honors of war. No flags flying, no American march, only surrender. Remember that. Since then, Lord Cornwallis had fought his way north through the Carolinas and into Virginia.

## PART 1 — THE MARCH (~1:25 – ~6:00)

[MAP: p1-1 | `east` wide: title card PART 1 · THE MARCH; date scroll JUL 1781; Clinton banner (red) at NEW YORK after his name; in Virginia the red army (Cornwallis banner after his name) moving east down the peninsula, Lafayette's small blue force shadowing it; on "Yorktown" + "Gloucester Point" push in toward the York River; date scroll 1 AUG 1781 on "first of August"; red boxes settle on both banks]
The British plan was simple. In New York, the British commander-in-chief, Sir Henry Clinton, was sure the Americans were coming for him. So in July he told Cornwallis to stop chasing the Americans across Virginia, and to build a base on deep water, where the navy's big warships could shelter. Cornwallis chose Yorktown, on the York River, with Gloucester Point across the narrows. On the first of August, his army began to dig in.

[MAP: p1-2 | `peninsula` medium: the York River and its narrows; red ships move in and out of the river mouth freely (water streaks, living map); a thin sepia ink trace runs along the river on "supply line" and out to sea on "escape route"; stamp THE SEA on "the sea" (cream on dark, empty water)]
In other words, Cornwallis was not cornered. He had chosen the spot. As long as the Royal Navy ruled the sea, the river behind him was his supply line, and his escape route. Every British plan that summer rested on that one idea: the sea was theirs.

[MAP: p1-3 | `east` wide: blue camps at DOBBS FERRY on the Hudson (label on the word); the French army arrives from Rhode Island (blue arrow from Newport, French flag chip); Rochambeau banner (blue, French pennant) after his name; a thin line from the bottom edge (the Caribbean) on "de Grasse"; date scroll 14 AUG 1781 on "fourteenth of August"; the line bends into the Chesapeake on "the Chesapeake Bay"; card on "the middle of October": UNTIL MID-OCTOBER]
The Americans wanted New York too. But their French ally, the Comte de Rochambeau, had quietly asked the French admiral in the Caribbean, the Comte de Grasse, to bring his fleet north. On the fourteenth of August, at Dobbs Ferry on the Hudson, the answer arrived. De Grasse was coming, with more than twenty-five ships of the line and three thousand soldiers. But not to New York. To the Chesapeake Bay. And he could stay only until the middle of October.

[MAP: p1-4 | `east` wide, dim: E.decide card on "put yourself in Washington's place": A ATTACK NEW YORK / B MARCH SOUTH, TELL US IN THE COMMENTS with 3 ticks (options pulse on their words, camera keeps a slow push, arrows keep drawing); reveal B on p1-5 start]
Now stop for a moment, and put yourself in Washington's place. You have planned all summer to attack New York, and the enemy is right in front of you. The French fleet will be in Virginia, more than four hundred miles away, for a few weeks at most. Do you attack New York, or do you march your army south? Tell us in the comments what you would have done. And then listen to what Washington did.

[MAP: p1-5 | `east` medium on the Hudson: reveal B lights blue (hit); a small group of blue boxes stays in the Highlands; date scroll 20 AUG → 25 AUG on the spoken dates; blue columns cross at KING'S FERRY (timber ferry landing with flatboats); then the deception beats ON their words: brick ovens painted at CHATHAM (small smoke puffs), a row of flatboats on wheels rolling across New Jersey, the three columns curling east toward STATEN ISLAND; Clinton's banner at New York with a small "?" over it]
He did not attack New York. Instead, he turned the whole campaign around, and he did not tell his own army where it was going. Leaving part of his army to guard the Hudson, between the twentieth and the twenty-fifth of August, about seven thousand American and French soldiers crossed the river at King's Ferry. Then the deception began. French bakers built big brick ovens at Chatham, in New Jersey, as if the armies would stay for weeks. Thirty flatboats rolled across New Jersey on wheels, as if for a landing on Staten Island. And the columns marched toward New York harbor, as if to attack.

[MAP: p1-6 | `east` wide, the day-by-day clock: date scroll 29 AUG on "twenty-ninth", the columns wheel due south through TRENTON (dust behind the columns); 2 SEP on "second of September", PHILADELPHIA (label on the word), the blue column passes through; a red "!" over Clinton's banner on "only then"; 3-4 SEP the French column follows (French flag chip)]
On the twenty-ninth of August, they turned south. On the second of September, the Americans marched through Philadelphia, and only around then did Clinton understand where they were going. On the third and fourth, the French followed in their white coats, with their bands playing, while the crowds cheered.

[MAP: p1-7 | `east` medium on the head of the bay: the blue column at HEAD OF ELK (label on the word); a small cream card on "twenty thousand dollars": $20,000 IN SILVER; coins glint on the column (small gold sparkle on the boxes) on "a month's pay"]
But the American soldiers had not been paid in months, and some would not go farther without real money. The superintendent of finance, Robert Morris, had almost none. So he borrowed about twenty thousand dollars in silver from Rochambeau's war chest, and at Head of Elk, at the top of the Chesapeake, the Americans got a month's pay in coin. For many, it was the first hard money they had seen in years.

[MAP: p1-8 | `east` wide: date scroll 5 SEP on "fifth of September"; CHESTER label on the word; a messenger box rides in; a big blue ring pulses on the Chesapeake mouth where the French fleet sits (ship boxes, French flag); result card THE ARMY IS GONE · CLINTON STILL IN NEW YORK; method card THE TRAP · 1. FOOL NEW YORK; cliffhanger: on "that same morning" red ship boxes appear at the bay mouth from the north]
On the fifth of September, at Chester, near Philadelphia, a messenger reached Washington. De Grasse was in the Chesapeake. When Rochambeau's boat came up to the wharf, he found Washington standing there, waving his hat and a handkerchief, shouting the news. The army had slipped away, and Clinton was still waiting in New York. That was the first part of the trap: fool the enemy where he is looking. But that same morning, a British fleet had appeared off the mouth of the Chesapeake. It had come to take the sea back.

[MAP: sub | `chesapeake` wide: the two fleets approach each other off CAPE HENRY (ship boxes only, slow); E.subscribe button bottom-right, cursor click on the subscribing clause; NEXT: PART 2 · THE SEA label]
The march was only the beginning. So how do you shut the sea against the strongest navy in the world? That's the kind of question we answer here, and subscribing is how you catch the next one. Here's how the trap was closed.

## PART 2 — THE SEA (~6:00 – ~9:30)

[MAP: p2-1 | `chesapeake` wide: title card PART 2 · THE SEA; date scroll 5 SEP 1781; Graves banner (red, on a ship-mast pole by his flagship) after his name; nineteen red ship boxes in a column coming down from the north-east with the wind (small wind streaks, clear sky); Hood named later; on "a small French squadron" a ghost outline of a few French ships inside the bay]
The British plan was simple again. From New York, Admiral Thomas Graves had sailed south with nineteen ships of the line, to find the French and beat them. He expected only part of the French fleet. Destroy it, and the sea would be open again, and Cornwallis could be supplied, reinforced, or taken away.

[MAP: p2-2 | `chesapeake` wide: the ink trace from the open sea up the bay into the York River, a soft focus wash on the bay mouth; stamp HARBOR on "harbor" then PRISON on "prison" (one at a time)]
In other words, one afternoon at sea would decide whether Yorktown was a harbor or a prison.

[MAP: p2-3 | `chesapeake` medium on the bay mouth: CAPE HENRY + CAPE CHARLES labels on their words; de Grasse banner (blue, French pennant) on a ship-mast pole; twenty-eight blue ship boxes at anchor in LYNNHAVEN BAY; small boats near the shore (sailors ashore); on "cut his anchor cables" the French boxes start to move out one by one in a straggling line against the tide (water streaks against them)]
At about half past nine that morning, French lookouts saw the British sails. De Grasse had twenty-eight ships of the line at anchor just inside Cape Henry, and many of his sailors were ashore, ferrying troops. He did not wait. He cut his anchor cables, and his ships began to work their way out of the bay, one by one, in a ragged line.

[MAP: p2-4 | `chesapeake` medium: the red line comes down with the wind (wind streaks behind it) toward the straggling French; then the red line slows and straightens into a neat line, holding; time-of-day light warms toward afternoon; date scroll stays 5 SEP; by "twenty-four French ships" the French line is out in open water]
Graves had the wind behind him. He could have fallen on the French ships as they came out, a few at a time. But he did not. Instead, he did what the navy's fighting rules told him to do. He formed his ships into a proper line of battle, and waited. It took hours. By the time the fighting began, around four in the afternoon, twenty-four French ships were out in the open sea.

[MAP: p2-5 | `chesapeake` close on the two lines, angled toward each other: Graves's flagship marked; two small signal flags on its mast on "two signals" (caption KEEP IN LINE / CLOSE WITH THE ENEMY on the spoken words); the leading red ships turn in: ship guns fire (K.gun → arc → K.impact, visible, boom on impact), powder smoke; the red rear stays far back in line (Hood banner after his name); on "five British ships" five red boxes darken with smoke columns]
Then came the mistake. On his flagship, Graves flew two signals at once: keep in line, and close with the enemy. His leading ships turned in and fought at close range. His rear division, under Admiral Samuel Hood, stayed in line, far away, and hardly fought at all. For about two and a half hours, the front of the French line hammered the front of the British line. When darkness fell, five British ships were badly damaged.

[MAP: p2-6 | `chesapeake` wide, day-by-day: the two fleets drift south-east off the map edge (date scroll 6 SEP → 9 SEP); 10 SEP on "tenth of September": Barras banner after his name, eight blue ship boxes slip into the bay from the north with a gun symbol cargo (artillery box) on "heavy siege guns"; de Grasse's fleet comes back in; 11 SEP a red ship burns and sinks (HMS TERRIBLE label on the word); 13 SEP the red fleet turns north and leaves]
For the next few days, the two fleets drifted south, out of sight of land. Behind them, a second French squadron slipped into the bay: the Comte de Barras, from Rhode Island, with eight more ships of the line, and the heavy siege guns. De Grasse came back in to join him. On the eleventh of September, the British burned the wrecked HMS Terrible. On the thirteenth, Graves gave up and sailed back to New York.

[MAP: p2-7 | `chesapeake` wide: casualty card (both sides spoken): BRITISH ~330 KILLED & WOUNDED · FRENCH ~220; then the French ships sit across the bay mouth (blue line across the capes, first FORM 1 hatching: the bay mouth hatched blue for ~3 s on "shut"); method card THE TRAP · 2. CLOSE THE SEA]
The British lost about three hundred and thirty men killed and wounded, the French about two hundred and twenty. It was not a famous victory. Washington was not even there to see it. But the door to the sea was shut, and Cornwallis was on the wrong side of it. That was the second part of the trap: close the sea.

[MAP: p2-8 | `east` wide → `peninsula`: the blue army boards boats at HEAD OF ELK and ANNAPOLIS (labels on their words) and sails down the bay; Washington's banner rides south by road, stops at MOUNT VERNON (label + a soft warm glow on the word, ~3 s), date scroll 9 SEP → 14 SEP; arrives at WILLIAMSBURG on "fourteenth of September"; cliffhanger: the camera turns toward Yorktown, red works around the town]
On land, the armies were coming. Most of the troops sailed down the bay in boats. Washington rode. On the way, he stopped at Mount Vernon, his home, for the first time in six years, and stayed three nights. On the fourteenth of September he reached Williamsburg, twelve miles from Yorktown. Cornwallis was still waiting for his navy.

## PART 3 — THE SIEGE (~9:30 – ~16:30)

[MAP: p3-1 | `peninsula` → `yorktown`: title card PART 3 · THE SIEGE; date scroll 28 SEP 1781 on the spoken date; two blue columns march east from WILLIAMSBURG along two roads (dust); they spread into a half circle from the river above the town to the river below it: the French on the left (French flag), the Americans on the right; across the river at GLOUCESTER POINT (label on the word) blue cavalry + militia boxes close the neck; card ABOUT 17,000 on "seventeen thousand"; FORM 2 stripes once (~2.5 s) on "closed around the town" to show the whole ring]
On the twenty-eighth of September, about seventeen thousand French and American soldiers marched out of Williamsburg and closed around the town, in a half circle from the river above it to the river below. The French took the left, the Americans the right. Across the river, French cavalry and Virginia militia shut in Gloucester Point.

[MAP: p3-2 | `yorktown`: Cornwallis banner (red) at the town; a letter icon arrives by boat at night (date scroll 29 SEP); card on "about the fifth of October": RESCUE · ABOUT 5 OCTOBER; the red boxes in the outer works pull back to the inner line around the town (short moves); at dawn (light) blue boxes walk into the empty works; FORM 1 blue hatching on the abandoned ground ~3 s; on "Alexander Scammell" (if kept) a small cross]
Cornwallis's plan was now simply to hold on. On the twenty-ninth of September, a letter from Clinton reached him. More than twenty warships and about five thousand troops would sail from New York to rescue him, about the fifth of October. Remember that date. In other words, he only had to last a week or so. So that night, he pulled his men back from his outer works into a tighter line around the town. At dawn, the French and Americans walked into the empty earthworks without a fight.

[MAP: p3-3 | `yorktown` north (Gloucester side) or `peninsula`: date scroll 3 OCT; Tarleton banner (red) after his name rides north out of GLOUCESTER with red cavalry boxes; THE HOOK label on the word; blue hussars (Lauzun banner after his name) charge (cavalry slash boxes); battle mark lands on "went down"; red cavalry pulls back fast inside the Gloucester works]
Across the river, on the third of October, Banastre Tarleton, the most feared British cavalry commander in the South, rode out of Gloucester to gather food. French hussars under the Duc de Lauzun caught him at a place called the Hook. Tarleton's horse went down, and he barely escaped. After that, the British stayed inside their lines at Gloucester Point.

[MAP: p3-4 | `yorktown` NIGHT + rain (FACT_NOTES: rainy night 6-7 Oct): date scroll 6 OCT; blue engineer boxes (white GEAR) + infantry walk out onto the open plain south-east of the town; a long line of fresh-dug earth draws across the plain (the first parallel, ~2,000 yards) on "dug a trench"; cards on the spoken numbers: 2,000 YARDS · 600-800 YARDS FROM THE BRITISH; on the west a small blue noisy feint at the FUSILIERS' REDOUBT (musket flashes, no artillery); dawn light on "By dawn"]
On the night of the sixth of October, in the dark and the rain, about fifteen hundred men with picks and shovels walked out onto the open ground south-east of the town, with nearly three thousand more to guard them. On the far side of town, the French made a noisy attack to draw the British eye. By dawn the diggers had a trench almost two thousand yards long, only six to eight hundred yards from the British line. The British had not seen it coming.

[MAP: p3-5 | `yorktown` day: date scroll 9 OCT; artillery boxes (the kit gun box) along the trench, French on the left, American on the right; the French batteries fire first (K.gun → arc → K.impact on the British line, visible); Washington's banner at the American battery on "fired the first American gun" (one gun, flash, arc, impact in the town); then a steady visible barrage; 10 OCT at night red-hot shot: CHARON label on the word, a red ship box in the river catches fire (smoke column); British gun boxes go grey one by one]
On the ninth of October, the guns were ready. The French opened fire first, in the afternoon. Then, so the story goes, Washington himself fired the first American gun. Soon dozens of heavy guns and mortars were firing into Yorktown day and night. On the night of the tenth, red-hot French cannonballs set the British warship Charon on fire in the river. One by one, Cornwallis's guns fell silent.

[MAP: p3-6 | `yorktown` close on the south-east: date scroll 11 OCT; a second trench draws closer (the second parallel, ~300 yards from the British) from the left; it stops short of the river; REDOUBT 9 and REDOUBT 10 labels on their words, the two red earthworks close to the bluff; their muskets/guns flash at the new trench (visible); soft focus wash on the two redoubts]
On the night of the eleventh, the allies dug a second trench, only about three hundred yards from the British. But it could not reach the river. Two British earthworks stood in the way, close to the bluff: Redoubts Nine and Ten. Their fire swept the new trench. They had to be taken.

[MAP: p3-7 | `yorktown` close, NIGHT (clear): date scroll 14 OCT; three pairs of signal shells burst in the sky on "signal shells" (visual flashes, no boom); four hundred French (blue boxes, French flag chip) form up toward REDOUBT 9; four hundred Americans toward REDOUBT 10; Hamilton banner after his name at the American column; cards on the numbers: 400 · 400]
On the night of the fourteenth of October, at about eight o'clock, signal shells burst over the lines. Some four hundred French grenadiers and chasseurs went for Redoubt Nine. Some four hundred American light infantry went for Redoubt Ten, led by a young lieutenant colonel who had begged for the command. His name was Alexander Hamilton.

[MAP: p3-8 | `yorktown` close NIGHT: sharpened stakes (abatis) painted in front of both ditches; on "Hamilton did not wait" the American column goes straight over the stakes (no musket flashes from the Americans: bayonets only, a small caption UNLOADED MUSKETS on the spoken words); a small party (Laurens banner after his name) swings around to the rear; battle mark on "taken"; FORM 1 blue hatching on Redoubt 10; at Redoubt 9 the French halt at the stakes under red musket fire (flashes), axe marks, then storm in; battle mark; blue hatching on Redoubt 9; time cards UNDER 10 MINUTES / ABOUT HALF AN HOUR on the spoken words]
The usual way was to wait while axemen chopped a path through the sharpened stakes in front of the ditch. Hamilton did not wait. Instead, his men went straight over the stakes, with unloaded muskets, bayonets only, so that no nervous shot could give them away. John Laurens took a party around to the rear and captured the British commander. In under ten minutes, Redoubt Ten was taken. The French at Redoubt Nine waited for their axemen under heavy fire, then stormed the wall. It took them about half an hour, and cost them nearly a hundred men.

[MAP: p3-9 | `yorktown` close, before dawn: casualty card (both sides spoken): AMERICANS ~40 KILLED & WOUNDED · FRENCH ~90; the second trench draws on through both redoubts to the river (line extends on "joined"); guns move up into it (gun boxes); 15 OCT on the date scroll]
The Americans lost about forty men killed and wounded, the French about ninety. Before dawn, both redoubts had been joined to the new trench, and the allied guns could now fire into the British camp from close range.

[MAP: p3-10 | `yorktown` NIGHT 16 OCT: a red sortie box (~350) slips out at 4 AM toward the allied trench (caption 4 AM on "four in the morning"), small bayonet flashes, two gun boxes go grey (spiked), blue reserves push it back; on "firing again" the gun boxes light up again]
Cornwallis wrote to Clinton that his situation was now very critical. At four in the morning on the sixteenth, he sent about three hundred and fifty men to spike the allied guns. They spiked several, and were driven back. Within hours, the guns were firing again.

[MAP: p3-11 | `yorktown` NIGHT with the STORM (FACT_NOTES: violent storm of wind and rain, ~midnight 16-17 Oct): red boat boxes cross the narrows from YORKTOWN to GLOUCESTER POINT (first wave lands); then wind + driving rain sweep down the river (weather.js), the boats in mid-river are pushed downstream and scatter; the escape arrow breaks; at first light the boats come back across]
That night he tried one last thing. He would ferry his army across the river to Gloucester Point, break through the French and the militia there, and march north. The first boats crossed. Then, around midnight, a violent storm of wind and rain swept down the river and scattered the boats. By morning, the escape was over.

[MAP: p3-12 | `yorktown` close on the British line, day 17 OCT, guns firing (visible barrage on the town); date scroll 17 OCT; a lone red figure-sized box on the parapet (drummer) on "a drummer"; a small white flag beside him; on "The guns stopped" all firing stops, smoke drifts; card on "Saratoga": 4 YEARS AFTER SARATOGA]
On the morning of the seventeenth of October, four years to the day after the British surrender at Saratoga, a drummer in a red coat climbed onto the British wall and beat for a parley. Nobody could hear him over the guns. But everyone could see him, and an officer beside him waving a white handkerchief. The guns stopped.

[MAP: p3-13 | `yorktown` wide: date scroll 18 OCT; MOORE HOUSE (painted house, label on the word) behind the allied lines; 19 OCT on the spoken date: the red army marches out along the road south between two long lines (French line on one side with the French flag, American line on the other with the 13-star flag), to SURRENDER FIELD (label on the word); banners: Cornwallis's banner stays in the town (greyed, small caption "ILL" not used: no unspoken words), O'Hara named only; the sword beat on the words: Rochambeau's banner → Washington's banner → Lincoln's banner (Lincoln banner appears on his name); result card (both spoken): OVER 7,000 SOLDIERS · ABOUT 800 SAILORS SURRENDER]
On the eighteenth, officers from both sides met at the Moore House to settle the terms. Washington refused the British the honors of war. They would march out with their flags cased, just as the Americans had at Charleston. At two in the afternoon of the nineteenth of October, the British marched out between two long lines, the French on one side of the road and the Americans on the other. Cornwallis did not come. He said he was ill. His deputy, General Charles O'Hara, offered the sword to Rochambeau. Rochambeau pointed to Washington. Washington pointed to his own deputy: Benjamin Lincoln, the general who had surrendered at Charleston. Lincoln took the sword, and handed it back. More than seven thousand British and German soldiers laid down their arms, and some eight hundred sailors with them.

[MAP: p3-14 | `east` wide: a red fleet box leaves NEW YORK on "the nineteenth" (date scroll 19 OCT), sails south, arrives off the capes on "twenty-fourth" (date scroll 24 OCT), finds the blue French line across the bay mouth, turns back; card on "Five days too late": 5 DAYS TOO LATE; method card THE TRAP · 3. CLOSE THE LAND]
And the rescue? Clinton's fleet did not sail on the fifth of October. It sailed on the nineteenth, the day of the surrender, and reached the Chesapeake on the twenty-fourth. Five days too late. It turned back to New York. The last part of the trap had been the simplest: once the sea was shut, close the land.

## ENDING (~16:30 – ~17:15)

[MAP: end-1 | `east` wide: FORM 2 stripes (~2.5 s) over the coast as of late 1781 (British still in New York, Charleston, Savannah); calm map; small caption only on spoken words]
When the news reached London a month later, the prime minister, Lord North, is said to have cried out, "Oh God! It is all over!" It was not quite over. The peace treaty came almost two years later. But Britain never sent another army to win the war in America. Washington had seen what the British could not: their safe harbor was only as safe as the sea, and for a few weeks in seventeen eighty-one, the sea belonged to France.
