Fact-check notes (corrections to the Gemini research, checked against the Wikipedia article "Battle of Goose Green" and its cited sources: Middlebrook, Fitz-Gibbon, Adkin, Freedman):
- The battle ran 28-29 May 1982 (not 28-30). The attack began 03:35 on 28 May; Jones was killed on the morning of 28 May; the surrender was the morning of 29 May (Argentine Army Day).
- The BBC World Service report came on 27 May, the day before the attack.
- MILAN was used by Support Company to break Boca House / Boca Hill (with B Company), not at Darwin Hill. Darwin Hill fell to A Company (Maj. Farrar-Hockley), reported taken 13:13 local.
- The Pucará shot DOWN a British Scout helicopter (Lt Richard Nunn killed) that was flying to evacuate Jones; it was not driven off.
- Estévez was a 2nd Lieutenant of C Company, 25th Regiment; he died on Darwin Ridge. Jones was reportedly shot by Corporal Osvaldo Olmos.
- The ultimatum went by CB radio via farm manager Eric Goss, then two Argentine POWs carried the written terms after midnight. The terms threatened to bombard the settlements and held the Argentine commander responsible for civilians. Paraphrased in the script, not quoted.
- 2 Para was short of ammunition, water and food; Keeble also got reinforcements overnight (J Company 42 Commando, more guns and mortars). The script says "short", not "out".
- Casualties: UK 18 killed, ~64 wounded; Argentina 45-55 killed, 961 prisoners (some accounts say more).
- Not used: the "sodding politician" quote (legend) and the Daniel Morgan cross-reference (not supported).

## HOOK (0:00 – ~1:20)

[ARCHIVE: 1980s radio set / BBC World Service broadcast still, slow push-in; low static SFX]
On the twenty-seventh of May, nineteen eighty-two, a group of British paratroopers sat around a radio in a farmhouse on the Falkland Islands, eight thousand miles from home. They were listening to the BBC World Service. And then they heard the newsreader announce, to the entire world, that a parachute battalion was poised to attack Darwin and Goose Green. It was their battalion. It was their target. And it was supposed to be a secret.

[MAP: hook-falklands | South Atlantic: Argentina coast, Falkland Islands; East Falkland highlighted; San Carlos landing arrow; red Argentine garrison markers at Stanley and Goose Green]
Weeks earlier, Argentina had invaded the Falklands, a British territory in the South Atlantic. Britain had sent a task force across the ocean to take them back. The landings at San Carlos had succeeded, but the fleet was paying a terrible price. The destroyer Coventry and the container ship Atlantic Conveyor had just been sunk, and with the Conveyor went most of the heavy helicopters the army had been counting on.

[ARCHIVE: Downing Street / House of Commons exterior 1982; newspaper front pages]
In London, the government needed a victory, and it needed one quickly. So the nearest battalion was ordered south, to attack the Argentine garrison on a narrow strip of land called the Darwin isthmus.

[MAP: hook-isthmus | Close terrain map of the Darwin isthmus: Camilla Creek House, Burntside House, Darwin Hill, Boca House, Darwin, Goose Green, airfield; blue 2 PARA counter north, red counters in lines across the isthmus: TASK FORCE MERCEDES ~1,000]
That battalion was the Second Battalion of the Parachute Regiment, known as Two Para. Around five hundred fighting men, with just three light guns in support. Waiting for them were around a thousand Argentine soldiers, dug in across open, treeless ground, with more artillery, anti-aircraft guns, and ground-attack aircraft on call. And now, thanks to the BBC, they knew exactly who was coming.

[MAP: hook-bio | Photo cut-out of Lt Col H. Jones + bio card: LT. COL. HERBERT 'H' JONES · 1940 – 1982 · COMMANDING OFFICER, 2 PARA]
Two Para's commander was Lieutenant Colonel Herbert Jones, known to everyone simply as "H". He was forty-two years old, fiercely driven, and furious. He told a reporter he would sue the BBC and the War Cabinet. Then he went back to planning the attack anyway.

[MAP: hook-bio-2 | Photo cut-out of Major Chris Keeble + bio card: MAJOR CHRIS KEEBLE · SECOND IN COMMAND]
By the end of the next day, H Jones would be dead, and his quiet second in command, Major Chris Keeble, would be leading a battalion that was cold, exhausted, and almost out of ammunition. And yet, within thirty-six hours, more than nine hundred Argentine soldiers would lay down their weapons in front of them. So how did an outnumbered battalion win the first great land battle of the Falklands War? Let's take a closer look at Two Para's three greatest tactical moves at Goose Green.

## MOVE 1 — THE NIGHT ASSAULT: OWNING THE DARK (~1:20 – ~5:40)

[MAP: move1-1 | Title card MOVE 1 · THE NIGHT ASSAULT · 28 MAY 1982. Route march from San Carlos to Camilla Creek House, dashed blue line; label: 13 MILES ON FOOT]
With most of the heavy helicopters gone down with the Atlantic Conveyor, Two Para had to walk. They marched thirteen miles across wet peat from San Carlos to an empty farmhouse called Camilla Creek House, carrying everything they needed on their backs. From there, the Darwin isthmus stretched away to the south.

[MAP: move1-2 | Camera pushes down the isthmus: narrow neck of land, water on both sides; labels GORSE LINE, DARWIN HILL, BOCA HOUSE; red defensive lines across the isthmus]
The ground ahead was a gift to any defender. The isthmus is only a few miles long and in places little more than a mile wide, with water on both sides, so there is no way around. There are no trees, no walls, and almost no cover. Just rolling, boggy grass, a few gullies, and thick lines of gorse bushes. Across this neck of land, the Argentine commander had built his defences in layers.

[MAP: move1-3 | Argentine commander plaque: LT. COL. ÍTALO PIAGGI · TASK FORCE MERCEDES; counters: 12th REGIMENT, C COY 25th REGIMENT, AIR FORCE AA GUNS; forward screen, main line along the gorse from Darwin Hill to Boca House]
That commander was Lieutenant Colonel Ítalo Pee-AH-jee, at the head of a force called Task Force Mercedes. He placed a screen of outposts forward, and his main line along a gorse hedge that ran right across the isthmus, from Darwin Hill in the east to Boca House in the west. Behind it lay the settlement of Goose Green and its airfield, where anti-aircraft guns could also fire straight along the ground.

[MAP: move1-4 | Speech bubble from the Argentine line: "THEY WILL COME IN DAYLIGHT"; sun icon over the open ground; kill-zone shading]
Pee-AH-jee's position rested on a simple idea. Any attacker would have to cross open ground in front of prepared trenches, heavy machine guns and artillery. In daylight, that ground was a killing field. And after the BBC broadcast, he had every reason to keep his men alert and waiting.

[MAP: move1-5 | Night falls over the map: darkness overlay, stars; 2 PARA counters split into A, B, C, D companies; plaque: NIGHT = BRITISH ADVANTAGE]
But H Jones saw something different. Two Para was one of the best-trained night-fighting units in the British army. Many of the defenders were young conscripts, cold and poorly supplied after weeks in the peat. In the dark, the defender's artillery observers could see nothing, his machine gunners could not pick out targets, and his numbers mattered far less. Jones planned to fight the first part of the battle entirely at night: a six-phase attack, company leapfrogging past company, to break through the forward positions before the sun came up.

[MAP: move1-6 | Offshore: frigate icon HMS ARROW west of the isthmus; muzzle flashes; artillery arcs from the three guns at Camilla Creek; time stamp 03:35]
At three thirty-five in the morning of the twenty-eighth of May, the frigate HMS Arrow opened fire from offshore with her four-and-a-half-inch gun. The three British guns at Camilla Creek joined in. Under that fire, A Company, on the left, moved off toward its first objective, a farmhouse called Burntside House.

[MAP: move1-7 | A Company arrow to BURNTSIDE HOUSE on the east side; B Company arrow down the west side toward BURNTSIDE HILL; small red outposts fall back]
A Company cleared Burntside House, and B Company pushed down the western side of the isthmus, along the shore. In the darkness, the paratroopers went trench by trench, clearing positions with grenades and rifles at close range. Some Argentine outposts fought and some fell back, but one by one, the forward positions gave way.

[MAP: move1-8 | D Company arrow passes through the centre; forward red counters grey out; A and B continue south; the blue front line advances down the isthmus]
Then D Company leapfrogged through the centre. Behind them, the mortar crews fired so much that the recoil drove their baseplates deep into the soft peat, and the men had to dig them out again and again. Through the night, the three light guns fired hundreds of rounds, until by dawn they were nearly empty.

[MAP: move1-9 | Dawn gradient sweeps across the map; frigate icon sails away with a 'GUN FAULT' tag; blue counters halted in the open short of the gorse line at DARWIN HILL and BOCA HOUSE]
By first light, the plan had worked, up to a point. Two Para had advanced several miles down the isthmus and overrun the forward screen. But the attack was hours behind schedule. HMS Arrow, troubled by problems with her gun, had to leave before dawn to be clear of air attack. And as the sun came up, A and B Companies found themselves in the open, right in front of the main Argentine line. The darkness that had protected them was gone.

[MAP: move1-10 | Method card appears, first line lit: OWN THE DARK]
This was the first part of Two Para's method. Own the dark. When you are outnumbered and outgunned, choose the conditions in which the enemy's strengths disappear. At night, the defender's artillery and numbers counted for very little, and the attacker's training counted for everything. The trouble would come when the night ran out.

## MOVE 2 — DARWIN HILL AND BOCA HOUSE: MATCHING THE WEAPON TO THE WALL (~5:40 – ~11:00)

[MAP: move2-1 | Title card MOVE 2 · DARWIN HILL · MORNING, 28 MAY. Close zoom on Darwin Hill: gorse gully at the bottom, Argentine trenches on the slope above, interlocking fire arcs]
Morning, the twenty-eighth of May. At the foot of Darwin Hill, A Company, around a hundred men under Major Dair Farrar-Hockley, lay pinned down in a gorse-filled gully. Above them, the Argentine trenches looked straight down the slope. Heavy machine guns, rifles and snipers covered every yard of open ground. Anyone who stood up was hit.

[MAP: move2-2 | Argentine plaque: 2nd LT. ROBERTO ESTÉVEZ · 1st PLATOON, C COY, 25th REGIMENT; a counter moves north onto the ridge; artillery arcs from Goose Green land around the gully]
The defenders here were no pushover. Among them was a platoon led by Second Lieutenant Roberto Es-TEH-vez, which had moved up in the dark to reinforce the ridge. Wounded again and again, Es-TEH-vez kept crawling between the trenches, directing artillery fire onto the British, until he was killed. His men held.

[MAP: move2-3 | West side: B Company pinned in front of BOCA HOUSE on the open slope; red fire arcs; caption: BOTH ATTACKS STOPPED]
On the western side of the isthmus, B Company was stuck too, in front of the Argentine positions at Boca House. Two Para's whole attack had ground to a halt, in daylight, in the open, exactly where the defender wanted it.

[MAP: move2-4 | Speech bubble from the ridge: "THEY CANNOT CROSS THIS GROUND"; British counters flicker under fire]
From the Argentine trenches, the battle now looked winnable. The British had no naval gun, their artillery was nearly out of shells, and the weather was keeping most of their jets away. The paratroopers could not go forward, and every hour they spent lying in the gorse, they were losing men.

[MAP: move2-5 | Blue HQ marker (JONES) runs from the rear to A Company's gully; small group of counters follows; red arrow toward a single trench]
H Jones could not stand it. He left his tactical headquarters and ran forward to A Company's position to get the attack moving himself. He first led a group of men up a small gully in a charge that failed; his adjutant and two others were killed following him. Then Jones ran along the base of the ridge, checked his submachine gun, and charged up the slope alone, toward an Argentine trench.

[MAP: move2-6 | Freeze on the trench; the JONES marker turns grey; a radio waveform graphic: "SUNRAY IS DOWN"]
He was hit from a second trench he had not seen, fell, got up, and was hit again. He died on the slope a few yards short of his objective. Over the radio came the message every battalion dreads: Sunray, the commanding officer, is down. A British helicopter sent to evacuate him was shot down by an Argentine Pucará ground-attack aircraft, and its pilot was killed.

[ARCHIVE: photo of H. Jones; Victoria Cross medal]
H Jones was awarded the Victoria Cross, Britain's highest award for valour. But many historians, including former paratroopers, see his charge as immense personal courage rather than good command. For a crucial hour, the battalion commander was fighting as a rifleman, and the rest of the battle had no one directing it.

[MAP: move2-7 | Command passes: plaque swap JONES → MAJOR CHRIS KEEBLE; a bird's-eye camera pull-back shows the whole isthmus]
Command passed to Major Chris Keeble. And Keeble looked at the problem very differently.

[MAP: move2-8 | Key insight card: TRENCHES BUILT TO STOP BULLETS · NOT MISSILES · NOT MASSED MORTARS; mortar arcs rain on the ridge]
Keeble saw that you could not beat these trenches by throwing more men at them. The paratroopers were brave enough, but rifles and courage were the wrong tools against machine guns dug into the peat. What cracked a trench was concentrated firepower, brought to bear from out of the enemy's reach. He let his company commanders run their own fights, and put his effort into getting heavier weapons to the points that mattered.

[MAP: move2-9 | A Company: mortar arcs hammer the ridge; blue arrows with LAW rockets and grenades work up the slope trench by trench; ridge counters grey out; time stamp 13:13 DARWIN HILL TAKEN]
On Darwin Hill, the mortars kept up a relentless barrage, eventually firing more than a thousand bombs. A Company worked up the slope one trench at a time, knocking out positions with sixty-six millimetre rocket launchers and grenades. With their officers dead or wounded, the surviving defenders on the ridge finally raised a white T-shirt tied to a rifle. At a quarter past one in the afternoon, A Company reported Darwin Hill taken.

[MAP: move2-10 | West side: Support Company MILAN teams on the high ground west of Boca House; straight missile lines with small flares strike the red trenches; B Company arrow sweeps onto BOCA HILL; time stamp 13:47]
On the other side of the isthmus, Keeble brought up the MILAN teams. MILAN was a wire-guided missile built to destroy tanks. There were no tanks at Boca House. But from long range, beyond the reach of the Argentine rifles, the crews guided their missiles straight into the trenches and firing positions. Position after position went silent. B Company swept forward, and Boca Hill fell a little over half an hour after Darwin Hill.

[MAP: move2-11 | The gorse line across the isthmus turns grey from end to end; blue arrows push south toward the airfield and Goose Green]
The main Argentine line across the isthmus, which had stopped Two Para cold all morning, was broken from end to end. The way south to the airfield and to Goose Green itself was open.

[MAP: move2-12 | Method card, second line lit: MATCH THE WEAPON TO THE WALL]
This was the second part of the method. Match the weapon to the wall. When the attack stalls, bravery alone will not restart it. Find the weapon that does what your riflemen cannot, even if it was built for a completely different war, and bring it to exactly the point where the enemy is strongest. Own the dark. Match the weapon to the wall.

## MOVE 3 — THE GOOSE GREEN BLUFF: ATTACKING THE MIND (~11:00 – ~15:00)

[MAP: move3-1 | Title card MOVE 3 · GOOSE GREEN · 28–29 MAY. Zoom to the south: GOOSE GREEN settlement, AIRFIELD, SCHOOLHOUSE, DAIRY; blue companies close in from north and west]
Afternoon, the twenty-eighth of May. C and D Companies pushed on toward the airfield and the settlement of Goose Green. The fighting was fierce and confused. Anti-aircraft guns fired flat across the open ground into the advancing paratroopers, the schoolhouse near Darwin burned, and Argentine jets and Pucarás attacked the forward companies. Two of the aircraft were shot down.

[MAP: move3-2 | Evening: Argentine helicopters icon lands southwest of Goose Green; red counter REINFORCEMENTS ~ COMBAT TEAM SOLARI; blue companies scattered around the settlement]
By nightfall, Two Para had surrounded Goose Green, but they had not taken it. And just after dark, Argentine helicopters flew a fresh company of reinforcements into the area. Inside the settlement, the garrison was still around a thousand strong.

[MAP: move3-3 | Blue counters with status tags: NO WATER · ONE RATION PER TWO MEN · LOW AMMUNITION · 16+ KILLED; a thermometer icon: FREEZING]
Keeble's battalion was in a terrible state. The men had been fighting for more than fifteen hours. They were soaked, freezing, and exhausted. There was no water, food was down to one ration pack for every two men, and ammunition was running low. Some companies could not even be reached. Keeble fully expected a counter-attack at dawn.

[MAP: move3-4 | Goose Green settlement zoom: COMMUNITY HALL highlighted; caption: 114 CIVILIANS LOCKED INSIDE]
And there was another problem. Inside Goose Green, locked in the community hall, were one hundred and fourteen civilians, the families of the settlement, held there by the Argentine garrison for almost a month. A full assault with artillery and aircraft on the settlement might kill them.

[MAP: move3-5 | Speech bubble from Goose Green: "A BRIGADE IS OUTSIDE"; oversized ghost blue counters ring the settlement (what Piaggi imagined)]
Inside the settlement, Pee-AH-jee's view was very different. He had watched the British break every line he had built. He had seen Harrier jets strike his positions that afternoon. He believed he was surrounded by a far larger, well-supplied force, one that could sit out of range and destroy the settlement at will. And his calls to his own command had brought him no rescue he could count on.

[MAP: move3-6 | Keeble plaque; key insight card: HE CANNOT SEE MY EMPTY POUCHES]
Keeble saw that the decisive weapon was no longer rifles. It was the other man's mind. Pee-AH-jee could not see how exhausted Two Para was. He could see only what the British had done to him that day. If Keeble could make him believe that the next day would be even worse, he might win without firing another shot.

[MAP: move3-7 | Dotted radio line from San Carlos to GOOSE GREEN: CB RADIO · ERIC GOSS, FARM MANAGER; then two small POW figures walk from the British lines into the settlement under a white flag; time: AFTER MIDNIGHT]
So Keeble chose to talk before he attacked. A message was passed by civilian radio to the farm manager in Goose Green, who took it to Pee-AH-jee. Then, soon after midnight, two captured Argentine warrant officers walked into the settlement carrying Keeble's terms. They were simple. Surrender, and march out without weapons. Or refuse, and face the consequences. The note warned that the Argentine commander would be held responsible for the civilians, and that the British intended to bombard Darwin and Goose Green.

[MAP: move3-8 | Overnight: new blue counters fly in from the north: J COY 42 COMMANDO · MORE GUNS · MORE MORTARS; caption: PLAN B – FLATTEN GOOSE GREEN]
It was not a pure bluff. Through the night, Royal Marines, more guns and more mortars were flown in, and Keeble had a plan to pound the settlement if the answer was no. But his men were in no condition for another day like the one they had just survived, and he knew it. Everything depended on the other man believing the threat more than Keeble himself wanted to carry it out.

[MAP: move3-9 | Dawn, 29 May: long grey columns of red counters march out of GOOSE GREEN onto the airfield; weapons icons pile up; counter ticks up to 961 PRISONERS]
Pee-AH-jee read the terms, and concluded that the British could simply sit out of range and destroy the settlement. On the morning of the twenty-ninth of May, the Argentine National Army Day, the garrison surrendered. The paratroopers watched in disbelief. They had expected a few hundred men. Instead, column after column marched out onto the airfield, and nine hundred and sixty-one soldiers laid down their weapons, to a battalion that had lost about a sixth of its fighting strength killed or wounded.

[ARCHIVE: photo of Argentine prisoners at Goose Green, 1982; the settlement's civilians released from the community hall]
The civilians in the community hall walked out free. The cost of the battle was eighteen British dead and more than sixty wounded. On the Argentine side, between forty-five and fifty-five men were killed, and around a hundred wounded.

[MAP: move3-10 | Method card, third line lit: ATTACK THE MIND, NOT THE MAN]
This was the final part of the method. Attack the mind, not the man. By the evening of the twenty-eighth, Two Para had very little left to fight with. But the enemy commander did not know that. Keeble turned a day of violence into a threat, handed it to his opponent in writing, and let the other man's imagination do the rest.

## ENDING (~15:00 – ~16:30)

[ARCHIVE: 1982 radio / BBC broadcast still again, then the isthmus in daylight]
Remember that radio broadcast we began with? The Argentine defenders had been warned. They were dug in, they outnumbered the attackers two to one, and they fought, in many places, with great courage. None of that was enough.

[MAP: end-1 | Whole isthmus: the three moves replay in fast sequence: night arrows, Darwin Hill and Boca House, the ring around Goose Green]
Two Para won by choosing how the battle would be fought, and changing that choice when it stopped working. First darkness, to cancel out the enemy's numbers. Then heavy weapons, to crack what bravery alone could not. And finally words, to finish what their rifles no longer could.

[ARCHIVE: photos of 2 Para marching on to Stanley; the Falklands liberation, June 1982]
Two weeks later, Two Para fought again at Wireless Ridge, and on the fourteenth of June the Argentine forces in the Falklands surrendered. Chris Keeble was awarded the Distinguished Service Order. H Jones lies buried at the military cemetery at San Carlos, alongside the men who fell with him.

[ARCHIVE: Goose Green memorial / Darwin cemetery]
Goose Green is still studied at military colleges today: as a lesson in night fighting, as a warning about commanders who fight like riflemen, and as proof that a battle can be won in the enemy commander's head.
