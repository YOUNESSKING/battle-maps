## BACKGROUND (~1:05 – ~2:40)

[ARCHIVE: Peng Dehuai portrait (PD, 3D depth); 1930s Red Army / Long March stills (licensed only); headline date card "1928 / HUNAN, CHINA"]
Peng was born in eighteen ninety-eight, into a poor peasant family in Hunan province. His mother died when he was a small boy, and at times he begged for food and worked in a coal mine to survive. He became a soldier as a teenager, joined the Communists in nineteen twenty-eight, and fought through the Long March and the war against Japan. He was blunt, stubborn and famously plain-spoken, one of the few men who would argue with Mao Zedong to his face.

[MAP: bg-1 | Korea overview (option 1, control per date): 25 JUNE 1950: red-brown North Korean arrows sweep south to the Pusan perimeter (KPA flag); 15 SEP: UN landing at Inchon (ships, arrow); the North Korean army collapses; UN arrows race north over the 38th parallel; 19 OCT: Pyongyang ring flips red (UN); UN arrows fan out toward the Yalu (China border glows); PRC flag north of the river]
In June nineteen fifty, North Korea invaded the South. Within weeks, the North Koreans had pushed the Americans and South Koreans into a small corner around the port of Pusan. Then in September, General Douglas MacArthur landed behind them at Inchon. The North Korean army collapsed, and the United Nations forces, led by the Americans, crossed the Thirty-Eighth Parallel and drove north toward the Yalu River, China's border.

[MAP: bg-2 | Close on the Yalu, NIGHT 19 OCT 1950: blue PVA columns cross the river on bridges and pontoons (dotted trails), the same night Pyongyang (ring) flips to UN; Peng badge (PRC flag) slides in; counter 4 ARMIES; caption COMMANDER, CHINESE PEOPLE'S VOLUNTEERS]
China had warned that it would not stand by. Mao decided to send an army into Korea, and he gave the command to Peng. On the nineteenth of October, the same day the Americans entered the North Korean capital, Pyongyang, the first Chinese armies began crossing the Yalu in the dark.

## MOVE 1 — OWN THE NIGHT: UNSAN (~2:40 – ~7:00)

[MAP: move1-1 | Title card MOVE 1 · UNSAN · OCTOBER-NOVEMBER 1950. North-west Korea wide (option 1, 24 Oct control): red UN columns race north up separate valleys (ROK 6th, ROK 1st, US 1st Cavalry behind), wide gaps between them; the Taebaek mountains split Eighth Army (west) from X Corps (east), gap labelled ~50 MILES; red arrow tips reach toward the Yalu]
By late October, the United Nations forces were racing for the border. They were not advancing as one solid front. They moved in separate columns, up separate valleys, with wide gaps between them, and with the mountains of the Taebaek range splitting the whole army in two.

[MAP: move1-2 | Red belief card (MacArthur badge, US 48-star): "VERY LITTLE CHANCE" (Wake Island, 15 Oct); intelligence card: CHINESE IN KOREA? FEW · "VOLUNTEERS"; red reconnaissance planes (c47g, red) sweep the mountains and see nothing]
MacArthur had told President Truman there was very little chance the Chinese would intervene, and that if they did, their army would be slaughtered. His intelligence staff believed that any Chinese in Korea were small groups of volunteers. American reconnaissance planes flew over the mountains every day. They saw almost nothing.

[MAP: move1-3 | NIGHT: Peng badge. Blue columns march through the mountains by night (dotted trails, a moon icon, clock 19:00 -> 03:00), at dawn they vanish under camouflage (ghost outlines, snow); a red recon plane passes over (prop sound), nothing moves; counters tick up: blue strength in the mountains grows]
Peng saw something different. The Americans ruled the sky and the roads. So his men would use neither. They marched only at night, along mountain trails, and before dawn every unit was under cover, its men hidden in villages, forests and gullies, with orders to stay still if a plane came over. Day after day, entire Chinese armies moved into the mountains around the advancing columns, and the most powerful air force in the world never saw them.

[MAP: move1-4 | 25 OCT: ring on Unsan; first blue strike on the ROK 1st Division north of the town (impacts, MG); a prisoner icon: caption CHINESE PRISONERS; 26 OCT: a red ROK 6th Division regiment touches the Yalu at Chosan (flag pin), then blue arrows close behind it from both sides; red ROK II Corps counters at Onjong shatter and grey (hit)]
On the twenty-fifth of October, the trap began to close. Near the town of Unsan, South Korean troops ran into fierce resistance and took their first Chinese prisoners. Further east, one South Korean regiment reached the Yalu itself, the only unit of the Eighth Army that ever would. It was cut off and destroyed. Within days, the South Korean Second Corps on the right flank had been routed.

[MAP: move1-5 | Unsan close-up (option 2: glowing fronts only), NIGHT: the Kuryong and Samtan rivers shimmer; the US 8th Cavalry Regiment (three battalion counters) in a loose arc north and west of Unsan, gaps between them; blue PVA divisions (39TH ARMY) creep down the ridges toward the gaps; ring on the road junction south of town]
The Americans sent the Eighth Cavalry Regiment of the First Cavalry Division to Unsan to steady the line. Its three battalions spread out in a wide arc north and west of the town, with gaps between them. Around them, in the hills, waited the Chinese Thirty-Ninth Army.

[MAP: move1-6 | NIGHT 1 NOV: bugle + whistle captions (static), blue arrows pour through the gaps from three sides (MG, impacts, shake); blue counters slip behind the US battalions and a blue roadblock slams onto the road south of town (hit); stamp SURROUNDED]
On the first of November, as darkness fell, the Chinese attacked. Out of the night came bugles, whistles and shouting, and then waves of infantry, not only against the front but through the gaps between the battalions. Chinese units slipped behind the American positions and set up a roadblock on the road south of Unsan, the only road out.

[MAP: move1-7 | 2 NOV: two red battalion counters break out on foot over the hills, leaving guns and vehicles (greyed icons); the 3rd Battalion counter alone, ringed; red 5th Cavalry arrow pushes north toward it, stops at the blue blocking line (impacts); stamp RELIEF FAILED; 4 NOV: the ring closes, red counter greys]
By dawn, two of the battalions were fighting their way out across the hills, leaving most of their vehicles and guns behind. The Third Battalion was trapped. The Fifth Cavalry tried to break through to it and was thrown back by Chinese troops dug in on the ridges. The division commander had to order the relief force to pull back. The Third Battalion held out for two more days before the survivors tried to escape in small groups.

[MAP: move1-8 | 5-6 NOV: the blue counters fade back into the mountains (whoosh-less, quiet), the front goes silent; red counters pull back to the Chongchon; result card: 8TH CAVALRY: OVER 800 MEN LOST · ROK II CORPS ROUTED · UN ADVANCE STOPPED; then the method card line 1: OWN THE NIGHT]
The Eighth Cavalry lost more than eight hundred men. The UN advance in the west had been stopped and thrown back to the Chongchon River. And then, on the fifth and sixth of November, the Chinese did something nobody expected. They broke contact and disappeared back into the mountains. Peng had shown what an army that owned the night could do. Own the night.

## MOVE 2 — BAIT THE TRAP: THE CHONGCHON AND KUNU-RI (~7:00 – ~12:00)

[MAP: move2-1 | Title card MOVE 2 · THE CHONGCHON RIVER · NOVEMBER 1950. North-west Korea wide (option 1, 24 Nov control): blue counters withdrawn deep into the mountains (ghost outlines); a blue arrow curls back north (LURE THEM IN); the Chongchon line; red UN counters re-forming south of the river]
Why had the Chinese vanished? Peng had hurt the Americans, but he had not destroyed them. His men were short of food and ammunition, and the enemy was still strong. So he chose an old Red Army method: lure the enemy in deep. He pulled his armies back into the mountains and let the Americans believe the danger had passed.

[MAP: move2-2 | Red belief card: "A LIMITED INTERVENTION"; MacArthur badge; 24 NOV: red UN arrows start north again from the Chongchon along the valleys (I Corps west, IX Corps centre, ROK II Corps on the right flank in the mountains); the gap between Eighth Army and X Corps glows; HOME BY CHRISTMAS caption with a press-headline card]
In Tokyo, MacArthur's headquarters concluded that the Chinese intervention had been a limited one. On the twenty-fourth of November, the Eighth Army set off north again in what was meant to be the final offensive of the war. MacArthur flew in to see it begin, and newspapers reported him hoping to have the troops home by Christmas.

[MAP: move2-3 | Peng badge. The plan draws on: blue counters (13TH ARMY GROUP, ~230,000) in a great arc in the mountains; the right flank of the UN line glows: ROK II CORPS (thin red counters, ROK flags) at Tokchon; a big blue arrow aimed at it, then curling behind the American divisions toward Kunu-ri; label THE WEAK FLANK]
Peng was waiting. More than two hundred thousand men of his Thirteenth Army Group were hidden in the mountains ahead. And he had studied the enemy's line. In the centre and on the left were the American divisions, with all their tanks and guns. But on the right, in the mountains, the flank was held by the South Korean Second Corps, the same corps he had already broken once. That was where he would strike.

[MAP: move2-4 | NIGHT 25 NOV: bugles, blue attacks all along the Chongchon (MG, impacts, shake) pin the US 2nd and 25th Divisions; at the same time two blue army arrows (38TH, 42ND ARMIES) smash into ROK II Corps at Tokchon (impacts, shake); red counters crack and grey; stamp RIGHT FLANK COLLAPSES]
On the night of the twenty-fifth of November, the mountains came alive. Chinese attacks pinned the American divisions along the Chongchon River, while the main blow fell on the South Korean Second Corps. Within two days it had collapsed. The right flank of the entire Eighth Army was gone.

[MAP: move2-5 | 27-28 NOV: blue 38th Army pours through the hole, swinging south-west behind the American divisions toward Kunu-ri and the road south (arrows); the Turkish Brigade (red, Turkish flag) at Wawon fights a delaying battle (impacts) and is pushed back; ring on Kunu-ri; red withdrawal arrows start south; stamp ENVELOPMENT]
Now Chinese troops poured through the gap and swung south-west, around the back of the American divisions, toward the crossroads at Kunu-ri and the roads leading south. A Turkish brigade sent to plug the hole fought hard, but it was pushed back. On the twenty-eighth of November, the Eighth Army began to retreat.

[MAP: move2-6 | Kunu-ri close-up (option 2), 30 NOV, cold dawn: the road from Kunu-ri south to Sunchon glows; blue counters line the ridges on both sides for ~6 MILES (bracket), a blue roadblock at a pass (ring); a long red column of trucks, guns and men enters the valley]
The US Second Infantry Division was the last to leave, and its road south ran through a narrow valley from Kunu-ri toward Sunchon. The Chinese had got there first. They had set up a roadblock at a pass and lined the ridges on both sides of the road with machine guns and mortars, for about six miles.

[MAP: move2-7 | The Gauntlet: MG fire + mortar impacts rake the column from both ridges (shake), trucks burn and jam (smoke columns, red counters grey one by one); US fighter-bombers (red c47g, few, spaced) strafe the ridges (K.bombRun, shake); caption THE GAUNTLET; a counter ticks the vehicles lost]
On the thirtieth of November, the division drove into it. Machine-gun and mortar fire swept the road from both sides. Trucks were hit and burned, blocking the way for the vehicles behind them, and men had to climb over the wrecks or run along the ditches under fire. American aircraft strafed the ridges again and again, but the column could only crawl forward. The soldiers who lived through it called it the Gauntlet.

[MAP: move2-8 | Zoom out to all Korea (option 1, control 24 Nov -> 15 Dec cross-fade): red arrows stream south; Pyongyang ring flips blue (5 DEC); X Corps in the east pulls back to Hungnam (ships, 24 DEC); the front settles on the 38th parallel; Walker badge greyed: KILLED 23 DEC (jeep accident); result card]
The Second Division lost around four thousand men in a matter of days, and most of its artillery. The Eighth Army fell back so fast that it lost contact with the Chinese altogether. On the fifth of December it abandoned Pyongyang, and by the middle of December it was back behind the Thirty-Eighth Parallel. In the east, the rest of the UN forces were evacuated by sea. Two days before Christmas, the Eighth Army's commander, General Walton Walker, was killed when his jeep collided with a truck.

[MAP: move2-9 | Casualty / RESULT card (figures from FACT_NOTES only): US 2ND DIVISION ~4,000 CASUALTIES (LATE NOV) · EIGHTH ARMY OUT OF NORTH KOREA · ~120 MILES IN 2 WEEKS; method card lines 1-2: OWN THE NIGHT · BAIT THE TRAP]
Peng's victory came at a cost: his own men had been fighting in the open through a brutal winter, and many were lost to the cold as well as to American firepower. But in two weeks he had turned the war around. The army that had been racing for the Chinese border was now fighting to hold South Korea. Own the night. Bait the trap.

## MOVE 3 — HIT THE SEAMS: THE THIRD PHASE AND SEOUL (~12:00 – ~16:10)

[MAP: move3-1 | Title card MOVE 3 · SEOUL · JANUARY 1951. Korea overview -> 38th parallel close-up (option 1, 26 Dec control): the UN line along the Imjin and the 38th parallel north of Seoul, the Han river behind Seoul; Ridgway badge (US 48-star) slides in: NEW COMMANDER 26 DEC]
Walker's replacement was General Matthew Ridgway, who arrived on the day after Christmas. He found an army that was exhausted and badly shaken, holding a line along the Thirty-Eighth Parallel just north of Seoul. Behind it lay the frozen Han River and the South Korean capital.

[MAP: move3-2 | Peng vs Mao "duel" text cards (no likeness of Mao): PENG: "REST UNTIL SPRING" vs MAO: "ATTACK NOW"; Peng's supply line drawn from the Yalu down to the parallel (~300 KM, dotted, flickering, bomb hits along it); stamp ORDERED TO ATTACK]
Peng's own army was in trouble too. His men were worn out, short of food and winter clothing, and his supply lines now stretched hundreds of miles back to China, under constant American air attack. He wanted to rest and rebuild until spring. But Mao wanted another blow, and he wanted it now. So Peng planned an offensive that his tired army could actually carry out.

[MAP: move3-3 | Red belief card: "HOLD NORTH OF SEOUL"; close-up of the UN line (option 2): US I Corps and IX Corps divisions (big red counters, tanks, artillery) with South Korean divisions in the line between and beside them (thin red counters, ROK flags); the boundaries between them glow white; Peng badge: THE SEAMS]
The UN line looked solid on the map. But Peng looked at who was holding each part of it. The American divisions had tanks, artillery and air support. The South Korean divisions beside them, many rebuilt after the November disaster, had far fewer heavy weapons. And the weakest points of all were the boundaries, where one division's sector ended and the next began.

[MAP: move3-4 | NIGHT 31 DEC 1950 (New Year's Eve), snow: blue guns fire (K.gun salvos, arcs, impacts with shake) on the ROK sectors; blue arrows cross the frozen Imjin and smash into the ROK 1st and 6th Divisions (impacts, MG); further east more blue arrows hit the ROK divisions in the centre; red ROK counters crack and stream south (hit); stamp NEW YEAR'S EVE]
On New Year's Eve, after dark, the Chinese guns opened fire. Then the infantry came across the frozen Imjin River and through the hills, and the heaviest blows fell exactly where Peng had aimed them: on the South Korean divisions, and on the seams between them and the Americans. Before dawn, the South Korean lines had broken in several places.

[MAP: move3-5 | 1 JAN 1951: Ridgway badge drives north (jeep dotted path); trucks of retreating ROK soldiers stream past him (red counters greyed, no weapons icons); caption A BEATEN ARMY ON THE ROAD; the flanks of the US divisions now open (glow); blue arrows start curling behind them]
On New Year's morning, Ridgway drove north to see for himself. He met truckloads of South Korean soldiers streaming south, many of them without their weapons. With the South Koreans gone, the flanks of the American divisions were open, and the Chinese were working their way around them.

[MAP: move3-6 | 3-4 JAN: red arrows pull back across the Han (bridges glow, then blast impacts as the UN blows them, shake); Seoul ring flips blue: 4 JAN 1951 (hit); stamp SEOUL FALLS; UN line slides south to LINE D (~37th parallel, 7 JAN)]
Ridgway would not let his army be trapped against the river. On the third of January, he ordered Seoul abandoned. The UN forces pulled back over the Han, blowing the bridges behind them, and on the fourth of January the Chinese marched into Seoul. Within days the UN line had been pushed back some fifty miles south of the parallel.

[MAP: move3-7 | Blue arrows stop at the Han / south of Seoul (they halt; stamp HALT); text cards: "PUSH ON" (Kim Il Sung, KPA flag) vs PENG: "REST"; Peng's supply line flickers; zoom out]
And then Peng stopped. North Korean leaders wanted him to drive on to the end of the peninsula. Peng refused. His men were starving and frozen, his supply lines were at breaking point, and he knew the Americans had not been beaten, only pushed back. He let his army halt.

[MAP: move3-8 | RESULT card (no reliable casualty figure): SEOUL TAKEN 4 JAN 1951 · UN LINE PUSHED ~50 MILES SOUTH · US DIVISIONS ESCAPE; method card complete: OWN THE NIGHT · BAIT THE TRAP · HIT THE SEAMS]
In ten weeks, Peng's army had marched from the Yalu River to south of Seoul. At Unsan, he owned the night. On the Chongchon, he baited a trap and sprang it on the weakest flank. And at Seoul, he hit the seams. Own the night. Bait the trap. Hit the seams.

## ENDING (~16:10 – ~17:20)

[MAP: end-1 | Korea overview (option 1, control Jan -> Mar -> Jul 1951 -> 1953): Ridgway's counter-offensive pushes the line back north past Seoul (14 MAR 1951); blue spring offensives (APR-MAY 1951) hit and are thrown back (impacts); the line freezes near the 38th parallel: 27 JULY 1953 ARMISTICE (Peng badge signs)]
His halt proved right. Within weeks, Ridgway struck back, and in March the UN retook Seoul. When Peng attacked again that spring, his men were caught in the open by American firepower and lost heavily. The war settled into a stalemate near the Thirty-Eighth Parallel, and in July nineteen fifty-three, Peng signed the armistice for China.

[ARCHIVE: Peng in the 1950s (PD); headline card "1959 / LUSHAN"; date card "1974 / BEIJING"]
He came home a hero and became China's minister of defence. But in nineteen fifty-nine, he wrote Mao a letter criticising the Great Leap Forward, as famine spread across the country. He was purged. During the Cultural Revolution, Red Guards dragged him before mass rallies, and he was beaten in interrogation after interrogation. He died in custody in nineteen seventy-four. Only after Mao's death was his name cleared.
