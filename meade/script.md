Five days before that, at three o'clock in the morning, an officer from Washington walked into a general's tent in Maryland, woke him up, and said he had come to give him trouble. ## BACKGROUND (~1:05 – ~2:10)

[ARCHIVE: Meade portrait (Brady studio / LoC, PD, 3D depth); headline date card "1815 / CADIZ, SPAIN"; lighthouse photo (Carysfort Reef or Brandywine Shoal, LoC/USCG PD) with caption "BUILT BY LT. MEADE"; headline date card "30 JUNE 1862 / GLENDALE, VIRGINIA"; never a slideshow]
Meade was an unlikely war hero. He was born in eighteen fifteen in Cadiz, in Spain, where his American father was a merchant, and he grew up in Philadelphia. He went to West Point, finished nineteenth in his class, and spent most of his career as an army engineer, building lighthouses on the reefs of Florida and in Delaware Bay. He was tall, thin and short-tempered, and people called him the Old Snapping Turtle.

[MAP: bg-1 | Virginia overview (option 1, summer 1862 territory, US 34-star + Confederate flags): WASHINGTON and RICHMOND rings; 30 JUNE 1862 GLENDALE (east of Richmond): red arrows hit a blue brigade line, impacts (shake), the blue PENNSYLVANIA RESERVES counter holds; stamp WOUNDED · SHOT THROUGH THE BODY (red pulse); then Meade badge (real photo) slides in, method card frame appears empty: MEADE'S METHOD]
He commanded a brigade of Pennsylvania volunteers, and in the summer of eighteen sixty-two, at Glendale, outside Richmond, he was shot in the arm and through the body, and stayed in the saddle until loss of blood forced him off the field. He was back within weeks. Meade was not a showman. He was a careful, stubborn professional who read ground the way an engineer reads a blueprint. And three times, that eye for ground let him do what other Union generals could not.

## MOVE 1 — FIND THE GAP: FREDERICKSBURG (~2:10 – ~7:00)

[MAP: move1-1 | Title card MOVE 1 · FREDERICKSBURG · 13 DECEMBER 1862. Rappahannock wide (option 1, Dec 1862): red LEE ~78,000 on the hills behind FREDERICKSBURG, from MARYE'S HEIGHTS south to PROSPECT HILL / HAMILTON'S CROSSING; blue BURNSIDE ~120,000 crossing the RAPPAHANNOCK on pontoon bridges (bridge icons) into the town and onto the plain below it; FRANKLIN'S GRAND DIVISION (~60,000) on the southern plain; Burnside badge]
In December eighteen sixty-two, the Army of the Potomac, under Ambrose Burnside, tried to smash its way to Richmond through the town of Fredericksburg, Virginia. Lee was waiting on the hills behind it. Burnside crossed the Rappahannock River, and planned two attacks: one against the heights right behind the town, and one on the southern end of Lee's line, where Stonewall Jackson held a long wooded ridge near a place called Prospect Hill.

[MAP: move1-2 | Close (option 2 glowing fronts), Prospect Hill: red line on the wooded ridge (JACKSON ~38,000 on the ridge: count card) behind the RF&P RAILROAD; open plain in front, RICHMOND STAGE ROAD across it; blue FRANKLIN's mass on the plain (~60,000, many counters), only ONE blue division counter steps forward: MEADE 4,500 (ring); card BURNSIDE'S ORDER: "A DIVISION AT LEAST" (small: NPS); morning FOG drifting over the plain]
On that southern flank, Burnside had placed some sixty thousand men under William Franklin. But his orders were vague. They asked Franklin to attack with a division at least, and Franklin took him at his word. Out of sixty thousand men, he sent one division forward to lead the attack, about forty-five hundred Pennsylvanians. They were Meade's.

[MAP: move1-3 | FOG lifting ~10 AM: blue lines advance across the plain; 2 red guns on the RICHMOND STAGE ROAD (PELHAM) fire -> impacts in the blue ranks (shake), blue halts (clock ticks ~1 HOUR); then the artillery duel: rows of blue guns vs red guns on the ridge (K.gun -> arc -> K.impact both ways, shake); the red ridge line glows]
The morning began in thick fog. When it lifted, around ten o'clock, Meade's men started across the open plain, and were stopped almost at once by just two Confederate guns firing into their flank from the road. It took nearly an hour to drive them off. Then the artillery on both sides opened up, and for another hour the plain shook with gunfire.

[MAP: move1-4 | Terrain beat: camera glides along the red line: LANE's brigade, then a dark wooded TONGUE OF WOODS jutting across the railroad (boggy texture, glow), then ARCHER's brigade; the gap between them lights up: 600 YARDS (ruler, ticks); red belief card: "IMPASSABLE" (marsh icon); behind the gap, GREGG's brigade on the MILITARY ROAD, arms stacked (small rifle-stack icon); Meade badge: aiming arrow at the tongue of woods]
But Jackson's line had a hole in it. Where a tongue of swampy woods jutted out across the railroad, there was a gap of about six hundred yards between two Confederate brigades. The ground there was so boggy that it was thought to be impassable, so it was covered by little more than a line of skirmishers. Behind it, a brigade of South Carolinians waited in reserve, with their rifles stacked. Meade's men had been told to aim for that point of woods.

[MAP: move1-5 | ~1 PM: blue lines rush the railroad, impacts all around (shake), the centre of the blue line wades into the boggy woods: they're EMPTY (ring flash, caption FOUND THE GAP); blue arrows pour through the gap, turn left and right into the flanks of LANE and ARCHER (MG, impacts), red counters crack; blue hits GREGG's resting brigade (surprise stamp), stamp GREGG MORTALLY WOUNDED (small); counter OVER 300 PRISONERS (ticks); blue reaches the MILITARY ROAD on the crest: OVER HALF A MILE (ruler)]
A little after one o'clock, they charged across the railroad, and when they plunged into those woods, they found them empty. Meade's men poured through the gap and burst out onto the South Carolinians behind, who at first took them for their own retreating men. Their general, Maxcy Gregg, was mortally wounded. The Pennsylvanians drove on for more than half a mile, took over three hundred prisoners, and reached the military road along the crest of the ridge. With Gibbon's division fighting on their right, they had made the only break in Lee's line all day.

[MAP: move1-6 | Meade badge (hat icon, bullet streak: caption A BULLET THROUGH HIS HAT); blue riders (staff) gallop back across the plain to GIBBON and BIRNEY (dotted paths), BIRNEY's blue division counter stays put (stamp REFUSED x2); Meade's badge rides back himself: caption "ALMOST MADE THE STONES CREEP" - A BYSTANDER (small: as told by historian Stephen Sears); meanwhile red reserves (EARLY's division) stream down from the rear of the ridge toward the gap (red arrows, rebel-yell wave)]
Now everything depended on support. Meade, who had a bullet pass through his hat, sent officer after officer back across the plain to ask for it. The division behind him, under David Birney, refused to move without orders from its own corps. So Meade rode back himself, and by one witness's account, cursed Birney with such fury that it almost made the stones creep. But Franklin's sixty thousand stayed where they were. And Jackson, calm as ever, was already sending his reserves forward.

[MAP: move1-7 | Red counterattack: red arrows from three sides close on the blue salient in the woods (impacts, MG, shake); blue counters grey (two brigade commanders down: stamps C. F. JACKSON KILLED · SINCLAIR WOUNDED, small); blue line falls back out of the woods, across the railroad, to the RICHMOND STAGE ROAD (dotted retreat); a blue brigade (Birney's) arrives too late (stamp TOO LATE); quote card "MY MEN WENT IN BEAUTIFULLY, CARRIED EVERYTHING BEFORE THEM ... BUT FINDING THEMSELVES UNSUPPORTED ON EITHER RIGHT OR LEFT ... THEY WERE CHECKED AND FINALLY DRIVEN BACK" - MEADE, LETTER TO HIS WIFE, 16 DEC 1862]
The Confederates came at the gap from three sides. One of Meade's brigade commanders was killed and another wounded, and his division was driven back out of the woods, across the railroad, and over the plain to the road it had started from. Help from Birney arrived only as his men were coming out. Three days later, Meade wrote to his wife that his men had gone in beautifully, carried everything before them, but finding themselves unsupported, they were checked and finally driven back.

[MAP: move1-8 | Zoom out to the Rappahannock wide (option 1): MARYE'S HEIGHTS: blue waves break against the stone wall (impacts, grey counters); CASUALTY card: MEADE'S DIVISION ~1,800 OF 4,500 (40%) · UNION ARMY 12,653 · CONFEDERATE ~5,300-6,000 (OR / ABT); Meade badge: promoted: V CORPS (stamp); method card line 1: FIND THE GAP]
Meade's division lost about eighteen hundred men, four in every ten. Up at the town, wave after wave of Union troops was cut down in front of a stone wall below the heights. By nightfall the army had lost more than twelve and a half thousand men, about twice Lee's losses. But Meade had shown what a single division could do when it hit the right spot, and within two weeks he was given command of an army corps. Find the gap.

[MAP: move1-sub | Woven subscribe (E.subscribe): small SUBSCRIBE button bottom-right, cursor clicks (SUBSCRIBED, bell), slides out; then NEXT: GETTYSBURG label; map idles on the Rappahannock with the blue army back across the river]
But Fredericksburg was only the beginning. Breakdowns like this, move by move on the map, are exactly what subscribing keeps bringing you. Because six months later, Meade would be fighting with the whole Army of the Potomac in his hands, and Lee coming straight at him.

## MOVE 2 — HOLD THE INSIDE LINE: GETTYSBURG, 2 JULY (~7:30 – ~12:10)

[MAP: move2-1 | Title card MOVE 2 · GETTYSBURG · 2 JULY 1863. Overview Virginia -> Maryland -> Pennsylvania (option 1, late June 1863): red LEE ~72,000 marching north down the SHENANDOAH, across the POTOMAC into Pennsylvania (arrows toward CHAMBERSBURG, YORK, the SUSQUEHANNA near HARRISBURG); red cavalry (cavalry icon) far to the east; blue army following north through Maryland to FREDERICK (ring, 28 JUNE: MEADE TAKES COMMAND, real photo badge); dotted blue arrows north to the Pennsylvania line]
In May eighteen sixty-three, Lee beat the Union army again, at Chancellorsville. Then he marched north, across the Potomac and deep into Pennsylvania, with some seventy-two thousand men. On the twenty-eighth of June, Lincoln replaced the army's commander, Joseph Hooker, with Meade. He had about ninety-four thousand men, spread out across Maryland, and no time to learn the job. Three days later, the two armies collided at a small crossroads town called Gettysburg.

[MAP: move2-2 | Gettysburg wide (option 1, 1 Jul): roads converge on GETTYSBURG like spokes; 1 JULY: red columns from the west and north hit blue cavalry (cavalry icon, BUFORD) and I CORPS west of town (impacts), stamp REYNOLDS KILLED (small); blue lines break back through the town to CEMETERY HILL (arrows, red territory spreads over the town); NIGHT 1-2 JULY: Meade's badge arrives 1 A.M. (clock ticks); the blue position lights up: CULP'S HILL - CEMETERY HILL - CEMETERY RIDGE - LITTLE ROUND TOP: label THE FISHHOOK]
On the first day, the Confederates won. They drove two Union corps back through the town, killed one of Meade's best generals, John Reynolds, and pushed the survivors onto the hills just south of Gettysburg. Meade arrived at one o'clock in the morning and found his army holding a strong position, shaped like a fishhook. The barb curled around Culp's Hill and Cemetery Hill, and the shank ran south along a low ridge called Cemetery Ridge, toward two rocky hills, the Round Tops.

[MAP: move2-3 | Geometry beat (key insight): the blue fishhook glows; the red line wraps around the OUTSIDE of it (Seminary Ridge to the town to the east of Culp's Hill), drawn as a long red arc; a blue chord arrow across the inside (CULP'S HILL -> LITTLE ROUND TOP) races a red arrow around the outside (clock ticks, blue arrives first, ring); caption INTERIOR LINES; Meade badge at the LEISTER HOUSE (HQ icon, behind the ridge)]
Meade saw what that shape gave him. Lee's army had to wrap around the outside of the hook, on a long arc of ridges and roads. Meade's army sat on the inside. Any regiment he moved from one end of his line to the other travelled a far shorter distance than Lee's men would have to march. If Lee attacked one flank, Meade could get help there first. He set up his headquarters in a small farmhouse, the home of a widow named Lydia Leister, right behind the ridge.

[MAP: move2-4 | 2 JULY ~2 PM: blue III CORPS (SICKLES badge) peels off its place on southern Cemetery Ridge and swings forward west to the PEACH ORCHARD, the WHEATFIELD and DEVIL'S DEN (arrows, ring on the salient), leaving a hole in the blue line (red pulse on the gap, caption UP TO 3/4 MILE FORWARD); caption WITHOUT ORDERS (stamp); ~3 PM: Meade's badge rides out to meet Sickles; red guns open on the salient (impacts, shake)]
Lee's plan for the second day was to attack both ends of the Union line, with his heaviest blow, under James Longstreet, against the southern end. But there, Dan Sickles, commanding the Third Corps, did not like his position on the low ground. Without orders, he moved his whole corps forward, up to three-quarters of a mile, to a peach orchard on higher ground. Meade later wrote that Sickles had not fully understood his instructions. When Meade rode out to see for himself, around three o'clock, Confederate guns opened fire while the two were still talking. It was too late to pull back.

[MAP: move2-5 | Close (option 2 glowing fronts), 2 JULY after 4 PM: red LONGSTREET brigades attack one after another from south to north (sequenced arrows, each with impacts + MG + shake): DEVIL'S DEN, the WHEATFIELD, the PEACH ORCHARD; blue salient front bends and cracks; LITTLE ROUND TOP glows EMPTY (only signal flag icons); Meade sends WARREN (badge) racing to the hill (dotted gallop)]
A little after four, Longstreet's attack crashed into Sickles's exposed corps, brigade after brigade, rolling in from south to north. Devil's Den, the Wheatfield and the Peach Orchard became some of the bloodiest ground of the war. And at the far end of the line, Little Round Top, the hill that looked down on everything, was almost empty. Meade had sent his chief engineer, Gouverneur Warren, to check it. Warren found only a few signalmen there.

[MAP: move2-6 | The interior shuffle (blue arrows racing along the inside of the hook, each with a counter + label, timed to the words): V CORPS (SYKES) from the reserve to the left -> VINCENT's brigade up LITTLE ROUND TOP just ahead of red (impacts on the slope, ring holds, small caption 20TH MAINE); CALDWELL's division from II CORPS to the WHEATFIELD; XII CORPS (WILLIAMS, LOCKWOOD) pulled off CULP'S HILL and sent south (long blue arrow across the hook, Culp's Hill left thin: small red pulse); VI CORPS arrives on the Baltimore Pike: 32 MILES (ticks)]
This is where Meade's move began. He took troops from wherever the line was quiet and sent them racing across the inside of the hook. The Fifth Corps came over from the reserve, and one of its brigades reached the top of Little Round Top only minutes ahead of the Confederates. A whole division of the Second Corps went into the Wheatfield. Meade even stripped most of the Twelfth Corps off Culp's Hill and sent it south, taking the risk on his right to save his left. And the Sixth Corps, which had marched thirty-two miles through the night, came up behind as his last reserve.

[MAP: move2-7 | Plum Run gap, ~7 PM: red brigades break through north of the Wheatfield toward CEMETERY RIDGE (red arrows, the blue line has a hole: red pulse); 1ST MINNESOTA (blue counter, 262) charges (MG, impacts), counter 262 -> 47 UNHURT (ticks); WILLARD's brigade strikes (arrows); Meade's badge rides at the head of LOCKWOOD's brigade into the gap (caption: BY HIS FAMILY'S ACCOUNT); stamp OLD BALDY HIT (horse icon, small); the red tide stops at PLUM RUN (ring holds), dusk]
Toward evening, the Confederates broke through north of the Wheatfield and charged up toward the ridge itself, where there was suddenly a hole in the line. Hancock threw in a regiment of Minnesotans to buy time. Of the two hundred and sixty-two men who charged, only forty-seven came out unhurt. Fresh brigades arrived just behind them, and by his family's account, Meade rode at the head of one of them himself, up into the gap. His old horse, Baldy, was hit by a bullet that passed within half an inch of Meade's leg.

[MAP: move2-8 | Dusk -> NIGHT (K.night): red attacks on CULP'S HILL and EAST CEMETERY HILL (impacts, MG) are thrown back (stamp HELD); zoom out (option 1): the Peach Orchard + Wheatfield salient now red (lost ground flickers), but the main blue fishhook unbroken (glow); RESULT card: 2 JULY · FORWARD LINE LOST · MAIN LINE HELD · LITTLE ROUND TOP HELD; method card line 2: HOLD THE INSIDE LINE]
By nightfall, the Peach Orchard and the Wheatfield had been lost, and Sickles had lost a leg. But the main line held, from Little Round Top to Cemetery Hill, and the attacks at the other end of the hook, on Culp's Hill and Cemetery Hill, were thrown back too. Lee had hit both of Meade's flanks in a single day, and both times Meade had got there first. Hold the inside line.

## MOVE 3 — READ THE NEXT BLOW: GETTYSBURG, 3 JULY (~12:10 – ~16:40)

[MAP: move3-1 | Title card MOVE 3 · GETTYSBURG · 3 JULY 1863. NIGHT 2 JULY: the LEISTER HOUSE glows (lamp), 12 tiny badge silhouettes in a room card "10 OR 12 FEET SQUARE" (Gibbon); Butterfield's questions appear on paper: 1. STAY, OR RETIRE NEARER OUR SUPPLY BASE? ; then E.decide: dimmed map, WHAT WOULD YOU DO? A: STAY ON THE HILLS AND WAIT FOR LEE · B: FALL BACK TO A STRONGER LINE NEARER THE SUPPLY BASE; TELL US IN THE COMMENTS (3 ticks); on "the generals voted" A lights blue, B greys (hit)]
That night, Meade called his generals to the little farmhouse. Twelve officers squeezed into a room about ten or twelve feet square. Now stop for a moment, and put yourself in Meade's place. Your army has lost thousands of men in two days. Your line has bent at both ends, but it is still holding. Do you stay where you are and wait for Lee's next attack, or do you fall back to a stronger position nearer your supply base? Tell us in the comments what you would have done. And then hear what happened. The question was put to a vote, and every general answered the same way: stay and fight. Such then is the decision, said Meade.

[MAP: move3-2 | NIGHT: Meade badge + GIBBON badge at the door; quote card "IF LEE ATTACKS TO-MORROW, IT WILL BE IN YOUR FRONT." / "BECAUSE HE HAS MADE ATTACKS ON BOTH OUR FLANKS AND FAILED, AND IF HE CONCLUDES TO TRY IT AGAIN IT WILL BE ON OUR CENTER." - MEADE, AS RECALLED BY JOHN GIBBON (1887); the map: red pulses on both blue flanks fade, a gold target ring lands on the CENTRE: the COPSE OF TREES, II CORPS]
As the meeting broke up, Meade turned to John Gibbon, whose division held the centre of Cemetery Ridge. As Gibbon remembered it, Meade told him: if Lee attacks tomorrow, it will be in your front. Gibbon asked why. Because, Meade said, he has made attacks on both our flanks and failed, and if he concludes to try it again, it will be on our centre.

[MAP: move3-3 | 3 JULY morning: the long quiet: red guns roll into line along SEMINARY RIDGE and the Emmitsburg Road ridge (gun icons appear one by one, counter 0 -> ~150, ticks); quote card "NEVER BEFORE HAD SUCH A SIGHT BEEN WITNESSED ON THIS CONTINENT" - HENRY HUNT, UNION CHIEF OF ARTILLERY; Hunt badge; blue gun row on CEMETERY RIDGE: card 77 GUNS vs NEARLY 150 (II CORPS FRONT, HUNT)]
Meade was right. That morning the field went quiet, and all morning, along the ridge to the west, the Confederates rolled out gun after gun, about a hundred and fifty of them, facing Meade's centre. Meade's chief of artillery, Henry Hunt, later wrote that never before had such a sight been witnessed on this continent. On the stretch of ridge where the blow would land, Hunt counted seventy-seven Union guns to oppose them.

[MAP: move3-4 | 1 PM: the bombardment: rows of red K.gun -> arcs -> K.impact on and BEHIND Cemetery Ridge (most shells overshoot, shake on every impact, smoke drifting), the LEISTER HOUSE is hit (impacts, smoke), horse icons fall: card "THERE WAS SEVENTEEN DEAD HORSES ON MY LAND" - LYDIA LEISTER; stamp BUTTERFIELD WOUNDED (small); HQ icon moves; blue guns answer slowly, one battery at a time]
At one o'clock, the guns opened fire, and for nearly two hours the ground shook. But most of the Confederate shells flew high, over the ridge, and burst in the fields behind it. Meade's headquarters was hit again and again. His chief of staff was wounded, and Lydia Leister later counted seventeen dead horses on her land. Meade's staff had to move. And through it all, Hunt was making the decision that would matter most.

[MAP: move3-5 | Hunt beat: blue battery icons fire slower and slower, then go silent one by one along the ridge (each with a small grey dot, ammunition chest icons stay FULL); caption HUNT: SAVE THE AMMUNITION FOR THE ASSAULT; II CORPS guns keep firing and run low (small red ammo bar: HANCOCK's batteries); red guns stop too: red belief card: "THE YANKEE GUNS ARE FINISHED"; silence (clock ticks)]
He ordered the Union guns to fire slowly, to save their ammunition for the infantry attack that was sure to follow, and then to stop firing altogether. Meade had sent the same order. When the Union batteries fell silent, the Confederates took it to mean that they had been knocked out. Only some of the guns in the centre, ordered by Hancock to keep firing, ran short. The rest were waiting, loaded.

[MAP: move3-6 | ~3 PM: red lines step out of the woods on SEMINARY RIDGE: PICKETT, PETTIGREW, TRIMBLE (count card 12,500 ticks), march across the open fields toward the COPSE OF TREES (3/4 MILE ruler, the EMMITSBURG ROAD fences as a line); blue guns along the ridge and from LITTLE ROUND TOP and CEMETERY HILL open: K.gun -> arc -> K.impact through the red ranks (shake on every impact), red counters grey]
At about three o'clock, some twelve and a half thousand Confederate infantry stepped out of the woods, under George Pickett, Johnston Pettigrew and Isaac Trimble, and began to march across three-quarters of a mile of open fields, straight at the trees in Meade's centre. And the Union guns that had gone silent opened fire all along the ridge, from Cemetery Hill to Little Round Top, tearing gaps in the lines as they came.

[MAP: move3-7 | Close (option 2), the ANGLE: red lines cross the EMMITSBURG ROAD fences (slow-down), blue infantry at the STONE WALL fire volleys (MG), canister blasts from the guns at close range (big impacts, shake); blue STANNARD's Vermont brigade swings out and fires into the red flank (arrow, impacts); ~200 red men follow ARMISTEAD over the wall at THE ANGLE (ring), stamp ARMISTEAD MORTALLY WOUNDED; blue reserves rush in (arrows), stamp HANCOCK WOUNDED · GIBBON WOUNDED (small); red lines break and stream back (grey, arrows west)]
When the attackers reached the fences along the Emmitsburg Road, the infantry behind the stone wall opened fire, and the guns switched to canister, firing like giant shotguns at close range. A Vermont brigade swung out and fired into their flank. About two hundred men followed General Lewis Armistead over the wall at a bend called the Angle, and Armistead fell, mortally wounded. Hancock and Gibbon were both wounded. But the line closed around the breach, and in less than an hour, it was over.

[MAP: move3-8 | Zoom out (option 1, 3 July evening): red survivors stream back to SEMINARY RIDGE; CASUALTY card (charge): CONFEDERATE ~6,000 (ABOUT HALF) · UNION ~1,500 (ABT/NPS); then battle card: GETTYSBURG 1-3 JULY · UNION 23,049 · CONFEDERATE 28,063 (ABT); quote card "ALL THIS HAS BEEN MY FAULT" - LEE TO GEN. WILCOX, RECORDED BY ARTHUR FREMANTLE; method card line 3: READ THE NEXT BLOW]
About half of the attackers were killed, wounded or captured, some six thousand men, against roughly fifteen hundred Union losses. Lee rode out to meet the survivors. A British officer watching him, Arthur Fremantle, wrote down what Lee told one of his shaken generals: never mind, general, all this has been my fault. Across the three days, both armies together had lost about fifty-one thousand men. Meade had read where Lee would strike, the night before he struck. Read the next blow.

The next day was the Fourth of July. Lee's army stood on its ridge all day, and that night, in pouring rain, it began to retreat toward Virginia. On the same day, nearly a thousand miles away on the Mississippi, Ulysses Grant took the surrender of Vicksburg. In the same week, the Union had won two of its greatest victories of the war.

But the story did not end in triumph. The Potomac was flooded, and for a week Lee's army was trapped with its back to the river. Meade followed carefully, his generals advised against attacking Lee's trenches, and on the night of the thirteenth of July, Lee slipped across. Lincoln was furious. He wrote Meade a letter saying Lee had been within his easy grasp, and that now the war would be prolonged indefinitely. He never sent it. But when Meade learned of the President's disappointment, he asked to be relieved. The request was refused.

Meade kept his army to the end of the war. When Grant came east the next year, he travelled with the Army of the Potomac, and history remembered Grant. But at Fredericksburg, Meade found the one gap in Jackson's line. On the second day at Gettysburg, he held a breaking line together by moving faster on the inside. And on the third, he told his general exactly where Lee would strike, the night before. Congress thanked him by name for Gettysburg. And Robert E. Lee never invaded the North again.
