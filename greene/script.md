## HOOK

[MAP: hook-1 | The American South 1780: Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia. CHARLESTON falls (May 1780, 5,000 captured), British red units spread inland to a chain of outposts (Camden, Ninety Six, Augusta); American army marches south to CAMDEN and is shattered (Aug 1780), blue units scatter north]
In the summer of seventeen eighty, the American Revolution was dying in the South. In May, the city of Charleston surrendered, and with it an entire American army of more than five thousand men. Three months later, a second American army marched to a place called Camden, in South Carolina, and was destroyed in a single morning. Its commander fled the field on horseback and didn't stop for sixty miles. The British now held Georgia and South Carolina, and they were already marching north.

[ARCHIVE: camden | Battle of Camden, death of de Kalb — slow push-in]
Congress had no army left in the South worth the name. What remained was a starving, half-naked remnant of a few thousand men, camped in the woods of North Carolina. Against them stood a veteran British force of more than eight thousand regulars and loyalists, led by one of the finest generals in the British army, Lord Charles Cornwallis.

[MAP: hook-2 | Bio card: Greene cut-out, name in big red letters: NATHANAEL GREENE; rows: Major General, Continental Army / Born 1742, Rhode Island / Quaker, iron forge, self-taught / Southern command: Dec 1780]
Into this disaster stepped Major General Nathanael Greene. He was thirty-eight years old, the son of a Quaker ironmaster from Rhode Island. He had no military schooling at all. He walked with a limp, he had taught himself war from books, and his own Quaker meeting had thrown him out for taking an interest in soldiers.

[ARCHIVE: greene | Peale portrait of Greene — slow zoom to the eyes]
And yet George Washington trusted him above every other officer in the army. When Congress asked Washington to choose a man to save the South, he didn't hesitate. He chose Greene.

[MAP: hook-3 | The Carolinas; Greene portrait stake under the 13-star flag at Charlotte; Cornwallis stake under the Union flag at Winnsboro; British outposts glow; blue arrows north-south across the Carolinas]
Greene took command in December, seventeen eighty. Over the next nine months, he would fight Cornwallis and his successors across the Carolinas, and he would lose almost every battle he fought. And by the end of it, the British had been driven out of the entire southern interior, penned into two coastal cities, and Cornwallis himself was marching toward a place called Yorktown. So how does a general lose his battles and win the war? Let's take a closer look at Nathanael Greene's three greatest tactical moves.

## MOVE 1 — THE RACE TO THE DAN

[MAP: dan-1 | Title card MOVE 1 · THE RACE TO THE DAN · JANUARY–FEBRUARY 1781. Region map of North Carolina and southern Virginia; rivers CATAWBA, YADKIN, DAN highlighted; Greene splits his army: Greene east at Cheraw, Morgan west; COWPENS victory flash (Jan 17)]
January, seventeen eighty-one. Greene has done something every military textbook warned against. Facing a stronger enemy, he has split his small army in two, sending one wing west under Daniel Morgan. On the seventeenth of January, at a cattle pasture called the Cowpens, Morgan wins a stunning victory, and destroys the British light troops of the notorious cavalry commander Banastre Tarleton.

[MAP: dan-2 | Cornwallis stake; British army at Ramsour's Mill; baggage wagons burn (fire effect); caption: BAGGAGE, TENTS, RUM BURNED; counters: ~2,500 BRITISH REGULARS]
Cornwallis is furious. He decides to hunt down Morgan and Greene before they can unite, and to do it, he takes one of the most extraordinary decisions of the war. At a place called Ramsour's Mill, he burns his own baggage train. Tents, wagons, spare clothing, even the army's supply of rum. His soldiers watched their rum poured out into the mud. Around two and a half thousand British regulars were now stripped down to run.

[MAP: dan-3 | Cornwallis plaque with speech bubble: "TRAP THEM AGAINST THE RIVERS"; the three rivers pulse; British arrow aims north at the Dan]
Cornwallis believed the geography was on his side. Between the Americans and safety in Virginia lay three great rivers, the Catawba, the Yadkin, and the Dan, all swollen with winter rain. Somewhere, Greene would be caught with his back to a flooded river, and there, the British would destroy him.

[MAP: dan-4 | Camera pushes to the Dan; boats icons collect along the north bank at BOYD'S FERRY and IRWIN'S FERRY; caption: EVERY BOAT ON THE DAN]
But Greene saw something different. Weeks earlier, before the chase had even begun, he had sent officers to survey the rivers of North Carolina and to find every boat on the Dan. He ordered those boats gathered and kept ready, and he planned his marches around them. To Cornwallis, the rivers were an anvil. To Greene, if he controlled the crossings, they were a shield.

[MAP: dan-5 | Morgan's column crosses the YADKIN at Trading Ford by boat; rain; river rises; British arrow stops on the south bank; caption: THE RIVER RISES]
The race began. Morgan's men crossed the Catawba and then the Yadkin by boat, and behind them, the rain kept falling. When the British vanguard reached the Yadkin, the river had risen, and the Americans had taken the boats with them. Cornwallis could only fire his cannon across the water and wait.

[ARCHIVE: winter_march | soldiers marching barefoot through snow — slow pan]
The march was brutal for both armies. It was winter, the roads were rivers of freezing mud, and many of Greene's men had no shoes. Witnesses remembered the bloody footprints they left on the frozen ground.

[MAP: dan-6 | The two American columns reunite at GUILFORD COURTHOUSE (Feb 9); Otho Williams's light corps (blue, light icons) peels off and positions between the armies; Greene's main army arrows toward the lower Dan ferries]
In early February, Greene's two wings reunited at a village called Guilford Courthouse. His army was still too weak to fight. So he formed a rearguard of about seven hundred of his best light troops and cavalry under Colonel Otho Williams, and gave it one task: stand between the two armies, and make Cornwallis look the wrong way.

[MAP: dan-7 | Williams's corps skirmishes on the British front, drifting northwest toward the upper fords (DIX'S FERRY); British arrow follows it; main American army races northeast unseen; caption: 40 MILES IN 24 HOURS]
For four days, Williams danced in front of the British army, always close enough to be seen, and never close enough to be caught. He led Cornwallis toward the upper fords of the Dan, while Greene's main army marched hard for the lower ferries, far to the east. The men of the light corps slept six hours out of forty-eight. In the final day, they marched forty miles.

[MAP: dan-8 | Greene's army crosses at the ferries (Feb 13-14); Williams's corps breaks contact and crosses at night; boats pull to the north bank; British arrive Feb 15 at the south bank: caption NO BOATS]
On the fourteenth of February, the last of Greene's army crossed the Dan into Virginia, and that night Williams's exhausted rearguard followed. The next morning, the British vanguard reached the river. The water was high and fast, the far bank was lined with American troops, and there was not a single boat on the southern shore.

[MAP: dan-9 | Pull back: British force stranded 200 MILES FROM SUPPLY; British arrow back to HILLSBOROUGH; counters: ~200 MILES · 0 BATTLES · NO TENTS, NO FOOD]
Cornwallis had chased Greene some two hundred miles across North Carolina, burned his own supplies to do it, and had nothing to show for it. His army was exhausted, far from its bases, and without tents in the middle of winter. He had no choice but to turn back to Hillsborough. Greene hadn't fought a battle, but he had just crippled the British army.

[ARCHIVE: cornwallis | Gainsborough portrait of Cornwallis — slow push]
In the words of the British officer Charles O'Hara, the army had arrived at the river without baggage, without provisions of any sort, for officer or soldier, in the most barren, inhospitable, unhealthy part of North America.

[MAP: method-1 | Method card: three lines appear one by one: 1. MAKE THE LAND FIGHT FOR YOU 2. MAKE EVERY VICTORY COST MORE THAN IT'S WORTH 3. NEVER LOSE THE ARMY; line 1 highlighted]
This was the first part of Greene's method. Make the land fight for you. Make every victory cost more than it's worth. And never lose the army.

## MOVE 2 — GUILFORD COURTHOUSE

[MAP: guilford-1 | Title card MOVE 2 · GUILFORD COURTHOUSE · 15 MARCH 1781. Region view: Greene recrosses the Dan with reinforcements (militia arrows join from Virginia and N. Carolina); counters US ~4,400 vs BRITISH ~2,000]
By March, fresh militia and Continental reinforcements had swelled Greene's army to around four thousand four hundred men. He crossed back into North Carolina and chose his ground, near Guilford Courthouse, the very village he had marched through a month before. Cornwallis now had only about two thousand men. But they were among the best soldiers in the world, and more than half of Greene's army were militia, farmers who had never faced a British bayonet charge.

[MAP: guilford-2 | Battlefield map: Great Salisbury road running west-east up a gentle slope to the COURTHOUSE; three clearings; woodland; Cornwallis plaque with speech bubble "THE MILITIA WILL RUN"]
Cornwallis knew what militia usually did in a pitched battle. At Camden, they had thrown away their muskets and run before firing a shot. He believed a single determined charge would sweep them away, and then the Continentals would be left alone, outnumbered, and beaten.

[MAP: guilford-3 | Greene portrait stake; three blue lines appear one after another, ~400 YARDS apart: 1ST LINE N. CAROLINA MILITIA behind a rail fence at the edge of the woods, 2ND LINE VIRGINIA MILITIA in the woods, 3RD LINE CONTINENTALS on the hill near the courthouse; cavalry and riflemen on both flanks]
But Greene saw something different. He took an idea from Daniel Morgan's victory at the Cowpens, and made it deeper. Instead of one line, he built three, each about four hundred yards behind the last. The North Carolina militia held a rail fence at the edge of the woods. Behind them, in the trees, stood the Virginia militia. And on the rise near the courthouse waited his Continental regulars. Greene didn't ask the militia to win. He asked them to fire two volleys, and then fall back.

[ARCHIVE: guilford_battle | Battle of Guilford Courthouse painting]
The British attacked shortly after noon, across a muddy open field, in long scarlet lines with bayonets fixed. At the fence, the North Carolinians waited. Then, at close range, they fired.

[MAP: guilford-4 | British red lines advance east across the first clearing; volley flashes from the fence; red units lose strength (gray edges); caption: TWO VOLLEYS; militia fall back into the woods]
The volley tore into the British line. One British officer wrote that half of the Highlanders dropped on the spot. Most of the militia then fell back through the woods, and some did not stop running. But they had done what Greene had asked. The British had paid heavily just to reach the trees.

[MAP: guilford-5 | In the woods: second line; the British line fractures into separate clusters, wheeling right and left; caption: FIGHT IN THE WOODS; Hessian and Guards units pulled apart]
In the woods, the battle broke into pieces. The Virginians fought from tree to tree, and the neat British lines were pulled apart into separate battalions, each fighting its own battle in the undergrowth. By the time they emerged on the far side, the British units were bloodied, exhausted, and scattered.

[MAP: guilford-6 | Third clearing: British 2nd Guards break the 2nd Maryland; then 1st MARYLAND (Gunby) and WASHINGTON'S CAVALRY counter-charge; British 2nd Guards unit shatters (gray)]
Now they came up the slope toward the Continentals. The Second Battalion of the Guards broke a newly raised Maryland regiment and captured two cannon. But the veteran First Maryland wheeled into its flank, and Colonel William Washington's cavalry crashed into it from behind. The Guards, among the most elite soldiers in the British army, were cut to pieces.

[MAP: guilford-7 | British artillery on the road; grapeshot cone fired into the melee (both sides hit); caption: GRAPESHOT INTO THE MELEE; the melee breaks apart]
According to tradition, Cornwallis made one of the most terrible decisions of his career. Seeing the fight turning against him, he ordered his artillery to fire grapeshot into the struggling mass of men, knowing it would kill his own Guardsmen along with the Americans. It worked. The melee broke apart.

[MAP: guilford-8 | Greene orders the withdrawal: Continentals pull back north-west in good order along the Reedy Fork road; British hold the field; counters: US ~260 KILLED & WOUNDED · BRITISH 532 (27%)]
Greene could have thrown in everything he had left. Instead, he looked at the one thing he could never replace, his Continental army, and ordered a retreat. He left the field to the British. On paper, Cornwallis had won. But he had lost more than a quarter of his army, over five hundred men, and he could not afford to lose a single one.

[ARCHIVE: fox | Charles James Fox portrait]
When the news reached London, the opposition leader Charles James Fox rose in Parliament and declared: another such victory would ruin the British army.

[MAP: guilford-9 | Region map: Cornwallis marches east to WILMINGTON, then north into VIRGINIA; arrow ends at YORKTOWN with a question-mark flash]
He was right. Cornwallis could no longer hold North Carolina. He retreated to the coast at Wilmington, and then, instead of going back to protect South Carolina, he marched north into Virginia, toward the peninsula at Yorktown.

[MAP: method-2 | Method card, line 2 highlighted]
This was the second part of Greene's method. Make the land fight for you. Make every victory cost more than it's worth. And never lose the army.

## MOVE 3 — THE RECONQUEST OF THE SOUTH AND EUTAW SPRINGS

[MAP: eutaw-1 | Title card MOVE 3 · THE RECONQUEST · APRIL–SEPTEMBER 1781. Region map: Cornwallis's arrow heads north to Virginia; Greene's arrow turns SOUTH into South Carolina; British outposts: CAMDEN, NINETY SIX, FORT WATSON, FORT MOTTE, ORANGEBURG, AUGUSTA, CHARLESTON; Rawdon stake at Camden]
When Cornwallis marched away into Virginia, everyone expected Greene to follow him. Instead, he turned his back on the British field army and marched south, into South Carolina. The British still held the state with a web of fortified posts, from Camden to Ninety Six to Augusta, with some eight thousand troops spread across them.

[MAP: eutaw-2 | Hobkirk's Hill flash (April 25): Greene's army pushed back from Camden, but outposts fall one by one: partisan arrows (Marion, Sumter, Lee) and forts flip from red to blue: FORT WATSON, FORT MOTTE, ORANGEBURG, AUGUSTA; Camden evacuated; siege of NINETY SIX then abandoned]
In April, near Camden, the British commander Lord Rawdon attacked Greene at Hobkirk's Hill and drove him back. It didn't matter. While Greene's army kept the British garrisons pinned in place, partisan bands under Francis Marion, Thomas Sumter and Henry Lee went after the smaller posts. Fort Watson fell. Fort Motte fell. Orangeburg and Augusta surrendered. Within weeks, the British abandoned Camden, and then Ninety Six.

[ARCHIVE: greene_quote | Greene portrait — quote card: "We fight, get beat, rise, and fight again."]
Greene summed up his whole campaign in a single sentence, in a letter to the French minister. We fight, get beat, rise, and fight again.

[MAP: eutaw-3 | Battlefield map EUTAW SPRINGS, 8 SEPTEMBER 1781: River road from the west; clearing with the British camp, tents; the brick EUTAW HOUSE with a walled garden on the east side; thick BLACKJACK OAK thicket by the creek; counters: US ~2,200 vs BRITISH ~2,000; Stewart stake]
By September, only one British field army remained in the interior, about two thousand men under Lieutenant Colonel Alexander Stewart, camped at Eutaw Springs, fifty miles from Charleston. Their camp stood in a clearing beside a large two-story brick house, with a high-walled garden behind it. Greene came for them with around twenty-two hundred men.

[MAP: eutaw-4 | Dawn: American columns march east along the road; small red foraging parties (ROOTING PARTY) surprised and captured; British hurried into line]
He marched before dawn. On the road, his advance guard stumbled on a British foraging party, out in the woods digging sweet potatoes, and scooped it up. Stewart barely had time to form his line in front of the camp.

[MAP: eutaw-5 | Two American lines: militia first, Continentals second; they advance out of the treeline; the militia fires volley after volley before giving way; caption: THE MILITIA HOLDS]
Greene attacked in two lines, militia in front and Continentals behind, the same idea he had used at Guilford. And this time, the militia astonished everyone. They stood in the open and traded volley after volley with British regulars, firing, some said, as many as seventeen rounds before they gave way.

[MAP: eutaw-6 | Continentals charge with bayonets; the British line breaks and is driven east through its own camp; blue arrows sweep through the tents]
Then Greene sent in his Continentals. The Maryland and Virginia regiments charged with the bayonet, and the British line collapsed. Stewart's army fled back through its own camp. For a few minutes, Greene had the victory that had escaped him for a year.

[MAP: eutaw-7 | Blue units stop in the tents (units scatter, lose formation); caption: THE CAMP IS PLUNDERED; Marjoribanks' red unit holds the thicket; red units fill the BRICK HOUSE, fire lines rain from windows]
And then it slipped away. His starving soldiers, many of them half-naked, burst into a camp full of food and rum, and discipline collapsed. Men stopped to eat and drink in the middle of the battle. Meanwhile, British troops barricaded themselves inside the brick house and began firing from the upper windows, and on the flank, Major John Marchbanks held a thicket of blackjack oak.

[ARCHIVE: eutaw_battle | Battle of Eutaw Springs engraving]
Greene's cavalry charged the thicket and was shot to pieces. Its commander, William Washington, was wounded and taken prisoner. Greene brought up his cannon against the house, but they were too light to break the brick walls.

[MAP: eutaw-8 | Greene withdraws west in good order; counters: US ~520 KILLED, WOUNDED & MISSING · BRITISH ~700+ (over a third); British field army retreats to CHARLESTON]
Greene gathered his army and withdrew. Once again, the British held the field. And once again, it didn't matter. Stewart had lost around seven hundred men, more than a third of his army. The next day, he abandoned Eutaw Springs and retreated toward Charleston. The British would never again challenge Greene in the interior.

[MAP: eutaw-9 | The South: red areas shrink to just CHARLESTON and SAVANNAH; everything else turns blue; caption: DECEMBER 1781]
By the end of the year, a British force that had once controlled Georgia and both Carolinas held nothing but two coastal cities, Charleston and Savannah. Greene had lost at Guilford, at Hobkirk's Hill, and at Eutaw Springs. And he had taken back the entire South.

This was the third part of Greene's method. Make the land fight for you. Make every victory cost more than it's worth. And never lose the army.

## ENDING

[MAP: end-1 | Eastern seaboard: Cornwallis's army at YORKTOWN; French fleet arrows close the Chesapeake; Washington and Rochambeau arrows from the north; caption: 19 OCTOBER 1781]
Six weeks after Eutaw Springs, the army that Greene had worn down across the Carolinas was trapped at Yorktown, in Virginia, between George Washington's army and a French fleet. On the nineteenth of October, seventeen eighty-one, Lord Cornwallis surrendered.

[ARCHIVE: yorktown | Trumbull, Surrender of Lord Cornwallis — slow pan]
The men who laid down their arms that day included many of the same soldiers who had burned their baggage at Ramsour's Mill, chased Greene to the Dan, and bled on the slopes at Guilford. Greene never fought a battle at Yorktown. He didn't have to. He had already done his part.

[ARCHIVE: greene_statue | Greene equestrian statue at Guilford Courthouse]
Nathanael Greene never won a major battle in the South. But he never lost his army, and he never let the British turn their victories into control of the land. He understood, perhaps better than anyone of his generation, that battles are only a means, and that a war is won by whoever is still standing at the end.

[ARCHIVE: greene | Peale portrait of Greene — final slow push]
He died only a few years later, in seventeen eighty-six, at the age of forty-three, on a plantation in Georgia given to him by a grateful state. Today, towns and counties across America bear his name. Cornwallis himself is said to have admitted it: he never felt secure when Greene was in his neighborhood.
