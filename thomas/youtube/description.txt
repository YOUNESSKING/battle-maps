George H. Thomas held the line at Chickamauga after a third of the Union army ran from the field. Fifteen months later, days before Ulysses Grant could relieve him of command, he broke John Bell Hood's army at Nashville.

He was a Virginian from the same state as Robert E. Lee, and when Virginia left the Union in 1861 he stayed loyal to his oath. His own sisters turned his portrait to the wall and never spoke to him again. On 20 September 1863, one confusing order opened a quarter-mile gap in the Union line at Chickamauga, Longstreet's column poured through it, and the army commander rode back to Chattanooga convinced everything was lost. Thomas gathered the fragments on Horseshoe Ridge and held until dusk.

This mini documentary breaks down the three moves that made Thomas "the Rock of Chickamauga": the stand on Horseshoe Ridge, the charge up Missionary Ridge that nobody ordered, and the hammer blow at Nashville. Then it follows him to the end, when he refused to write his memoirs and history nearly forgot him.

Three campaigns, one method. Refuse to be hurried. Hold the ground that matters. Strike to destroy, not just to defeat.

A NOTE ON THE NUMBERS

Chickamauga cost the Union about 16,170 casualties and the Confederates about 18,454. For Missionary Ridge the video uses the totals for the whole Chattanooga fighting (Union about 5,800; Confederate about 6,700, over 4,000 of them captured), because figures for the charge alone vary between sources. Nashville figures are approximate: about 3,000 Union and about 6,000 Confederate losses, most of them prisoners. The claim that Thomas "never lost a battle he commanded" is a popular one and is presented as such; at Chickamauga he was a corps commander in a battle the Union lost. His nickname "Slow Trot" came from his West Point cadets, whom he would not let gallop the academy's worn-out horses, not from a later injury.
