## S01 Cold open

[S01 | wide 3D campaign map from altitude, late afternoon light, the great bend of the Mississippi; clouds; ONE continuous push-in down the river onto the bluffs; VICKSBURG rises on its word]
In the spring of 1863, one city stood between the Union and the Mississippi River. Its name was Vicksburg.

[S01 | the cloud dive begins; 3D gold title 5 BATTLES · 18 DAYS lands on its words and flies past the lens; white-out]
In just eighteen days, one Union army fought five battles to reach it, with no supply line behind it, marching between two Confederate armies.

[S01 | emerge over the river at the city: guns on the bluffs flash (each with its sound), the river bend below; the river line from Vicksburg south pulses red]
Its guns stood on high bluffs over the river. While they held, the Mississippi was closed to the North, and the Confederacy stayed in one piece. Every Union attempt to take the city had failed.

[S01 | fast montage on the wide map, each on its word: blue arrow across the river; red boxes splitting; a bridge burning; white flags over the city]
Then one army crossed the river below the city, turned its back on Vicksburg, and marched inland. And seven weeks later, nearly thirty thousand Confederate soldiers marched out of Vicksburg and stacked their arms.

[S01 | pull back over the whole river valley, the river line glows blue from Vicksburg south]
This is the story of the campaign that split the Confederacy in two.

## S02 The fortress and the plan

[S02 | wide map: the Mississippi from Memphis to New Orleans as a strip, Confederate stretch Vicksburg-Port Hudson in red; plaque WINTER 1862-63]
By the winter of 1862, the Union held the Mississippi almost from end to end. Only one stretch was still Confederate, from Vicksburg down to Port Hudson in Louisiana. Across it came men, cattle and supplies from the West. Close it, and the Confederacy would be cut in two.

[S02 | the city close: bluffs, river batteries along the waterfront, the bend in front; oval painting inset of Vicksburg from the river]
Vicksburg was built for this. The river swung in a hairpin bend under the town, and every boat had to slow down to round it. Above the bend, batteries lined the bluffs for miles. To the north lay the swamps of the Yazoo. To the south, more guns at Grand Gulf.

[S02 | Pemberton medallion card on his word; red boxes in the city lines; legend THE ARMIES]
The man holding it was John Pemberton, a Pennsylvanian who had married a Virginian and chosen the South. His orders from Jefferson Davis were simple: hold Vicksburg. His plan was simpler still. Let the river and the swamps do the work.

[S02 | stakes line: the wide map tilts, the river from Vicksburg to Port Hudson pulses red]
In other words, while Vicksburg stood, the North could never use the river, and the South could never be cut in two.

[S02 | winter of failures, quick on the wide map: blue arrow down from the north stopped (Chickasaw Bayou, Dec); the canal across De Soto Point drawn and washed out; the Yazoo route blocked; each stamped FAILED]
The Union general facing him had already tried everything. In December, his friend William Sherman attacked the bluffs north of the city at Chickasaw Bayou, and was thrown back with heavy losses. All winter, his men dug canals across the river bends and cut through flooded forests to get round the guns. Every attempt failed.

[S02 | NAME MOMENT: the Grant medallion card rises on his name, over the camp at Milliken's Bend in rain]
In the North, newspapers called for his removal. His name was Ulysses S. Grant. He was forty-one years old, and that spring, his own generals thought his next idea was the riskiest yet.

[S02 | Grant's plan on the wide map, step by step on its words: blue column down the Louisiana shore; the fleet past the guns; a crossing arrow below the city]
He would march his army down the west bank of the river, through the Louisiana swamps, far below the city. The navy would have to run its boats past the guns of Vicksburg, at night, to carry the army across. And once over the river, he would be on the enemy's side, with the fortress between him and every supply he had.

[S02 | Sherman medallion card; a letter card with the quote]
Sherman thought the army should go back north and try again by land. But whatever Grant decided, he wrote, it would have his zealous cooperation.

## S03 The night of 16 April

[S03 | plaque 16 APRIL 1863 · 10 P.M.; night grade over the river bend at Vicksburg; the dark fleet in a line upstream; moonlit water]
On the night of the sixteenth of April, at about ten o'clock, the boats cast off. Seven gunboats led the way, with Admiral David Porter in the flagship Benton. Behind them came three river transports, their boilers padded with bales of cotton and hay, towing barges of coal.

[S03 | the line slides into the bend; the first battery flash on the bluffs; bonfires catching along the shore]
The Confederates saw them. Guns opened from the bluffs. On the shore, houses were set on fire and bonfires lit, to light the river for the gunners. The boats were in plain view, slowing for the bend, under fire for more than two hours.

[S03 | close on the transports; one (Henry Clay) hit; its cotton bales catch; flames]
Every gunboat was hit, many times, and kept going. One transport, the Henry Clay, was disabled, and her crew abandoned her. Then a shell burst in the cotton around her boilers.

[S03 | the Henry Clay burning to the waterline, drifting down past the fleet; quote card "The sight was magnificent, but terrible." on Grant's words]
She burned to the water's edge and drifted on downstream, past the fleet, in the dark. Grant watched from the deck of a transport above the city. "The sight was magnificent, but terrible," he remembered.

[S03 | dawn, the fleet below the city at anchor; ledger: 7 gunboats through, 1 transport lost, 0 killed]
By morning, the fleet was below Vicksburg. One transport lost. And by Grant's count, no one on the transports had been killed. Six nights later, more steamers ran the guns with supplies, crewed by soldiers who volunteered. Grant tells us so many came forward that he could have manned the boats five times over.

## S04 Grand Gulf and Bruinsburg

[S04 | wide map: plaque 29 APRIL 1863; Porter's eight gunboats against the forts on the Grand Gulf bluffs; transports full of blue boxes waiting in the river]
Now the army needed a landing. On the twenty-ninth of April, Porter's gunboats attacked the forts at Grand Gulf, while ten thousand men waited on transports to go ashore. For five and a half hours the boats fired. They did not silence a single gun.

[S04 | the plan fails: the transports pull back; then a single small figure marker on the shore and a dotted road appears south to Bruinsburg on "a good landing"]
That night, Grant tells us, a local Black man came in with news. There was a good landing a few miles further down, at a place called Bruinsburg, and from it a good road inland, to Port Gibson. The information, Grant wrote, was found correct.

[S04 | feints on the wide map: north, Sherman's boats up the Yazoo at Haynes' Bluff; far east, a thin cavalry arrow (Grierson) across Mississippi; red boxes pulled away from the river]
Meanwhile, Sherman's men steamed up the Yazoo north of the city and made a show of attacking, to keep Pemberton looking north. And far to the east, a Union cavalry column under Benjamin Grierson rode the length of Mississippi, tearing up railroads and drawing Confederate troops after it.

[S04 | dive into the Bruinsburg close world; dawn 30 APRIL 1863; transports at the landing, blue boxes coming ashore; DAY 1 on the clock]
On the morning of the thirtieth of April, the landing began at Bruinsburg. Nobody was waiting for them. By the end of the day, some seventeen thousand Union soldiers were on the Mississippi shore.

[S04 | Grant medallion close; the Vicksburg bluffs far upriver behind; quote card]
"I felt a degree of relief scarcely ever equalled since," Grant wrote. "I was now in the enemy's country, with a vast river and the stronghold of Vicksburg between me and my base of supplies. But I was on dry ground on the same side of the river with the enemy."

## S05 Port Gibson (1 May)

[S05 | night march inland on the road, plaque 1 MAY 1863 · DAY 2; Port Gibson close world: two ridges split by deep ravines, the Rodney road and the Bruinsburg road]
They marched through the night. A little after midnight, near a farmhouse on the road to Port Gibson, they ran into Confederate pickets. The defenders were the division of John Bowen, rushed down from Grand Gulf, perhaps seven or eight thousand men against an army more than twice their size.

[S05 | the ground explains the fight: the roads on narrow ridges, ravines choked with cane and vines; red boxes astride both roads]
But the ground was on Bowen's side. Two roads ran along narrow ridges toward the town, and between them the ravines were choked with cane and vines. An army could only come at him along the ridge tops.

[S05 | the battle as action: blue boxes press along both ridges; smoke curtains; gun flashes; Bowen's line holds, then shifts]
All day the Union divisions pushed along both roads. Bowen held, counterattacked, and held again. By late afternoon, outflanked on both ridges, the Confederates fell back through Port Gibson and burned the bridges over the bayou behind them.

[S05 | ledger card: Union loss 131 killed, 719 wounded, 25 missing; the road to the interior opens]
Grant lost about eight hundred and seventy men. The road inland was open. But one more question had to be answered, and Grant's answer would decide the whole campaign.

## S06 Cut loose

[S06 | wide map: Grand Gulf now abandoned by the Confederates and in Union hands; a thin blue line back to the river (supply line); news card: Banks delayed]
Grant had expected help. General Nathaniel Banks was supposed to come up from New Orleans with his army. Now word came that Banks could not join him for weeks, and then with only some fifteen thousand men.

[S06 | Grant medallion; the letter card in his hand; quote]
Waiting would give the enemy time. So Grant decided, as he put it, "to move independently of Banks, cut loose from my base, destroy the rebel force in rear of Vicksburg and invest or capture the city."

[S06 | Sherman card; the warning as a letter card; a single road jammed with wagons]
Sherman, coming up with his corps, was alarmed. One road could never feed an army, he warned. "This road will be jammed, as sure as life."

[S06 | wagons, mills, cattle: the army foraging across plantations; small wheels turning]
Grant's reply was short. He would bring up what hard bread, coffee and salt he could, "and make the country furnish the balance." The army had left the river with about two days' rations. Every plantation had a corn mill turned by mules, and Grant kept them grinding day and night.

[S06 | THE TWIST on the wide map: everyone expects a blue arrow north to Vicksburg; instead it bends north-EAST, away from the city, toward the railroad and Jackson; red Johnston forces gather at Jackson]
Everyone expected him to march north, straight at Vicksburg. But instead, he turned north-east, away from the city, toward the railroad that fed it, and toward Jackson, the state capital, where a second Confederate army was gathering under Joseph Johnston.

[S06 | stakes line: Grant's army in the middle, Pemberton on one side, Johnston on the other; two red arrows that could close]
In other words, he was marching into the space between two enemy armies, with no supply line, deep in their own country. If they joined, he could be crushed between them.

## S07 Raymond (12 May)

[S07 | plaque 12 MAY 1863 · DAY 13; Raymond close world, hot and dusty; Gregg's red brigade by Fourteenmile Creek, the Union column coming up the road]
On the twelfth of May, outside the little town of Raymond, a Confederate brigade lay in wait along a creek. Its commander, John Gregg, had been told he faced a single brigade on the road.

[S07 | dust cloud of a whole corps on the road; blue boxes multiplying]
In fact it was James McPherson's entire corps, several times his strength. Gregg attacked anyway.

[S07 | battle in the creek bottom: smoke in the trees, confused fight, blue lines bend then hold; Logan banner]
For hours the fight swayed back and forth in the thick woods along the creek, in heat and dust so dense that neither side could see how many men the other had. Then the weight of McPherson's corps told. Gregg fell back through Raymond, toward Jackson.

[S07 | ledger: Union 66 killed, 339 wounded, 39 missing; the wide map: Jackson pulses red]
It was a small battle, but it changed Grant's plan. If a Confederate force was gathering at Jackson, he would not leave it behind him. First Jackson. Then Vicksburg.

## S08 Jackson (14 May)

[S08 | plaque 14 MAY 1863 · DAY 15; heavy rain over the wide map; two blue corps converge on Jackson from the west and the south-west]
On the fourteenth of May, two Union corps marched on Jackson through a downpour. Johnston had arrived the night before. He took one look and decided the city could not be held.

[S08 | Jackson close world: thin red line on the approaches holding while red columns leave north; blue lines break through in the rain]
A thin rearguard fought long enough for the rest to get away north. By afternoon, the Stars and Stripes flew over the capital of Mississippi. Grant slept that night, he tells us, in the room Johnston was said to have used the night before.

[S08 | the factory: the fire in the city; quote card on the tent cloth]
Before they left, Grant and Sherman walked into a factory that had never stopped working, not for the battle, not for the Union soldiers. They watched tent cloth roll off the looms, with the letters C.S.A. woven into every bolt. Then Grant told Sherman he thought they had done enough work. The workers were sent home with what cloth they could carry, and the factory went up in flames.

[S08 | SET-UP: a lone rider on a night road with a letter; the letter card: Johnston to Pemberton]
But the most important thing taken at Jackson was a piece of paper. The night before, Johnston had sent Pemberton an order, in three copies, by three different messengers: come up behind the Union army, at once. Time is all-important.

[S08 | the rider turns into the Union lines; McPherson card]
One of the three messengers was a Union man. Months earlier, he had been thrown out of Memphis with great public fuss for disloyal talk. It was an act. He carried his copy straight to McPherson, and McPherson sent it to Grant.

[S08 | the wide map: Grant knows; blue arrows swing back west toward Bolton and Edwards; Pemberton's red mass near Edwards]
Now Grant knew exactly what his enemy had been ordered to do. He turned his army around, and marched west to meet Pemberton.

## S09 Champion Hill (16 May)

[S09 | the wide map, plaque 15 MAY 1863; Pemberton's council of war at Edwards; red arrow south-east toward Dillon's (to cut the supply line)]
Pemberton had not obeyed the order. He held a council of war and chose another plan: march south-east and cut Grant's supply line. But the supply line he was looking for was not there.

[S09 | the march goes wrong: red columns stuck at a flooded creek, turning in circles; night]
His columns lost a day at a flooded creek. Then, early on the sixteenth, Johnston's order reached him again. Pemberton turned his army around. And while his wagons were still turning on the road, Grant's army came at him from the east.

[S09 | cloud dive into the Champion Hill close world; plaque 16 MAY 1863 · DAY 17 · morning; the crest, the three roads, the ravine; red line along the ridge facing east]
Pemberton formed his line on the best ground for miles, a wooded hill called Champion Hill. It was, Grant wrote, "one of the highest points in that section, and commanded all the ground in range." Three roads crossed it, and behind it, one bridge led west over Baker's Creek.

[S09 | Hovey's blue division climbs the Jackson road onto the crest; red guns flash; Logan's division swings round to the north; McClernand's two divisions stand idle on the middle and Raymond roads, marked with a clock]
By eleven o'clock, the skirmishing had become a battle. Alvin Hovey's division climbed the hill and took it, guns and all. On his right, John Logan's division swung round the northern end of the ridge. Further south, two more Union divisions under John McClernand stood within easy hearing of the guns. Grant sent order after order to attack. They did not come.

[S09 | THE COUNTERATTACK: Bowen's red division pours up from the south; Hovey's boxes are pushed back down the slope; the crest goes red again]
Then Pemberton sent in John Bowen, the same general who had fought at Port Gibson. His division hit Hovey's men and drove them back down the hill, past the captured guns, almost to the bottom. For a moment the battle was swinging the other way.

[S09 | Union guns massed at the foot of the hill (battery flashes); Crocker's blue brigades arrive; the line holds; the crest changes hands again]
The Union rallied at the foot of the slope, behind a line of massed guns. Reinforcements came up, and the hill changed hands again. Hovey's division lost about twelve hundred men that day, more than a third of its strength.

[S09 | the twist of fate: Logan's boxes astride the only escape road behind the hill; then Grant's order pulls them away and the road reopens; red columns pour out across Baker's Creek]
And on the north end of the field, without knowing it, Logan's men had put themselves across the only road the Confederates could use to escape. "Neither Logan nor I knew that we had cut off the retreat of the enemy," Grant admitted. A call for help came from Hovey, Logan's men were moved, and the road opened again. Between three and four o'clock, Pemberton's army broke and fled west.

[S09 | Loring cut off: one red division turned south, away, fading east; Tilghman's banner falls]
One whole Confederate division, under William Loring, was cut off from the rest. It escaped south and east, and never got back into Vicksburg. One of its brigade commanders, Lloyd Tilghman, was killed by a shell covering the retreat.

[S09 | ledger card + number: Union 410 killed / 1,844 wounded / 187 missing; Confederates: over 3,000 killed and wounded, about 3,000 captured (Grant)]
It was the decisive battle of the campaign. Grant lost about two thousand four hundred men. Pemberton, by Grant's count, lost over three thousand killed and wounded, and about three thousand captured, and a whole division besides. That night, Grant slept on the porch of a house full of wounded Confederate soldiers.

## S10 Big Black River Bridge (17 May)

[S10 | the v8 Big Black close world; plaque 17 MAY 1863 · DAY 18; red bridgehead in the bend east of the river, breastworks of cotton bales; one bridge behind them]
The next morning, Pemberton made one more stand, at the Big Black River. His rearguard dug in on the east bank, in a loop of the river, behind breastworks, with the bridges at their backs.

[S10 | Lawler's brigade surges from the north across the open ground; volleys; the line breaks; the red boxes turn grey in place]
It lasted minutes. A Union brigade under Michael Lawler charged out of cover on the northern flank, straight through the breastworks. A little after nine, Grant tells us, the line was taken.

[S10 | the bridge burning, red columns crossing as it catches; number 1,751 prisoners · 18 guns]
The defenders ran for the bridges. Behind them, the Confederates set them on fire. Eighteen guns were taken, and seventeen hundred and fifty-one prisoners. The fire stopped Grant for a single day. By the next morning, three new bridges crossed the river.

## S11 The assaults (18-22 May)

[S11 | 18 MAY 1863; Sherman's blue boxes reach the Walnut Hills north of the city, the same bluffs as December; Sherman card; the quote]
On the eighteenth of May, Sherman's corps reached the bluffs north of Vicksburg, the same heights where his men had been beaten in December. Sherman rode up to the crest and looked down at the swamp where his men had lain helpless. Then, as Grant remembered it, he turned and said that up to this minute he had felt no positive assurance of success. This, he said, was the end of one of the greatest campaigns in history.

[S11 | the siege close world: the seven-mile line of forts on the ridges east of the city, plaque 19 MAY 1863; blue assault on the Stockade Redan fails]
But Vicksburg itself was still standing. Its land defences ran for some seven miles along the ridges east of the town: forts and redans on every high point, joined by trenches. On the nineteenth, Grant attacked at once, hoping the defenders were still shaken. They were not.

[S11 | 22 MAY 1863: a general assault along three miles of the line; smoke along the whole front; blue boxes reach the ditches and fall back]
On the twenty-second he tried again, along the whole front, three corps at once. A few flags reached the enemy's parapets. None stayed there. Grant later wrote that he had always regretted the last assault at Cold Harbor, and that he might say the same of the assault of the twenty-second of May at Vicksburg.

[S11 | the clock card: 30 APRIL -> 19 MAY: five battles, ~180 miles, five days' rations issued]
He had crossed the river twenty days earlier. Since then, by his own count, his army had won five battles, marched an average of about a hundred and eighty miles, and been issued just five days' rations. Now there was only one way left. He would starve the city out.

## S12 The siege and the surrender

[S12 | the siege close world from above; Union approach trenches zigzag forward over the weeks; plaque JUNE 1863; mortar boats on the river at night]
Grant's men dug. Their trenches zigzagged up the slopes toward the forts, closer every week, while gunboats and mortar boats shelled the town from the river. Inside, the people of Vicksburg dug too, into the hillsides, living in caves to escape the shells. As food ran out, the garrison ate mule meat.

[S12 | 25 JUNE 1863 · 3 P.M.: the mine under the 3rd Louisiana Redan explodes; crater; blue regiments rush in and are held]
On the twenty-fifth of June at three o'clock, Union engineers blew a mine under one of the forts. The explosion threw the top of the hill into the air, and left a crater. The Union troops rushed in, and were held there. The siege went on.

[S12 | the town's newspaper card, printed on wallpaper]
The town's newspaper was now printed on the back of wallpaper. It mocked the Yankee boast that they would dine in Vicksburg on the Fourth of July. The best recipe for cooking a rabbit, it said, was first catch your rabbit.

[S12 | 3 JULY 1863: white flags on the line; the meeting under a small oak on the hillside between the lines; Grant and Pemberton cards]
On the third of July, white flags appeared on the Confederate works. That afternoon, Grant and Pemberton met on a hillside between the lines, near a stunted oak tree. They had served in the same division in the Mexican War. That night Grant sent his terms: parole. Each man would sign a promise not to fight again until exchanged, and go home.

[S12 | 4 JULY 1863 · 10 A.M.: the Confederate boxes march out, stack arms (grey), march back; blue boxes stand silent; number ~29,500]
At ten o'clock in the morning of the Fourth of July, the garrison of Vicksburg marched out of its works, stacked its arms, and marched back again. Some twenty-nine and a half thousand men. Grant tells us his whole army watched without a cheer. The last issue of the newspaper announced that the Yankees had caught their rabbit.

[S12 | the wide map pulls up the whole river; Port Hudson falls 9 JULY; the river line turns blue to New Orleans; Lincoln quote card]
Five days later, Port Hudson surrendered too. The whole river was in Union hands, and the Confederacy was cut in two. In the same days, far to the east, Lee's army had been beaten at Gettysburg. "The Father of Waters," Lincoln wrote that summer, "again goes unvexed to the sea."

[S12 | END: slow pull-up to altitude over the river at sunset; Grant medallion; ledger of the campaign; fade]
For Grant it was the start of a road that would lead him east, to command every Union army, and in the end, to Appomattox. It began on a dark river, with seven gunboats and a few burning bales of cotton, and an idea that everyone around him thought was madness.
