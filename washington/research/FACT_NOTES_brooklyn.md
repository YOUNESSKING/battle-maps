# FACT_NOTES: New York 1776 (background + MOVE 1, the Brooklyn evacuation) and the wide-map aftermath

Researched 2026-11-25. Format per item: claim / wording to use on screen / sources / confidence + conflicts ("honest hedge").
Confidence: A = two or more independent sources agree; B = one good source or sources differ slightly; C = tradition / single secondary source, hedge or skip.

Short source keys (full list at the end):
- **JOHNSTON** = Henry P. Johnston, *The Campaign of 1776 around New York and Brooklyn* (1878; Long Island Historical Society; full text https://archive.org/download/campaignof1776ar00john/campaignof1776ar00john_djvu.txt , Gutenberg #21990). Old, but it uses the primary letters and corrects popular myths; page numbers below are the printed ones.

## A. The armada and the landings (summer 1776)

1. **First British ships off New York: 29 June 1776.** Washington to Hancock, 29 June: "forty-five arrived at the Hook to-day ... I suppose the whole fleet will be in within a day or two." Use: "29 June 1776: the first sails." Sources: JOHNSTON p.94 note (quoting the Washington letter; Founders Online has the letter in the Washington Papers, Revolutionary War series vol. 5). Confidence A- (letter quoted, original not opened).
2. **Staten Island landing: 2-3 July 1776, unopposed.** Howe's ships entered the Narrows on 2 July; troops landed on undefended Staten Island (3 July in WP-NYNJ). Use: "3 July: Howe lands on Staten Island." Sources: WP-NYNJ; JOHNSTON p.94. Conflict: WP gives 2 July for the ships and 3 July for the landing; treat as the same operation. Confidence A.
3. **Fleet and troop growth.** About 9,000 men with Howe from Halifax in early July (Washington was told "10,000 landed, 15,000 more expected", WP-NYNJ); Admiral Lord Howe's reinforcement convoy in mid-July (fleet "nearly 300 transports and ships of war", JOHNSTON p.94); Clinton and Cornwallis from the failed Carolina expedition on 1 Aug; the Guards and von Heister's Hessians on 12 Aug after "a tedious voyage of thirteen weeks", swelling the fleet in the Narrows to "more than four hundred ships" (JOHNSTON p.94-95). Use: "Over 400 ships; the biggest force Britain had ever sent abroad" (JOHNSTON: "England had never before this sent from her shores a more powerful military and naval armament upon foreign service"). Confidence B (the "400" is JOHNSTON's 1878 count; modern books often say ~130 ships at first arrival and "73 warships", which I could NOT verify, so do not put "73" on screen).
4. **Total British and Hessian strength: about 32,000 men.** WP-NYNJ infobox: 32,000 soldiers. Use: "about 32,000 British and Hessian troops". The Hessians were roughly 9,000 of the 22,000 who went to Long Island (WP-NYNJ text). Confidence B (round figure; sources count differently: effectives vs all ranks).
5. **American strength: about 19,000-20,000 fit for duty on 27 Aug, 28,000+ on the rolls.** JOHNSTON p.125 and note: 28,400 total on 12 Sep, ~8,000-9,000 sick; "between 19,000 and 20,000 effectives on August 27th", matching Washington on 2 Sep: "under 20,000". WP-NYNJ: ~19,000 effectives in July. Use: "under 20,000 fit men, mostly new levies and militia". Confidence A.
6. **Long Island landing: 22 Aug at Gravesend Bay, unopposed.** Advance guard of 4,000 left Staten Island at 05:10 and landed by about 08:00; by noon about 15,000 men and 40 guns were ashore. Frigates Phoenix, Greyhound and Rose with the bomb-ketches Thunder and Carcass covered it. Sources: WP-LI; JOHNSTON pp.139-141 ("before noon the fifteen thousand"). Confidence A. After 24 Aug Hessian reinforcements brought it to ~20,000 on Long Island (WP-LI). Confidence B.

## B. The Battle of Long Island, 27 Aug 1776

7. **Howe's night flank march.** Clinton learned from Loyalists that the Jamaica Pass was lightly guarded and proposed the plan (via Erskine); Howe approved on 26 Aug. The column of "hardly less than ten thousand" (Clinton's light infantry first, then Cornwallis and Howe) left Flatlands about 21:00 on 26 Aug, guided by three Long Island Loyalists, struck "across the country through the new lots", and halted SE of the pass in front of Howard's Halfway House at about 2-3 a.m. Five American militia/patrol officers watching the pass were captured. The column was through by dawn. At 09:00 two signal guns started the frontal attacks (Grant on the coast, Hessians at Flatbush). Sources: WP-LI; JOHNSTON pp.170-172 (quoting Howe: "across the country through the new lots"). Use: "about 10,000 men marching all night". **Do not give a distance** (I found no sourced figure). Confidence A.
8. **American position and numbers on the Guan Heights.** About 10,000 Americans were on Long Island in all, but only ~3,000 held the forward Guan Heights line; four passes (Gowanus road, Flatbush, Bedford, Jamaica), the Jamaica Pass guarded only by five officers' patrol (WP-LI infobox note; JOHNSTON pp.146-156). Use: "about 3,000 on the ridge against 20,000". Confidence B (WP gives 6,000 on Long Island in text and 10,000 in the infobox; JOHNSTON has Putnam's force 8,000-ish; keep the 3,000 vs 20,000 frame).
9. **Stirling and the Old Stone House.** Lord Stirling held the Gowanus road against Grant with about 1,600 for about four hours; when Howe's flank column and the Hessians closed behind, most of his men escaped across Gowanus Creek (Brouwer's Millpond, ~80 yd wide). A rear guard of Marylanders under Maj. Mordecai Gist (~260-270 men) held the Vechte-Cortelyou (Old Stone) House against 2,000+ British in repeated charges; Stirling himself broke away toward the Hessians and surrendered to von Heister. Sources: WP-LI; Wikipedia "Old Stone House (Brooklyn)". **Myth to avoid:** "256 Marylanders killed" is repeated everywhere (incl. the Old Stone House page) but is disputed: Parry (2019) concludes "256 Marylanders were not killed on the battlefield, more likely only about a tenth of that number" (WP-LI). Use: "about 260 Marylanders held the house, only a handful got back" and avoid a death count. Confidence B on the overall story, C on any number of dead.
10. **Sullivan captured.** Maj. Gen. John Sullivan got most of his men back to the lines but was captured himself when the Hessians overran the left at Battle Pass (WP-LI). Johnston: Sullivan, Stirling and Brig. Gen. Woodhull (taken the next day) were the three generals in Howe's prisoner return. Confidence A.
11. **Washington watching: "Good God, what brave fellows I must this day lose."** Said while watching from the Cobble Hill redoubt (WP-LI, citing no primary). Confidence C: single source, a traditional quote; if used say "is said to have exclaimed".
12. **American losses: roughly 1,000 captured, a few hundred killed and wounded, about 1,400 in all, with real dispute.** The numbers:
    - Howe's report (3 Sep): 1,006 enlisted prisoners + 91 officers (3 generals, 3 colonels, 4 lt. cols., 3 majors, 18 captains...) = 1,097; he "computed" a further 2,200 killed/wounded/drowned (JOHNSTON pp.202-203, who calls this exaggerated). Howe's own loss: 5 officers + 56 men killed, 12 + 255 wounded, 1 + 30 missing (JOHNSTON); Hessians 2 killed, 26 wounded. WP-LI quotes Howe as 60 killed / 267 wounded / 31 missing, infobox 64 / 293 / 31.
    - Washington to Hancock (30 Aug): "700 to 1,000" killed and taken; to the Massachusetts Assembly (19 Sep): "about eight hundred, more than three fourths prisoners" (JOHNSTON pp.203-204).
    - Modern: WP-LI text "about 300 killed and over 1,000 captured"; infobox 300 killed, 800 wounded, 1,079 captured/missing. HistoryNet: about 300 killed, 700 wounded, 1,000 captured. Johnston argued the true dead were far fewer (Col. Silliman: "I do not think we had 50 men killed", i.e. mostly prisoners).
    - **Use on screen: "about 1,000 captured and a few hundred killed or wounded".** Do not state a killed figure; "about 1,400 in all" is only the sum of the commonly quoted figures (300 + 1,079) and is not a sourced total. Confidence B for 1,000 captured, C for killed/wounded.

## C. The trap on Brooklyn Heights, 28-29 Aug

13. **The Brooklyn works.** A chain of five forts/redoubts (Fort Box, Fort Greene [the 1776 work, not today's park], Oblong Redoubt, Fort Putnam, the "redoubt on the left") with connecting entrenchments from Wallabout Bay swamp to the Gowanus marsh, "about a mile and a half in extent" with ditches and abatis (Capt. Montressor, British engineer, testifying to Parliament 1779: "rather fortresses than redoubts", not to be taken "by assault, but by approaches"). Also Fort Stirling and a citadel on Columbia Heights (Clark St/Henry St), Fort Cobble Hill, Fort Defiance at Red Hook, a mill-dam redoubt on Gowanus Creek. Sources: JOHNSTON pp.70-75. Confidence A. Locations in geo_brooklyn.json are derived from Johnston's modern street descriptions.
14. **Howe chose to dig, not storm.** Howe halted the pursuit on 27 Aug over his officers' protests and ordered regular approaches. The night of 28-29 Aug the British broke ground: a parallel 60 rods (~300 m) long, 150 rods (~750 m) from Fort Putnam by the morning of the 29th (Lt. Col. Little, in JOHNSTON p.215; Gordon says 400-500 yards from the redoubt on the left). Washington's reason: "they intended to force us from [the lines] by regular approaches ... and at the same time meant, by the ships of war, to cut off the communication between the City and Island" (Washington to Trumbull, 6 Sep, in JOHNSTON p.217). Howe's own reason, told to Parliament in 1779: he would not "wantonly" risk troops "where the object was inadequate" (WP-LI). Historians dispute his motive; **do not say "Howe was lazy/cautious"**; say "Howe chose a siege rather than storm the works." Confidence A for the fact, B for any motive.
15. **Weather.** Rain began on the afternoon of 28 Aug (WP-LI) and was torrential on the 28th and 29th (JOHNSTON note p.223). A **north-east wind "which had prevailed for three days"** held the British warships from beating up the East River ("wind ahead", Col. Reed 30 Aug; Admiral Howe reported only the Roebuck "could fetch high enough" to exchange shots with Red Hook, 27 Aug; JOHNSTON p.217). Britannica: a storm drove the British warships downriver and Washington used the opportunity. Use: "A north-east gale kept the British fleet out of the East River." Confidence B (the NE wind is attested in the primary letters via Johnston; Britannica snippet is loose).
16. **The council of war, 29 Aug.** Held in the afternoon/evening (WP-LI: 16:00; Founders Online, Washington Papers vol. 6, doc. 03-06-02-0132: attendees incl. Putnam, Spencer, Mifflin, McDougall; unanimous for withdrawal; I could not open the page text). **Nuance:** JOHNSTON (pp.215-219) shows Washington decided that morning and sent the boat orders **before** the council: Mifflin's letter to Heath at King's Bridge ("send down ... all the boats ... fit for transporting troops ... manned by some of Colonel Hutchinson's men") and a verbal order via Joseph Trumbull to Assistant Quartermaster Hugh Hughes about noon "to impress every kind of water craft from Hell Gate on the Sound to Spuyten Duyvil Creek that could be kept afloat and that had either sails or oars, and have them all in the east harbor of the City by dark." Use: "At noon he ordered every boat from Hell Gate to Spuyten Duyvil seized." Confidence A (Washington confirmed the substance in an 1784 letter for Hughes; he misdated it to the 27th). Gordon's anecdote (Mifflin: "What is your strength?" - "Nine thousand." - "It is not sufficient, we must retreat.") is a good line but a retelling (C): say "reportedly".

## D. THE EVACUATION, night of 29-30 Aug 1776

17. **Numbers: about 9,000 men.** Washington told Mifflin "Nine thousand" (Gordon, JOHNSTON p.216); HistoryNet, NPS Saratoga "about 9,000"; WP-LI "9,000"; Encyclopedia.com "almost 10,000"; American Revolution Museum "nearly 10,000". Use: **"about 9,000 men"** (round). With them: field guns, ammunition, horses, cattle and provisions (NPS Saratoga: "field pieces, heavy ordnance, all ammunition, horses, cattle, and provisions"; HistoryNet: "most of their horses, equipment and supplies"). Confidence A for 9,000 as the round figure; B for any exact count (no muster exists; do not say 9,500).
18. **How the boats were gathered and by whom.** The quartermaster's order of item 16 (Hughes) plus Heath's boats from King's Bridge/Harlem manned by Hutchinson's Salem men; boats that already served the army from the Brooklyn ferry and New York. Johnston has no boat count; no source gives one. **Do not state a number of boats.** Types in use: "row-boats, flat-boats, whale-boats, pettiaugers, sloops, and sail-boats" (JOHNSTON p.222); HistoryNet: "rowboats, barges, canoes". Confidence A for the order, C for any count.
19. **Crews: Glover's 14th Continental (Marblehead) and Hutchinson's 27th Massachusetts (Salem).** "The boats were manned by Glover's and Hutchinson's men, and they went to work with sailor-like cheer and despatch" (JOHNSTON p.221). The embarkation point: Brooklyn Ferry, "the present Fulton Ferry", under Brig. Gen. William McDougall (JOHNSTON p.221; the marker at Fulton Ferry Landing says the army embarked there). Glover was a Marblehead merchant-mariner; his regiment had also ferried troops over to Brooklyn on 28 Aug and later rowed the Delaware crossing at Christmas (NPS Saratoga "A Hero of 1776"). Confidence A.
20. **Cover story and secrecy.** General Orders, 29 Aug: sick sent to the hospital across the river; troops from New Jersey were coming to relieve regiments, so all were to "parade with their arms, accoutrements and knapsacks at 7 o'clock ... and there wait for orders". Many subalterns and privates "thought only a few regiments were to go back" (Tilghman in JOHNSTON p.220 note). Troops left silently, "through mud and mire and not a ray of light visible" (Hitchcock's Rhode Islanders). Confidence A (JOHNSTON, Douglas order book).
21. **Timeline (best consensus).** Orders ~noon; regiments parade 19:00; boats manned 19:00-20:00 and embarkation begins "about 8 p.m." (HistoryNet; Encyclopedia.com 20:00; Wikipedia 23:00 for Glover is an outlier, but Johnston says "between seven and eight"). Militia and levies first. About 21:00 a north-east wind, pouring rain and the ebb tide made sail-boats useless; McDougall almost halted it. **Around 23:00 (11 p.m.) the NE wind died and a gentle south-west breeze rose** ("direct, easy, and expeditious", sea "smooth as glass", boats loaded within three inches of the water; JOHNSTON p.222; Encyclopedia.com "southwest wind ... light west breeze"). Mifflin's covering party stayed in the works until dawn. Last regiments left the lines "after it was fair day" (Lt. Col. Chambers, in JOHNSTON p.224); last boats left Brooklyn at about 6 a.m. (HistoryNet) and "before seven o'clock the entire force had crossed" (JOHNSTON p.224). Total roughly 9-11 hours (NPS: nine hours; Encyclopedia.com: about eleven). Use: "From dusk to dawn, about 9 hours". Confidence A for the shape; B for each clock time.
    - **Conflict: wind.** The "wind shift" is real in JOHNSTON, Encyclopedia.com and secondary sources (the wind that had pinned boats died at 11 p.m.). A different, popular version (Reed's biography) says the wind changed on the 29th and fog came the same day; Johnston, following Bancroft, shows this is wrong. WP-LI does not mention wind. Use the Johnston sequence.
22. **THE MIFFLIN BLUNDER.** About 2 a.m. Maj. Alexander Scammell, an aide, "mistook his orders" and started Mifflin's whole covering force (Hand's, Smallwood's, Haslet's, Shee's, Magaw's, Chester's regiments) marching toward the ferry; Washington met them on the road and ordered them back, warning that otherwise "the most disagreeable consequences" might follow; they faced about and re-occupied the works until dawn (JOHNSTON pp.222-223). Reported exchange: "General Mifflin, I am afraid you have ruined us by so unseasonably withdrawing" / "I did it by your order" (WP-LI; Encyclopedia.com; secondary). Conflict: WP-LI says ~04:00; JOHNSTON and Encyclopedia.com ~02:00; Encyclopedia.com adds the lines were empty "for nearly an hour". Use: "around 2 a.m. (some say 4)", and "reportedly". Confidence B on the event, C on the dialogue wording.
23. **THE FOG. It came late; it covered the rear guard, not the whole night.** The fog "began to settle over Long Island" just before dawn of 30 Aug; so dense it "effectually hid the American lines from the British pickets" (JOHNSTON p.223). Johnston's own correction (note p.223): "Washington did not, as often stated in popular accounts, take advantage of a fog to cover his retreat. More than half the army was over before the fog appeared; but it protected the covering party, and saved us the loss of considerable baggage and other material." Tallmadge (below), British historian Stedman ("a thick fog, which prevented the British troops from discovering the operations of the enemy"), Chambers ("a great fog arose") all agree on the dawn fog. **Do not show fog before ~4:30-5 a.m.** Confidence A.
24. **Tallmadge's account (Maj. Benjamin Tallmadge, 2nd Continental Light Dragoons; *Memoir*, written 1830, published 1858; verbatim as printed in BFT's primary-source page https://www.battlefields.org/learn/primary-sources/1776-fog-saved-part-our-army-being-captured and, for the first passage, in JOHNSTON p.223 note):**
    - "As the dawn of the next day approached, those of us who remained in the trenches became very anxious for our own safety, and when the dawn appeared, there were several regiments still on duty. At this time a very dense fog began to rise, and it seemed to settle in a peculiar manner over both encampments. I recollect this peculiar providential occurrence perfectly well; and so very dense was the atmosphere that I could scarcely discern a man at six yards' distance." (JOHNSTON)
    - "After all, the providential appearance of the fog saved a part of our army from being captured, and certainly myself, among others who formed the rear guard." (BFT)
    - "In the history of warfare I do not recollect a more fortunate retreat." (BFT page and a historycentral.com copy; **I did not see it in a scan of the 1858 book**, so quote it as Tallmadge's but double-check the printed page before burning it onto the screen.)
    - "I think I saw Gen. Washington on the ferry stairs when I stepped into one of the last boats that received the troops." (BFT). Note: Tallmadge does NOT say Washington was in the same boat.
    - After the mistaken march: "Col. Chester immediately faced to the right about and returned, where we tarried until the sun had risen, but the fog remained as dense as ever." (JOHNSTON note p.223)
    Confidence A (BFT reprint); the middle quote B.
25. **Washington among the last.** "General Washington," adds Lt. Col. Chambers, "saw the last over himself" (JOHNSTON p.224). Tallmadge saw him on the ferry stairs boarding one of the last boats. Popular accounts (Deseret, American Revolution Museum, MVE snippet: "according to eyewitnesses, George Washington was the last man to leave Brooklyn") go further; **use "among the last to leave"** (any claim about his sleep is unverified: omit). Confidence B for "among the last"; C for "last man / last boat".
26. **Losses in the evacuation.** Washington to Trumbull, 6 Sep: "I withdrew our troops ... without any loss of men and but little baggage" (JOHNSTON p.217). WP-LI: "no loss of life". Encyclopedia.com: three stragglers who stayed to plunder were lost, five heavy cannon stuck in the mud abandoned, "no boats collided, swamped, or capsized, and no lives were lost". JOHNSTON p.224 note: a British patrol "discovered the retreat of the Americans very soon after the latter left the lines ... pursuit was delayed until too late. One boat with four stragglers was taken by the enemy." **Honest hedge: "no men lost in the crossing; a few stragglers (3-4) and a handful of heavy guns left behind". Do not say "not a single man or gun lost."** Confidence A for "essentially no loss", C for exact straggler/gun counts (3 vs 4; 5 guns single source).
27. **"Muffled oars".** Reported by HistoryNet ("the oars were muffled with cloth"), NPS Saratoga "A Hero of 1776" (oars "muffled against the splashing of the water"), but **absent from Johnston 1878, Tallmadge and the other contemporary letters I read**; many modern popular accounts add it. Use only as "reportedly" or skip. Confidence C.
28. **Manhattan landing place.** Sources I could open do not name the Manhattan stairs. The Brooklyn Ferry's Manhattan end was at Peck Slip ("Peck Slip ferry"; not confirmed on a fetched page, so confidence B-) and the Fly Market at the foot of Maiden Lane was an adjoining stage; Johnston says only that the boats were ordered into "the east harbor of the City". Brief claim for screen: "landed on the Manhattan shore near Peck Slip" with a **hedge in the voiceover ("on the East River waterfront")**. The 1776 shore was ~100-220 m inland (west) of today's South Street (landfill). Confidence B-.
29. **British reaction.** "The Americans heard the pickaxe and shovel at the British works" as they withdrew (JOHNSTON p.224). A British patrol under Capt. John Montressor found the lines empty and reported at once; British occupied the forward positions around 04:30 (Encyclopedia.com: ~4 a.m. patrol, occupation half an hour later) but "pursuit was delayed until too late". HistoryNet: the British "were astonished when the fog lifted around mid-morning on 30 August and found the Americans gone." Conflict between "found at night" and "found at mid-morning": the forward lines were found early; the ferry and the last boats were not. Howe entered the empty works and found the lines "could not be taken by assault" (Montressor). Confidence B. Stirling's line "Howe never noticed the wind shift" (secondary quote) is C: skip.
30. **Contemporary verdicts.** Greene: "Considering the difficulties, it was the best effected retreat I ever read or heard of." "This evacuation is a masterpiece." (both JOHNSTON p.224). JOHNSTON adds that nationally the retreat "was generally associated with the defeat of the 27th". Confidence A (letters quoted).

## E. The aftermath that sets up the retreat

31. **New York abandoned: Kip's Bay, 15 Sep 1776.** About 12,000 British landed at Kip's Bay (WP-NYNJ); the militia broke and Washington withdrew north (BFT). BFT page on Brooklyn gives a conflicting "11 Sep" because it mixes the Staten Island peace conference (11 Sep) with the landing; 15 Sep is right (MVE, WP, BFT White Plains article). Confidence A.
32. **Harlem Heights, 16 Sep.** A skirmish in which three British light-infantry battalions were driven back; a small tactical win that restored morale (BFT; MVE snippet). Confidence A.
33. **White Plains, 28 Oct 1776.** Howe attacked with ~13,000 British and Hessians, took Chatterton Hill, and Washington withdrew north (BFT). Casualties were small (a secondary site gives ~217 American and ~233 British; unverified, skip). Confidence A for the date and ~13,000.
34. **Fort Washington, 16 Nov 1776.** About 3,000 Americans in the fort, **2,837 captured**; Americans 59 killed, 96 wounded; British and Hessians 84 killed, 374 wounded (WP "Battle of Fort Washington"; BFT "Forts Washington and Lee" gives 2,837 and ~3,000). Morris County NJ says Howe used ~8,000 men. Use: "almost 3,000 captured". Confidence A.
35. **Fort Lee, 20 Nov 1776.** Four days after Fort Washington, ~5,000 British and Hessians crossed the Hudson (Cornwallis) and landed below Fort Lee; its garrison of ~2,000 left in haste (BFT; Morris County NJ). Washington's army, which had been ~19,000, shrank to "fewer than 5,000 fit for duty" in the retreat (WP-NYNJ). Confidence B (the 5,000 is a general estimate; terms expired Nov 30: ~2,000 Maryland and New Jersey militia left on 30 Nov per a NJ source).
36. **Retreat across New Jersey.** Route: Fort Lee, Hackensack, Newark (Washington left 27 Nov), Elizabeth, Woodbridge, New Brunswick (reached the Raritan about 29 Nov, crossed and burned the bridge 1 Dec), Princeton (about 2 Dec), Trenton (Morris County NJ for the towns; the specific dates are from search snippets, to be double-checked). Cornwallis pursued closely, but Howe "delayed in attacking" (NJ Military Museum). **Dates for each town: confidence B-; use only "20 Nov-8 Dec" and the sequence of towns.**
37. **Delaware crossing: 7-8 Dec 1776.** NJ Military Museum: Washington moved across the Delaware into Pennsylvania on 7 and 8 December; Howe and Cornwallis entered Trenton on the 8th (search snippet) while other sources say crossings began as early as 2-3 Dec (rear guard last). The boats "for miles in both directions were destroyed or moved to the western shore" (WP, Washington's crossing of the Delaware). The commonly quoted "all boats within about 70 miles" figure is **not verified** (I found no source); use "every boat for miles". When the army reached McConkey's Ferry it had 4,000-6,000 men, "about 1,700 unfit for duty" (WP crossing article). Confidence B for the dates, B for "boats collected", C for any mileage.
38. **Congress leaves Philadelphia: 12 Dec 1776; sits in Baltimore from 20 Dec.** Left "fearing a British invasion" on 12 Dec (ALSSAR blog; WP says only "Congress had abandoned Philadelphia"). Reconvened in Henry Fite's house, Baltimore, 20 Dec 1776 - 27 Feb 1777 (Maryland State Archives) / 22 Feb (Wikipedia; Historical Society of Maryland), and on 27 Dec gave Washington extraordinary powers (Wikipedia "Henry Fite House"). Use: "12 Dec: Congress flees to Baltimore". Confidence A for 12 Dec (secondary) and 20 Dec; B for the departure from Baltimore date.
39. **Mirror motif (for the film).** The same boat-gathering skill (Glover's Marbleheaders) that saved the army at Brooklyn rowed it over the Delaware again on Christmas night (NPS "A Hero of 1776"); the boats were collected a second time on the Delaware. This is the planned set-up -> pay-off; both facts are sourced above.

## F. Myths and hedges (do NOT say)

- "Not a single man (or gun) was lost." Washington himself wrote "without any loss of men and but little baggage"; but a boat of stragglers was taken (Johnston), ~3 plunderers/stragglers and ~5 heavy guns (Encyclopedia.com) were left. Say "almost nothing lost".
- "The fog hid the whole night." It rose just before dawn of the 30th and shielded only the last regiments (Johnston, Bancroft). Over half the army was across before it.
- "Washington was in the very last boat." Sources say he was among the last (Chambers, Tallmadge on the stairs).
- "256 Marylanders died at the Old Stone House." Disputed (WP-LI citing Parry 2019).
- "Muffled oars", "73 warships", "boats collected from 70 miles", "Washington wept/sleepless 48 hours": not verified in the sources I could open.
- Long Island battle "Americans 6,000 vs British 20,000" etc.: the numbers vary by source; use "outnumbered".
- Howe "let them go on purpose" is an old suspicion; Howe told Parliament (1779) his reason. No reliable evidence of purpose; skip.
