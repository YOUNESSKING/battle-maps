### 2. For EACH of the three battles

#### Battle 1: Shenandoah Valley Campaign
- **Date and place:** March to June 1862. Shenandoah Valley, Virginia. **Latitude/Longitude:** 38.449° N, 78.868° W (Harrisonburg, valley center). **[VERIFIED]** `[S5]`
- **Forces:** 
  - *Confederate:* ~17,000 men (Army of the Valley). **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 235]`
  - *Union:* ~60,000 men split across three independent commands. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 236]`
- **Commanders:** 
  - *Confederate:* Maj. Gen. Thomas "Stonewall" Jackson (victorious). **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 237]`
  - *Union:* Maj. Gens. Nathaniel Banks, John Frémont, and James Shields (defeated). **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 238]`
- **Terrain:** A wide, fertile valley split perfectly down the middle for 50 miles by the imposing Massanutten Mountain, offering heavily shielded, parallel marching routes. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 240]`
- **Phase-by-phase movement list:**
  1. **May 8:** Jackson marches west to defeat a Union detachment at McDowell, preventing them from joining Banks. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 245]`
  2. **Mid-May:** Jackson utilizes the massive shield of Massanutten Mountain to hide his army's movement, marching rapidly north while the Union thinks he is elsewhere. **[PLANNED deception]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 250]`
  3. **May 23:** Jackson suddenly appears at Front Royal, crushing a Union garrison and exposing Banks's flank. **[PLANNED surprise]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 255]`
  4. **May 25:** Jackson violently routs Banks's main army at Winchester, driving them completely out of the Valley and across the Potomac River. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 260]`
  5. **Early June:** Two new Union armies (Frémont and Shields) converge to trap Jackson. Jackson rapidly retreats south, then turns to defeat them individually at Cross Keys (June 8) and Port Republic (June 9) before they can unite. **[PLANNED withdrawal and counterattack]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 280]`
- **What the enemy believed:** Union commanders believed Jackson was trapped, vastly outnumbered, and desperately fleeing, unaware he was using the mountain to dictate the engagements. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 275]`
- **What the general saw differently (Key Insight):** Jackson realized that Massanutten Mountain acted as a tactical screen; by moving faster than the Union armies could communicate, he could achieve local numerical superiority and "defeat in detail" a force three times his size. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 242]`
- **Result:** Strategic Confederate victory. Jackson tied down 60,000 Union troops, preventing them from joining the siege of Richmond. Casualties: Confederate ~2,500; Union ~7,000. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 290]`
- **Vivid details:** Jackson's infantry marched so fast and so far (often 25+ miles a day) that they earned the oxymoronic nickname "foot cavalry." **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 238]`

#### Battle 2: The Flank March to Second Bull Run
- **Date and place:** August 25–30, 1862. Manassas Junction, Virginia. **Latitude/Longitude:** 38.813° N, 77.521° W. **[VERIFIED]** `[S5]`
- **Forces:** 
  - *Confederate:* ~24,000 (Jackson's Left Wing). **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 150]`
  - *Union:* ~62,000 (Army of Virginia). **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 152]`
- **Commanders:** 
  - *Confederate:* Maj. Gen. Stonewall Jackson (victorious). **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 155]`
  - *Union:* Maj. Gen. John Pope (defeated). **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 156]`
- **Terrain:** The rolling Virginia Piedmont, defined by the Orange & Alexandria Railroad, which served as Pope's sole supply umbilical cord. **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 158]`
- **Phase-by-phase movement list:**
  1. **Aug 25–26:** Leaving Lee behind, Jackson force-marches 54 miles in 36 hours entirely around Pope's right flank. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 160]`
  2. **Aug 26, Night:** Jackson captures Manassas Junction, Pope's massive supply depot, placing his army directly in the Union rear. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 165]`
  3. **Aug 27:** Jackson's starving men feast on Union delicacies, then burn the remaining supplies to the ground. **[PLANNED outcome]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 170]`
  4. **Aug 28:** Jackson hides his force in woods along an unfinished railroad grade near the old Bull Run battlefield, waiting for Pope to blindly retreat. **[PLANNED deception]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 180]`
  5. **Aug 28, Evening:** Jackson deliberately attacks a passing Union column at Brawner's Farm to reveal his position, goading Pope into attacking his prepared defensive line. **[PLANNED bait]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 185]`
- **What the enemy believed:** Pope initially believed Jackson was retreating to the mountains; upon finding the depot burned, he believed he had Jackson trapped and unsupported. **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 190]`
- **What the general saw differently (Key Insight):** Jackson understood that severing the railroad would force Pope to abandon his river defenses and retreat blindly; by taking up a defensive position, Jackson anchored the enemy in place while Lee brought up the rest of the army to crush Pope's flank. **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 195]`
- **Result:** Decisive Confederate victory. Pope launched piecemeal attacks against Jackson's railroad grade until Longstreet's wing arrived and shattered the Union flank. Casualties (entire battle): Confederate ~8,300; Union ~13,800. **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 210]`
- **Vivid details:** Confederates at the railroad grade ran out of ammunition during the intense fighting and resorted to throwing heavy rocks at advancing Union soldiers to break their charges. **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 198]`

#### Battle 3: The Flank Attack at Chancellorsville
- **Date and place:** May 2, 1863. Chancellorsville, Virginia. **Latitude/Longitude:** 38.310° N, 77.635° W. **[VERIFIED]** `[S5]`
- **Forces:** 
  - *Confederate:* ~28,000 (Jackson's flanking column). **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 300]`
  - *Union:* ~12,000 (XI Corps on the right flank). **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 302]`
- **Commanders:** 
  - *Confederate:* Lt. Gen. Stonewall Jackson (victorious, but mortally wounded). **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 305]`
  - *Union:* Maj. Gen. Oliver O. Howard (routed). **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 306]`
- **Terrain:** The "Wilderness"—a dense, tangled, nearly impenetrable second-growth forest that made visibility and artillery placement almost impossible. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 310]`
- **Phase-by-phase movement list:**
  1. **May 2, Morning:** Lee divides his heavily outnumbered army. Jackson takes 28,000 men on a 12-mile stealth march across the Union front via hidden logging roads. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 315]`
  2. **5:15 PM:** Jackson's corps deploys in the woods perfectly perpendicular to the exposed, unfortified right flank of the Union XI Corps, who are eating dinner. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 340]`
  3. **5:30 PM:** Jackson attacks. The massive Confederate wave completely surprises and routes the Union flank, driving them miles back through the woods. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 345]`
  4. **9:00 PM:** The assault bogs down in the dark and dense thickets. Jackson rides ahead of his lines to reconnoiter for a rare night attack. **[PLANNED recon]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 380]`
  5. **9:30 PM:** Returning to his lines in the dark, Jackson is misidentified by a North Carolina regiment and shot three times by his own men. **[ACCIDENTAL friendly fire]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 385]`
- **What the enemy believed:** Union commander Joe Hooker believed Lee's army was retreating, interpreting brief glimpses of Jackson's sideways march as a flight south. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 320]`
- **What the general saw differently (Key Insight):** Jackson understood that the dense Wilderness negated the Union's massive numerical and artillery advantage; striking an unprotected flank from the woods would cause a domino-effect panic that sheer numbers couldn't fix. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 310]`
- **Result:** Massive Confederate tactical victory, but strategically devastating due to Jackson's death. Jackson's arm was amputated, and he died of pneumonia eight days later. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 450]`
- **Vivid details:** As Jackson lay bleeding in the dark, panicked litter-bearers dropped his stretcher twice in the chaotic brush while dodging Union artillery fire, severely worsening his injuries. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 395]`

### 3. The general's method

**Jackson's 3-Part Method:**
1. **Extreme Mobility:** Outmarch the enemy's expectations. Force infantry to march at cavalry speeds to achieve total operational surprise before the enemy can react. `[S1, p. 238]`
2. **Defeat in Detail:** Use geographical barriers (like Massanutten Mountain or the Wilderness) to isolate numerically superior armies and destroy them piece by piece. `[S1, p. 242]`
3. **The Deep Flank:** Never attack a prepared front if a flank is available. Willingly divide your forces to strike the enemy's rear supply lines or exposed edges. `[S3, p. 315]`

*Alternative phrasing 1:* Move faster than the enemy's intelligence, use terrain to screen your numbers, and always target the supply lines. 
*Alternative phrasing 2:* Weaponize infantry marching speed, strike isolated detachments before they combine, and exploit unprotected flanks to induce panic.

### 6. Common myths and errors to avoid
- **Myth:** Jackson got the nickname "Stonewall" because his command style was rigidly defensive.
  - **Correction:** He earned the nickname at First Bull Run for standing firm, but his actual command philosophy was the exact opposite of a static wall. He was a master of hyper-aggressive maneuver warfare, speed, and continuous flanking attacks. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 88]`
- **Myth:** The Confederate Army lost at Gettysburg entirely because Jackson was not there.
  - **Correction:** While "If Jackson were there" is the most famous counter-factual of the Civil War, modern historians note that Lee's orders at Gettysburg were vague, and Jackson's presence would not have magically solved the logistical, terrain, and numerical disadvantages the Confederates faced on Days 2 and 3. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 460]`
