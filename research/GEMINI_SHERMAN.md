#### Battle 1: Flanking Maneuvers of the Atlanta Campaign (Rocky Face Ridge to Jonesboro)
- **Date(s) and place:** May 7 – September 2, 1864. Northwestern Georgia (Dalton to Atlanta and Jonesboro). **Battlefield Coordinates:** 33.7490° N, 84.3880° W (Atlanta, GA) and 33.5215° N, 84.3538° W (Jonesboro battlefield) via Wikipedia / National Park Service CWSS. **[VERIFIED]** `[S7]`
- **Forces on both sides:**
  - *Union (Military Division of the Mississippi):* Three combined armies—Army of the Cumberland (Thomas), Army of the Tennessee (McPherson), and Army of the Ohio (Schofield).
    - **Range:** 98,797 present for duty on May 1, 1864 (Sherman's official return in *Memoirs* `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 25]`, comprising 88,188 infantry, 6,149 cavalry, 4,460 artillery with 254 guns) up to ~112,000 peak strength including June reinforcements (Castel `[S2, p. 112]`). **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 112; S5, Vol. 2, p. 25]`
  - *Confederate (Army of Tennessee):*
    - **Range:** ~45,000 to 50,000 initially at Dalton on May 1 (Johnston's memoirs claim ~42,856 infantry/artillery plus cavalry `[S2, p. 114]`), rising to 65,000–75,000 by late May after Leonidas Polk's corps and cavalry joined (Castel estimates 74,000 effective in late May `[S2, p. 190]`; Livermoor/OR estimates ~60,000–65,000 `[S6, Vol. 38, Pt. 3, p. 676]`). Equipped with ~144–187 artillery pieces. **[DISPUTED exact peak]** `[S2, p. 114, 190; S6, Vol. 38, Pt. 3, p. 676]`
- **Commanders on both sides:**
  - *Union:* Maj. Gen. William T. Sherman (Overall Commander, victorious); Maj. Gen. George H. Thomas (Army of the Cumberland, survived); Maj. Gen. James B. McPherson (Army of the Tennessee, **killed in action** outside Atlanta on July 22, 1864, replaced by Maj. Gen. Oliver O. Howard); Maj. Gen. John M. Schofield (Army of the Ohio, survived). **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 68, 405]`
  - *Confederate:* Gen. Joseph E. Johnston (Commander until **relieved of command** by President Jefferson Davis on July 17, 1864); Gen. John Bell Hood (Commander from July 17, defeated and forced to evacuate Atlanta); Lt. Gen. Leonidas Polk (Corps Commander, **killed in action** by a Union artillery shell at Pine Mountain on June 14, 1864); Lt. Gen. William J. Hardee (Corps Commander, survived). **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 276, 365]`
- **Terrain for the map:**
  - A rugged 100-mile corridor in northwest Georgia anchored by a single railroad: the **Western & Atlantic Railroad** running south-southeast from Chattanooga (35.0456° N, 85.3097° W) to Atlanta (33.7490° N, 84.3880° W).
  - Key obstacles running northeast-to-southwest across Sherman's path: **Rocky Face Ridge** (steep 800-foot quartzite ridge protecting Dalton, 34.77° N, 85.01° W, with **Snake Creek Gap** 15 miles to its south); the **Oostanaula River** at Resaca (34.57° N, 84.94° W); the **Etowah River** and **Allatoona Pass** (34.16° N, 84.71° W); **Kennesaw Mountain** (691-foot prominence 20 miles NW of Atlanta, 33.98° N, 84.58° W); the deep, rapid **Chattahoochee River** 8 miles NW of Atlanta; and the **Macon & Western Railroad** running south from Atlanta through **Jonesboro** (18 miles south of Atlanta, 33.52° N, 84.35° W). **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 120–125; S7]`
- **Phase-by-phase movement list:**
  1. **May 7–9, 1864 (Daylight):** Sherman pins Johnston's 50,000 men in front of the impregnable Rocky Face Ridge at Dalton using Thomas's and Schofield's armies (advancing south/southeast), while secretly sending McPherson's 24,000-man Army of the Tennessee 15 miles south on a concealed flanking march through Snake Creek Gap to strike the railroad at Resaca in Johnston's rear. **[PLANNED maneuver by Sherman; McPherson hesitates to cut the rail]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 140–155]`
  2. **May 12–15, 1864:** Threatened with encirclement, Johnston abandons Dalton at night and retreats 15 miles south to Resaca. Sherman shifts his entire 100,000-man force south to press Resaca, while sending Brig. Gen. Thomas Sweeny's division across the Oostanaula River at Lay's Ferry (3 miles southwest) on May 15 to outflank Johnston again, forcing Johnston to retreat south across the river that night. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 170–185]`
  3. **May 23–25, 1864:** Rather than attacking Johnston's strong bottleneck defense at Allatoona Pass, Sherman temporarily leaves the railroad, swings his armies 20 miles southwest across the Etowah River toward Dallas, Georgia (Battle of New Hope Church, May 25), forcing Johnston to abandon Allatoona Pass and slide southwest to block him. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 210–225]`
  4. **June 27, 1864 (08:00–11:00 AM):** After weeks of torrential rain and trench deadlock along the Kennesaw Mountain line, Sherman uncharacteristically orders a direct frontal assault by Thomas and McPherson uphill against entrenched Confederates. The assault fails with ~3,000 Union casualties. **[PLANNED frontal attack; tactical mistake by Sherman]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 305–320]`
  5. **July 2–9, 1864:** Returning to maneuver, Sherman shifts McPherson's army right (southwest) around Kennesaw toward the Chattahoochee River on July 2, forcing Johnston to evacuate Kennesaw Mountain that night. On July 8, Schofield's army makes a surprise amphibious crossing of the Chattahoochee at Sope Creek (upstream/northeast of Johnston's river line), unhinging the last natural barrier before Atlanta. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 330–345]`
  6. **July 17–28, 1864:** Jefferson Davis fires Johnston and appoints Gen. John Bell Hood. As Sherman approaches Atlanta from the north and east in three separated wings, Hood launches three ferocious counter-offensives: Peachtree Creek (July 20, north of city, 33.81° N, 84.40° W), Battle of Atlanta (July 22, east of city, 33.75° N, 84.33° W—where McPherson is killed), and Ezra Church (July 28, west of city, 33.75° N, 84.44° W). Union forces hold their breastworks and inflict ~13,000–15,000 casualties on Hood. **[PLANNED by Hood; repulsed by Union]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 365–435]`
  7. **August 25–30, 1864 (Night of Aug 25 start):** With Atlanta's earthworks too strong to storm, Sherman leaves Slocum's XX Corps at the Chattahoochee bridges and pulls 60,000 men out of their trenches north/west of Atlanta. He wheels his entire force 25 miles south around the west side of the city to sever Hood's last supply artery—the Macon & Western Railroad—near Rough and Ready and Jonesboro. **[PLANNED decisive maneuver]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 480–495]`
  8. **August 31 – September 2, 1864:** At the Battle of Jonesboro, Sherman's armies repulse Hardee's attack on Aug 31, and Thomas's XIV Corps storms the Confederate flank on Sept 1 while Union troops twist the railroad tracks into "Sherman's neckties." With his last rail lifeline severed, Hood blows up his ordnance trains on the night of Sept 1 and evacuates Atlanta; Union forces march in on September 2. **[PLANNED outcome]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 500–528]`
- **What the enemy believed vs. What the general saw differently (Key Insight):**
  - *Enemy belief:* Johnston believed Sherman was tethered to the Western & Atlantic Railroad and would have to hurl his men against prepared mountain trenches; later, Hood believed on August 26 that Sherman's disappearance from the Atlanta trenches meant Union cavalry raids had starved Sherman into retreating back across the Chattahoochee River `[S2, p. 484]`.
  - *Key Insight:* Sherman treated Thomas's heavy Army of the Cumberland as a giant defensive "anvil" to pin the enemy in place, while using McPherson's and Schofield's mobile armies as flanking "hammers" aimed not at enemy infantry, but at the single railroad track behind them—forcing the Confederates to choose between retreating or starving `[S1, p. 245; S5, Vol. 2, p. 18]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 245; S2, p. 484]`
- **Result as hard numbers:**
  - *Tactical & Strategic Result:* Union holds the field and captures Atlanta; decisive strategic victory that secures Lincoln's November 1864 re-election `[S2, p. 542]`.
  - *Casualties (Four-Month Campaign Total, Killed + Wounded + Captured/Missing):*
    - **Union:** **31,687** in Sherman's official report (4,423 killed, 22,822 wounded, 4,442 missing `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 134]`), up to **~37,000** when including cavalry and rear-guard losses (Castel `[S2, p. 555]`).
    - **Confederate:** **34,979** according to medical director Foard's partial returns + prisoners (3,044 killed, 18,952 wounded, 12,983 captured `[S2, p. 556]`), or **~35,000 to 38,000** total in modern scholarship (Castel `[S2, p. 557]`). **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 555–557; S5, Vol. 2, p. 134]`
- **Vivid, sourced details:**
  1. On June 14, 1864, atop Pine Mountain, Sherman personally spotted a cluster of Confederate generals through his field glasses and ordered Thomas to have an artillery battery fire three volleys at them to make them scatter; the third 3-inch Parrott shell tore directly through the chest of Confederate Lt. Gen. Leonidas Polk (an Episcopal bishop before the war), killing him instantly. **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 275; S5, Vol. 2, p. 53]`
  2. To feed 100,000 men and 35,000 animals down a single-track railroad over 100 miles from Chattanooga, Sherman's railroad engineers rebuilt blown bridges faster than Confederate raiders could burn them—prompting Confederates to joke that Sherman carried a duplicate tunnel and pre-built bridges in his pocket. **[TRADITION/CONTEMPORARY SOLDIER HUMOR recorded by Sherman]** `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 398]`
  3. On the night of September 1, 1864, as Hood evacuated Atlanta, his rear guard detonated 81 railcars loaded with gunpowder and artillery shells and 7 locomotives; the explosions shook the earth 20 miles south at Jonesboro, alerting Sherman that the city had fallen. **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 522; S5, Vol. 2, p. 108]`

#### Battle 2: The March to the Sea (Savannah Campaign)
- **Date(s) and place:** November 15 – December 21, 1864. From Atlanta across central Georgia to Savannah. **Battlefield Coordinates:** 32.0809° N, 81.0912° W (Savannah, GA) and 31.8894° N, 81.1939° W (Fort McAllister State Park) via Wikipedia / NPS. **[VERIFIED]** `[S7]`
- **Forces on both sides:**
  - *Union (Military Division of the Mississippi, stripped of sick/weak men):*
    - **Exact official strength (Sherman's Special Field Orders & Memoirs):** **62,204** men total—comprising **55,329 infantry**, **5,063 cavalry** (Kilpatrick's division), and **1,812 artillerymen** crewed on **65 guns** (organized into two wings: Right Wing / Army of the Tennessee under Howard, and Left Wing / Army of Georgia under Slocum). Accompanied by 2,500 supply wagons and 600 ambulances. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 40, 540; S5, Vol. 2, p. 176]`
  - *Confederate (Department of South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida):*
    - **Range:** ~8,000 to 13,000 scattered defenders initially—comprising ~3,500–5,000 cavalry under Maj. Gen. Joseph Wheeler, ~3,000 Georgia militia (old men and teenaged boys known as "Joe Brown's Pets") under Maj. Gen. Gustavus W. Smith, and ~10,000 garrison troops convened inside the Savannah defenses by mid-December under Lt. Gen. William J. Hardee (Trudeau `[S3, p. 52, 415]`). **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 52, 415]`
- **Commanders on both sides:**
  - *Union:* Maj. Gen. William T. Sherman (Overall Commander, victorious); Maj. Gen. Oliver O. Howard (Right Wing Commander); Maj. Gen. Henry W. Slocum (Left Wing Commander); Brig. Gen. Judson Kilpatrick (Cavalry Commander). **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 38–42]`
  - *Confederate:* Lt. Gen. William J. Hardee (Overall Departmental Commander, forced to evacuate Savannah); Maj. Gen. Joseph Wheeler (Cavalry Corps, failed to stop march); Maj. Gen. Gustavus W. Smith (Georgia Militia, defeated at Griswoldville); Maj. George W. Anderson (Commander at Fort McAllister, captured). **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 50–54, 430]`
- **Terrain for the map:**
  - A 285-mile southeasterly swath, 40 to 60 miles wide, across the rich agricultural heartland of central Georgia, bounded on the north/east by the **Savannah River** and on the south/west by the **Ocmulgee** and **Altamaha Rivers**.
  - Key waypoints: **Atlanta** (33.75° N, 84.39° W); **Macon** (80 miles SE of Atlanta, 32.84° N, 83.63° W, southern wing feint target); **Augusta** (140 miles E of Atlanta, 33.47° N, 81.97° W, northern wing feint target); **Milledgeville** (then the state capital of Georgia, centrally located between the two at 33.08° N, 83.23° W); **Griswoldville** (10 miles east of Macon, 32.87° N, 83.46° W); the **Ogeechee River**; and **Savannah** (32.08° N, 81.09° W), guarded on its flooded rice-plantation western land approaches by Hardee's trenches and on the Ogeechee River southern water approach by the massive earthen walls of **Fort McAllister** (31.89° N, 81.19° W). **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 60–65, 420; S7]`
- **Phase-by-phase movement list:**
  1. **November 12–15, 1864:** On Nov 12, Sherman orders his telegraph wires to the North cut after sending a final message to Thomas, burning the railroad bridges behind him. On Nov 15 (07:00 AM), after Union engineers demolish and burn Atlanta's foundries, depots, and rail yards, the 62,000-man army steps off in four parallel corps columns with only 20 days of hardbread rations in their wagons. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 75–92; S5, Vol. 2, p. 177]`
  2. **November 15–22, 1864 (The Dual Feint):** Howard's Right Wing (XV and XVII Corps) marches south down the railroad toward **Macon**, joined by Kilpatrick's cavalry demonstrating against Macon's defenses, while Slocum's Left Wing (XIV and XX Corps) marches east down the Georgia Railroad toward **Augusta**. Confederate commanders split their forces to defend Macon and Augusta, leaving the center (the state capital at Milledgeville) completely undefended. **[PLANNED operational deception]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 110–145]`
  3. **November 22, 1864 (Afternoon – Battle of Griswoldville):** Near Macon at Griswoldville, Georgia militia under Brig. Gen. Pleasant Philips rashly launch three frontal charges across an open field against Brig. Gen. Charles Walcutt's single Union brigade (armed with repeating Spencer rifles behind a rail barricade). The militia is slaughtered (~650 Confederate casualties vs. 62 Union). **[UNPLANNED Confederate blunder; Union tactical defense]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 185–198]`
  4. **November 23 – December 9, 1864:** Slocum's wing occupies Milledgeville on Nov 23 (where Union soldiers hold a mock legislative session repealing Georgia's secession ordinance), and both wings converge east, carving a 60-mile-wide path of economic destruction toward Savannah. Daily "foraging parties" (50 men per brigade, nicknamed "Bummers") fan out 5 miles on each flank under Special Field Orders No. 120 to seize corn, sweet potatoes, cattle, hogs, and mules while burning cotton gins and twisting 300 miles of the Georgia Central Railroad. **[PLANNED logistical & psychological warfare]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 210–255; S5, Vol. 2, p. 175, 182]`
  5. **December 10, 1864:** Sherman's 60,000 men arrive before Savannah, only to find Hardee's 10,000 Confederates entrenched behind flooded rice fields and causeways. With his 20 days of wagon rations running low, Sherman needs to open the Ogeechee River to reach Rear Adm. John Dahlgren's US Navy supply fleet waiting off the coast—which is blocked by **Fort McAllister**. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 395–410]`
  6. **December 13, 1864 (4:45 PM – 5:00 PM):** Sherman watches from the roof of a rice mill across the Ogeechee River as Brig. Gen. William B. Hazen's 2nd Division (XV Corps, 4,000 men) storms Fort McAllister through buried landmines (torpedoes) and abatis. In a vicious 15-minute hand-to-hand assault, Hazen captures the fort and its 230-man garrison, linking Sherman to the US Navy fleet that night. **[PLANNED assault]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 425–440; S5, Vol. 2, p. 198]`
  7. **December 20–21, 1864:** As Sherman prepares heavy siege guns from the Navy and maneuvers to cut the last escape route north into South Carolina, Hardee builds a crude pontoon bridge out of rice barges across the Savannah River on the night of Dec 20 and slips his 10,000 troops out of the city. Union troops enter Savannah at dawn on Dec 21. **[PLANNED Union siege pressure; Confederate escape]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 480–505]`
- **What the enemy believed vs. What the general saw differently (Key Insight):**
  - *Enemy belief:* Confederate President Jefferson Davis publicly boasted in speeches at Macon and Columbia in autumn 1864 that Sherman was doomed to experience "Napoleon's retreat from Moscow" if he marched deep into Georgia while Hood attacked Tennessee `[S1, p. 308; S5, Vol. 2, p. 145]`.
  - *Key Insight:* Using exact 1860 US Census agricultural tables for every county in Georgia, Sherman calculated mathematically that the Georgia countryside held millions of bushels of corn and hundreds of thousands of livestock—meaning a moving 60,000-man army could feast off the land without a supply train, and that splitting into two wings would paralyze Confederate defenders who could never tell which city was the true target `[S1, p. 315; S5, Vol. 2, p. 178]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 308, 315; S5, Vol. 2, p. 145, 178]`
- **Result as hard numbers:**
  - *Tactical & Strategic Result:* Complete Union tactical and strategic victory; Savannah captured along with 250 heavy siege guns and 25,000 bales of cotton; the Confederacy's eastern breadbasket and rail network between Virginia and the West was permanently severed `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 231]`.
  - *Casualties (Nov 15 – Dec 21, 1864, Killed + Wounded + Missing):*
    - **Union:** **1,338** total in Sherman's official report (103 killed, 428 wounded, 278 missing during the march, plus Hazen's 134 at Fort McAllister and skirmishes `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 225]`), or **~2,100** in compiled modern breakdowns (Trudeau `[S3, p. 521]`).
    - **Confederate:** **~2,300 to 3,000** killed/wounded/captured (including ~650 at Griswoldville, 230 at Fort McAllister, and ~1,300+ captured overall `[S3, p. 522]`).
    - **Economic Destruction:** Sherman officially estimated **$100,000,000** in damage in his report to Washington—stating "$20,000,000 of which inured to our advantage, and the remainder is simple waste and destruction," including 300+ miles of railroad twisted and 13,000+ head of cattle and 9.5 million pounds of corn seized `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 225–228]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 521–522; S5, Vol. 2, p. 225–228]`
- **Vivid, sourced details:**
  1. While Sherman's army was completely out of contact for 32 days, President Abraham Lincoln told nervous White House visitors: *"I know what hole he went in at, but I can't tell what hole he will come out of."* **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 328; S3, p. 380]`
  2. At Griswoldville on November 22, when Union soldiers walked over the field after repulsing the Georgia Militia, they wept upon finding the dead bodies of gray-haired old men in their sixties lying beside boys barely fourteen years old who had been thrown against veteran Union regulars. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 197]`
  3. On December 22, 1864, Sherman sent his famous telegraph to President Lincoln: *"I beg to present you as a Christmas-gift the city of Savannah, with one hundred and fifty heavy guns and plenty of ammunition, also about twenty-five thousand bales of cotton."* **[VERIFIED]** `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 231; S6, Vol. 44, p. 783]`

#### Battle 3: The Carolinas Campaign (Savannah to Bentonville and Bennett Place)
- **Date(s) and place:** January 19 – April 26, 1865. From Savannah, GA, north through Columbia, SC, to Bentonville and Durham (Bennett Place), NC. **Battlefield Coordinates:** 34.0007° N, 81.0348° W (Columbia, SC); 35.3054° N, 78.3242° W (Bentonville Battlefield State Historic Site); and 36.0289° N, 78.9756° W (Bennett Place State Historic Site) via Wikipedia / NC Historic Sites. **[VERIFIED]** `[S7]`
- **Forces on both sides:**
  - *Union (Military Division of the Mississippi):*
    - **Exact official strength on Feb 1, 1865 (Sherman's Memoirs):** **60,079** men leaving Savannah—comprising **53,923 infantry**, **4,438 cavalry**, and **1,718 artillery** with **68 guns** `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 256]`. By late March at Goldsboro, NC, joined by Schofield's XXIII Corps and Terry's X Corps from the coast, swelling Sherman's total command to **88,948** men `[S4, p. 285; S5, Vol. 2, p. 308]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 285; S5, Vol. 2, p. 256, 308]`
  - *Confederate (Fragments of Dept. of SC/GA/FL, Army of Tennessee remnants, and Bragg's/Hardee's troops consolidated under Gen. Joseph E. Johnston on Feb 22, 1865):*
    - **Range:** ~20,000–25,000 widely scattered across South Carolina in February (Hardee had ~12,000 in Charleston, Wheeler/Wade Hampton ~5,000 cavalry, Beauregard ~4,000 `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 265]`). At the **Battle of Bentonville (March 19–21)**, Johnston concentrated **~16,895 to 21,000** effectives against Slocum's 16,000–20,000 Left Wing before Sherman's Right Wing arrived (Bradley gives ~19,000–21,000 for Johnston `[S4, p. 142]`; Johnston himself claimed only 14,100–15,000 infantry/artillery `[S4, p. 143]`). **[DISPUTED Confederate strength at Bentonville]** `[S4, p. 142–143; S5, Vol. 2, p. 265]`
- **Commanders on both sides:**
  - *Union:* Maj. Gen. William T. Sherman (Overall Commander, victorious); Maj. Gen. Oliver O. Howard (Right Wing); Maj. Gen. Henry W. Slocum (Left Wing); Maj. Gen. John M. Schofield (Army of the Ohio, joined in March). **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 25]`
  - *Confederate:* Gen. P.G.T. Beauregard (Initial nominal commander in Feb, outmaneuvered); Gen. Joseph E. Johnston (Reappointed overall commander by Robert E. Lee on Feb 22, 1865; defeated at Bentonville and formally surrendered the army on April 26); Lt. Gen. William J. Hardee; Lt. Gen. Wade Hampton (Cavalry). **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 28–32]`
- **Terrain for the map:**
  - A 425-mile march directly *across the grain* of all major coastal river systems during the heaviest winter rains in twenty years.
  - Key obstacles: The flooded **Savannah River**; the **Salkehatchie River** swamps (where water spread 1 to 3 miles wide and 4 feet deep, 33.03° N, 81.10° W); the North and South Forks of the **Edisto River** protecting the South Carolina Railroad at **Orangeburg** (33.49° N, 80.86° W); the **Congaree** and **Broad Rivers** converging at the state capital of **Columbia, SC** (34.00° N, 81.03° W); the coastal fortress city of **Charleston, SC** (100 miles SE of Columbia, 32.78° N, 79.93° W); the **Pee Dee** and **Cape Fear Rivers** at **Fayetteville, NC** (35.05° N, 78.88° W); the wooded crossroads of **Bentonville, NC** (35.31° N, 78.32° W); the rail junction of **Goldsboro, NC** (35.38° N, 77.99° W); and the farmhouse of **James Bennitt (Bennett Place)** near Durham Station, NC (36.03° N, 78.98° W). **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 15–22; S5, Vol. 2, p. 258–280; S7]`
- **Phase-by-phase movement list:**
  1. **Late January – February 2, 1865:** Repeating his dual-feint geometry, Sherman sends Howard's Right Wing by sea/land via Beaufort to threaten **Charleston, SC**, on the right, while Slocum's Left Wing threatens **Augusta, GA**, on the left—pinning Hardee's 12,000 men in Charleston and D.H. Hill's forces in Augusta. **[PLANNED operational deception]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 255–265]`
  2. **February 2–3, 1865 (Battle of Rivers' Bridge on the Salkehatchie):** Instead of attacking Charleston or Augusta, both wings drive north into the flooded Salkehatchie Swamp. At Rivers' Bridge (Feb 3), Maj. Gen. Francis P. Blair's XVII Corps soldiers wade for hours through chest-deep, ice-cold black swamp water under fire to flank Maj. Gen. Lafayette McLaws's Confederate division, breaking the river line in 24 hours. **[PLANNED tactical flanking]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 268–270]`
  3. **February 7–15, 1865:** Union pioneer corps fell tens of thousands of pine and cypress trees, laying over 400 miles of "corduroy roads" (logs tied side-by-side over bottomless mud) to haul 2,500 wagons north. Sherman cuts the South Carolina Railroad at Midway (Feb 7) and Orangeburg (Feb 12), isolating Charleston from the interior. **[PLANNED engineering]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 365; S5, Vol. 2, p. 272]`
  4. **February 17–18, 1865:** Sherman's army captures **Columbia**, the capital of South Carolina. Simultaneously, completely cut off from the rear without a single shot fired against its harbor forts, Hardee is forced to evacuate **Charleston** on Feb 17. That night, high winds, smoldering cotton bales ignited by Wade Hampton's retreating Confederates, and drunken Union soldiers burn one-third of Columbia to the ground. **[PLANNED capture; ACCIDENTAL/DISPUTED conflagration]** `[S4, p. 18; S5, Vol. 2, p. 279–288]`
  5. **March 11–16, 1865 (Fayetteville to Averasboro):** Crossing into North Carolina, Sherman captures Fayetteville (destroying the Confederate arsenal) and marches toward his coastal supply link at Goldsboro in two separated wings. On March 16 at **Averasboro**, Hardee conducts a stubborn delaying action with 6,000 men against Slocum's Left Wing, intentionally widening the physical gap between Sherman's Left and Right Wings. **[PLANNED delaying action by Hardee]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 95–118]`
  6. **March 19, 1865 (Battle of Bentonville – Day 1):** Gen. Joseph E. Johnston secretly concentrates ~19,000–21,000 Confederates in a hook-shaped ambush at Bentonville to crush Slocum's isolated XIV and XX Corps (~16,000 engaged). At 2:45 PM, Hardee and Hoke smash into the Union XIV Corps left flank, routing two divisions; however, Brig. Gen. William P. Carlin's and James D. Morgan's veteran brigades form an island defense and hold off five Confederate attacks until dusk, while Slocum rushes couriers to Sherman. **[PLANNED Confederate ambush; Union tactical stand]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 155–215]`
  7. **March 20–21, 1865 (Bentonville – Days 2 & 3):** Sherman rapidly counter-marches Howard's Right Wing overnight to Bentonville, surrounding Johnston on three sides with 60,000 men. On March 21, Union Maj. Gen. Joseph A. Mower leads a reckless two-brigade charge through a swamp directly into Johnston's rear, nearly capturing Johnston's headquarters and his sole escape bridge over Mill Creek—before Sherman orders Mower to halt and pull back rather than risk a bloody general battle when Goldsboro and Schofield's reinforcements are one day's march away. Johnston retreats that night. **[UNPLANNED aggressive charge by Mower; PLANNED operational restraint by Sherman]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 240–272; S5, Vol. 2, p. 304]`
  8. **March 23 – April 26, 1865:** Sherman unites with Schofield at Goldsboro (89,000 men total), captures Raleigh on April 13, and following Lee's surrender at Appomattox (April 9) and Lincoln's assassination (April 14), accepts Johnston's final surrender of **89,270** Confederate troops across four states (Florida, Georgia, South and North Carolina) at **Bennett Place** on April 26, 1865. **[PLANNED strategic victory]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 285, 385–410]`
- **What the enemy believed vs. What the general saw differently (Key Insight):**
  - *Enemy belief:* Confederate commanders (Hardee and Beauregard) believed that Charleston was Sherman's primary target and that the winter floods of the Salkehatchie and Edisto swamps made an inland winter march to Columbia physically impossible `[S5, Vol. 2, p. 258, 396]`.
  - *Key Insight:* Sherman knew that if he marched inland to Columbia and severed Charleston's rail lines from behind, "Charleston would fall like a ripe pear" without losing a single soldier assaulting its coastal forts—and he trusted his veteran Midwestern woodsmen to build log roads faster than the Confederates could fell trees to block them `[S1, p. 358; S5, Vol. 2, p. 258]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 358; S5, Vol. 2, p. 258, 396]`
- **Result as hard numbers:**
  - *Tactical & Strategic Result:* Union holds the field at Bentonville and completes the 425-mile march; triggers the largest troop surrender of the American Civil War at Bennett Place (89,270 Confederates vs. 28,231 at Appomattox) `[S4, p. 412]`.
  - *Casualties (Bentonville, March 19–21, 1865, Killed + Wounded + Missing):*
    - **Union:** **1,527** (194 killed, 1,112 wounded, 221 missing `[S4, p. 278; S5, Vol. 2, p. 305]`). (Total Union casualties for the entire Jan–March Carolinas march were ~4,200).
    - **Confederate:** **2,606** at Bentonville (239 killed, 1,694 wounded, 673 missing `[S4, p. 278]`), followed by **89,270** paroled in the final surrender under the Bennett Place convention `[S4, p. 412]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 278, 412; S5, Vol. 2, p. 305]`
- **Vivid, sourced details:**
  1. During the crossing of the Salkehatchie Swamp in February 1865, Union soldiers marched for days through icy black water up to their armpits while holding their leather cartridge boxes pinned atop the points of their fixed bayonets above their heads to keep their gunpowder dry. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 363; S5, Vol. 2, p. 269]`
  2. When Confederate Gen. Joseph E. Johnston heard how Sherman's engineers had corduroyed hundreds of miles of flooded South Carolina swamps in mid-winter at 10 miles a day, he declared: *"I made up my mind that there had been no such army in existence since the days of Julius Caesar."* **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 365; S5, Vol. 2, p. 396]`
  3. On April 17, 1865, minutes before boarding the train to meet Johnston at Bennett Place for peace talks, a telegraph operator handed Sherman a cipher dispatch revealing that President Abraham Lincoln had been assassinated; Sherman swore the operator to total secrecy so his 89,000 furious soldiers wouldn't burn Raleigh to the ground, and handed the dispatch directly to a horrified Johnston inside the Bennett farmhouse. **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 332–336; S5, Vol. 2, p. 347]`

### 3. The general's method

**Sherman's 3-Part Method:**
1. **Put the Enemy on the "Horns of a Dilemma":** Never march toward a single objective; always advance in wide, separated wings that threaten two vital cities at once (Macon/Augusta; Charleston/Augusta), paralyzing the enemy's defense until you strike the undefended space between them. `[S1, p. 315; S5, Vol. 2, p. 258]`
2. **Attack the Artery, Not the Shield:** Refuse to hurl infantry against entrenched mountain lines or coastal forts; pin the enemy's front with an anvil and swing wide around his flank or rear to twist his railroad lifelines into corkscrews, forcing him to abandon impregnable fortresses without a siege. `[S2, p. 495; S5, Vol. 2, p. 398]`
3. **Weaponize Logistics and Psychology:** Sever your own vulnerable supply tether to live off the enemy's wealthiest farmland, using combat engineering to cross "impassable" terrain and bringing the material destruction of war directly to the enemy's interior to break their collective will to fight. `[S3, p. 210; S5, Vol. 2, p. 225]`

- *Alternative phrasing 1:* Threaten two targets to blind the defender, sever the rail arteries instead of storming the trenches, and destroy the enemy's economy to shatter his will.
- *Alternative phrasing 2:* Pin with the anvil and flank with the hammer, turn the enemy's geography into your own supply depot, and conquer impossible terrain with the axe and the spade.

### 6. Common myths and errors to avoid

- **Myth:** Sherman screamed *"War is hell!"* as a battle cry while burning Atlanta in 1864.
  - **Accurate Version:** Sherman never used those words during the Civil War. He delivered the line 14 years *after* the war (June 19, 1879) to young cadets at the Michigan Military Academy—not as a boast of brutality, but as a somber warning to boys who had never seen combat: *"There is many a boy here today who looks on war as all glory, but, boys, it is all hell."* During the war itself, his documented phrase in his Sept 12, 1864 letter to the Mayor of Atlanta was *"War is cruelty, and you cannot refine it."* **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 430; S5, Vol. 2, p. 126]`
- **Myth:** Sherman burned the entire city of Atlanta to the ground out of personal malice against civilians, leaving nothing standing.
  - **Accurate Version:** First, the massive explosions and fires of September 1, 1864, were set by Confederate General John Bell Hood's retreating troops detonating 81 railcars of ammunition. Second, when Sherman burned Atlanta on November 14–15, his official engineering orders targeted rail yards, machine shops, foundries, and warehouses; while unauthorized fires ordered by rowdy soldiers spread to commercial blocks, roughly 400 residences, virtually all churches, and the hospital survived. **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 522; S3, p. 80–90]`
- **Myth:** Sherman's March to the Sea was a bloody slaughter of Confederate soldiers and civilians.
  - **Accurate Version:** Sherman's philosophy of "hard war" explicitly targeted *property, infrastructure, and food* instead of human lives, in order to end the war faster without another Chickamauga or Cold Harbor. Across the entire 36-day, 285-mile march of 62,000 men through Georgia, total combat deaths on both sides combined were fewer than 500 men, and violent crimes against civilians' persons were remarkably rare compared to European wars. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 520–525]`
- **Myth:** Robert E. Lee's surrender at Appomattox Court House on April 9, 1865, was the largest surrender of the Civil War and ended the conflict.
  - **Accurate Version:** Lee only surrendered the 28,231 remaining men of the Army of Northern Virginia. Seventeen days later (April 26, 1865), Joseph E. Johnston surrendered **89,270** Confederate soldiers across the Carolinas, Georgia, and Florida to Sherman at Bennett Place—making Sherman's surrender over three times larger than Grant's at Appomattox. **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 412]`
