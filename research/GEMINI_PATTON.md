### Move 3 Assessment (Oppenheim vs. Sicily vs. Moselle/Metz)
Stick with **Oppenheim (Move 3)** — it delivers a textbook operational surprise, a clear rivalry victory over Montgomery, and the legendary bridging-and-urinating visual payoff, whereas **Metz/Moselle** was a grinding three-month attrition slog that contradicts Patton’s "lightning war" brand, and **Sicily** (though dramatic) breaks the 1944–45 Third Army narrative arc and already overlaps with Lucian Truscott (Idea 16) and Terry Allen (Idea 19).

### Verification of Specific Requested Claims (December 19, 1944, Verdun Conference)

*   **The "48 hours" claim to Eisenhower on December 19, 1944:** **[DISPUTED / POPULAR CONDENSATION OF 72 HOURS]** `[S1, p. 193; S2, p. 600; S3, p. 363]`
    *   *The Primary Record:* The Verdun conference took place at **11:00 AM on Tuesday, December 19, 1944**. When Eisenhower asked when Patton could counterattack toward Bastogne, Patton writes in his diary/memoirs (*War As I Knew It*): *"I replied, on the morning of December 22, with three divisions: the 4th Armored, the 26th, and the 80th."* `[S1, p. 193]`. Omar Bradley (*A Soldier's Story*) records the exact same date: *"On the morning of December 22—with three divisions"* `[S8, p. 469]`.
    *   *Why it is Disputed:* From 11:00 AM on December 19 to 06:00 AM on December 22 is **67 hours (nearly 72 hours / 3 days)**. However, popular accounts, the 1970 film *Patton*, and some secondary summaries say "48 hours" because only two full calendar days (Dec 20 and Dec 21) sat between the meeting and the attack, OR because Patton offered that he could attack *sooner* (within 48 hours, on Dec 21) with two divisions, which Eisenhower rejected as too weak, ordering him to wait until Dec 22 so all three divisions could strike together `[S1, p. 193; S3, p. 363]`. In your script, state that he stunned the room by promising to disengage an entire army and counterattack with three divisions by the morning of December 22—less than 72 hours away `[S1, p. 193]`.
*   **The exact "Play ball" wording:** **[DISPUTED — CONFLATED WITH NOVEMBER 7, 1944]** `[S1, p. 166, 193; S4, p. 350; S9, p. 613]`
    *   *The December 19, 1944 Codes:* Before driving from Nancy to Verdun on the morning of December 19, Patton and his Chief of Staff, Maj. Gen. Hugh "Hap" Gay, established three pre-arranged code words for a telephone call from Verdun so German wiretappers wouldn't understand the axis of attack: **"Cent"** (attack toward Diekirch), **"Nickel"** (attack from Arlon toward Bastogne), and **"Dime"** (attack toward Luxemburg/Echternach) `[S1, p. 192; S9, p. 613]`. When the Verdun meeting ended, Patton phoned Gay and gave the code word for the Arlon–Bastogne axis (**"Nickel"**, along with moving the 26th Division to Arlon and the 80th to Luxembourg) `[S1, p. 193; S9, p. 613]`.
    *   *Where "Play ball" Actually Comes From:* Patton *did* use the exact two-word telephone code **"Play ball"** to launch a massive Third Army offensive—but according to the US Army Official History (*The Lorraine Campaign*, by Hugh M. Cole) and Patton's own memoirs, he used **"Play ball"** six weeks earlier on the night of **November 7, 1944**, when Bradley visited his headquarters and authorized the XII Corps November offensive across the Seille River during pouring rain `[S1, p. 166; S4, p. 350]`. Popular histories and biographers (such as Ladislas Farago) later conflated the November 7 "Play ball" phone call with the December 19 Verdun "Nickel/Dime" phone call `[S9, p. 613]`.

Patton promises to attack with three divisions on the morning of December 22. He immediately phones Chief of Staff Maj. Gen. Hap Gay with the pre-arranged code word (**"Nickel"**), and 133,000 vehicles begin rolling north night and day under strict radio silence across icy roads—driving 70 to 100 miles with headlights on at night despite Luftwaffe strafing. **[PLANNED logistical miracle]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 193–195; S3, p. 363; S9, p. 613]`
  3. **December 20–21, 1944 (Encirclement of Bastogne):** As Patton's columns race north toward staging areas at Arlon and Luxembourg, Lüttwitz's panzers cut the last road into Bastogne on Dec 20, trapping McAuliffe's 11,000 men inside a 15-mile circular perimeter. **[PLANNED by Germans]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 350–358]`
  4. **December 22, 1944 (06:00 AM III Corps Attack & "Nuts!"):** Exactly on schedule at 06:00 AM on Dec 22, Millikin's III Corps attacks north along a 20-mile front from Arlon toward Bastogne: Gaffey's **4th Armored Division** (divided into CCB on the left, CCA in the center on the main Arlon–Bastogne highway, and CCR on the right) leads on the west, **26th Infantry Division** in the center, and **80th Infantry Division** on the right toward Ettelbruck. At 11:30 AM that same morning inside Bastogne, four Germans under a white flag demand the 101st Airborne's surrender; McAuliffe replies in writing: *"To the German Commander, N U T S !"* **[PLANNED attack]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 374–378, 512–518]`
  5. **December 23–24, 1944 (Clear Skies & Brutal Fighting at the Sûre River and Chaumont):** After Patton orders Third Army Chaplain Col. James H. O'Neill to compose and distribute the famous "Weather Prayer" on Dec 22, a Russian high-pressure front sweeps away the fog on the morning of Dec 23, allowing C-47s to drop 144 tons of ammunition/supplies into Bastogne and P-47 Thunderbolts to hammer German positions. However, the German **5th Fallschirmjäger Division** fights fiercely from stone villages and blown bridges along the Sûre River; on Dec 23–24, 4th Armored's CCA and CCB are bloodily repulsed at Warnach and **Chaumont** (losing 11 Sherman tanks and 65 men at Chaumont). **[ACCIDENTAL weather break; PLANNED air support; German tactical delay]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 198–200; S3, p. 520–538]`
  6. **December 25, 1944 (Christmas Day Shift to the West Flank):** Realizing the direct Arlon–Bastogne highway (CCA) is heavily mined and blocked, Patton and Gaffey shift the weight of the 4th Armored Division's **Combat Command R (CCR)** west of the main highway toward a secondary road running through **Remoifosse** and **Assenois** (southwest of Bastogne). **[PLANNED tactical shift]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 542–546]`
  7. **December 26, 1944 (3:15 PM – 4:50 PM: Abrams Breaks the Siege at Assenois):** At 3:15 PM on Dec 26, **Lt. Col. Creighton Abrams** (37th Tank Battalion) and Lt. Col. George Jaques (53rd Armored Infantry Battalion), running short of infantry and facing fortified German paratroopers in Assenois, decide not to clear the woods systematically—instead ordering four batteries of artillery to drop a continuous rolling barrage on Assenois while **Capt. William Dwight's C Company** charges straight down the road through the falling artillery shells at top speed. **[PLANNED tactical audacity by Abrams]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 547–550]`
  8. **December 26, 1944 (4:50 PM Link-Up):** The lead Sherman M4A3E2 "Jumbo" assault tank (**"Cobra King"**, commanded by **1st Lt. Charles P. Boggess**) blasts through Assenois despite German troops throwing Teller mines onto the road behind it, reaches the 101st Airborne's outer foxholes at **4:50 PM**, and shakes hands with engineers of the 326th Airborne Engineer Battalion (and 20 minutes later with Gen. McAuliffe)—officially breaking the siege of Bastogne. **[PLANNED outcome]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 550–552]`
- **What the enemy believed vs. What the general saw differently (Key Insight):**
  - *Enemy belief:* Hitler, Model, and Manteuffel believed that an entire American army actively engaged in winter combat in the Saar could not possibly disengage, turn 90 degrees across perpendicular road networks, and organize a multi-division counteroffensive in less than 10 to 14 days `[S3, p. 360]`.
  - *Key Insight:* Patton understood that staff preparation *before* orders arrive is the secret to operational speed; by halting his eastward attack on Dec 18, pre-drafting three coded contingency plans with his staff, and moving divisions sequentially with headlights blazing at night, he hit the German southern shoulder days before German intelligence thought Third Army could even reach the starting line `[S1, p. 191–195; S9, p. 612]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 191–195; S3, p. 360; S9, p. 612]`
- **Result as hard numbers:**
  - *Tactical & Strategic Result:* Decisive US tactical and strategic victory; the siege of Bastogne was broken on Dec 26, the southern shoulder of the Bulge was crushed, and Hitler's last strategic reserve on the Western Front was destroyed `[S3, p. 610]`.
  - *Casualties & Logistics (Relief of Bastogne & Ardennes Pivot):*
    - **Logistics of the Pivot (Dec 18–31, 1944, Patton's official figures):** Third Army moved **133,178 motor vehicles** over an average distance of 100+ miles across icy roads (totaling **4,500,000 vehicle-miles**) and shifted **419,581 tons of supplies**, putting 6 divisions into combat within days `[S1, p. 211]`.
    - **US Casualties Inside Bastogne (101st Airborne, Dec 19 – Jan 6):** **341 killed**, **1,691 wounded**, and **516 missing** (~2,548 total, plus ~500 casualties in CCB 10th Armored) `[S3, p. 475]`.
    - **US Relieving Force Casualties (4th Armored Division, Dec 22–26):** **~1,000+ casualties** and ~40–50 medium tanks lost breaking through the corridor; across the entire Ardennes campaign (Dec 19 – Jan 29), Patton's Third Army suffered **~50,630 total casualties** (killed, wounded, missing, and non-battle trench foot/frostbite) while inflicting over **113,000** estimated German casualties/prisoners `[S1, p. 235; S3, p. 552]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 211, 235; S3, p. 475, 552]`
- **Vivid, sourced details:**
  1. In early December 1944 (confirmed by historian Rick Atkinson as Dec 8, and repeated via distribution of 250,000 printed cards to every soldier in Third Army on Dec 22), Patton summoned Third Army Head Chaplain Col. James H. O'Neill and ordered a custom prayer: *"Chaplain, I want you to publish a prayer for good weather. I'm tired of these soldiers having to fight mud and flood as well as Germans."* O'Neill wrote the prayer (*"Almighty and most merciful Father, we humbly beseech Thee, of Thy great goodness, to restrain these immoderate rains..."*), and when the skies turned crystal clear on December 23, Patton summoned O'Neill and pinned a **Bronze Star Medal** on his chest on the spot. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 199; S7, p. 442]`
  2. As Capt. William Dwight's lead Sherman tanks charged through the village of Assenois at 4:30 PM on December 26, the pace was so fast that fanatical German paratroopers emerged from basements *behind* the lead three tanks and threw green disk-shaped Teller mines across the street—separating Boggess's lead tanks from the half-tracks behind them, which had to fight hand-to-hand in the dark while Boggess raced the last 400 yards into the 101st Airborne's lines. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 549–550]`
  3. When 1st Lt. Charles Boggess stuck his head out of the turret of *Cobra King* at 4:50 PM outside Bastogne and spotted soldiers in olive-drab coats near a pillbox, he yelled through a megaphone, *"Come on out, this is the Fourth Armored!"*—and two days later, the US War Department released the famous captioned photograph to every newspaper in America: *"MacAuliffe Rescued by Patton."* **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 551; S7, p. 455]`

#### Battle 3: The Surprise Night Crossing of the Rhine at Oppenheim
- **Date(s) and place:** Night of March 22–23, 1945 (22:00 hours start). Oppenheim and Nierstein (12 miles south of Mainz), Rhineland-Palatinate, Germany. **Battlefield Coordinates:** 49.8556° N, 8.3603° E (Oppenheim Rhine crossing site) via Wikipedia / US Army CMH. **[VERIFIED]** `[S6, p. 255; S10]`
- **Forces on both sides:**
  - *United States (Spearhead of Maj. Gen. Manton S. Eddy's XII Corps, US Third Army):*
    - **Assault Force:** Maj. Gen. S. LeRoy Irwin's **5th Infantry "Red Diamond" Division** (~15,000 men; led across the river at 22:00 by the **11th Infantry Regiment** under Col. Paul J. Black—specifically Company K and Company I of 3rd Battalion and 1st Battalion—alongside the **10th Infantry Regiment** and **2nd Infantry Regiment**), supported by the **204th Engineer Combat Battalion**, USN LCVPs (Landing Craft, Vehicle, Personnel) trucked overland from the coast, and the **748th Tank Battalion** (equipped with floating Duplex Drive [DD] amphibious Sherman tanks) `[S6, p. 256–262]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S6, p. 256–262]`
  - *Germany (Remnants of German 7th Army under Army Group G):*
    - **Defending Force:** Scattered, depleted elements of Gen. Hans Felber's **7th Army** (specifically the **159th Infantry Division** remnants, *Kampfgruppe* units, a machine-gun company in earth bunkers opposite Nierstein/Oppenheim, and an officer candidate school at Gross-Gerau totaling **~2,000–3,000** disorganized troops on the immediate east bank, supported by roughly **150 Luftwaffe sorties** launched on March 23 to bomb the bridges) `[S6, p. 258–264]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S6, p. 258–264]`
- **Commanders on both sides:**
  - *US:* Lt. Gen. George S. Patton Jr. (Commander, Third Army, ordered the immediate crossing); Maj. Gen. Manton S. Eddy (Commander, XII Corps, victorious); Maj. Gen. S. LeRoy Irwin (Commander, 5th Infantry Division); Col. Paul J. Black (Commander, 11th Infantry Regiment); Lt. Col. Kelley B. Lemmon Jr. (Commander, 3rd Battalion, 11th Infantry, personally paddled across in the very first boat). **[VERIFIED]** `[S6, p. 255–260]`
  - *Germany:* Gen. der Infanterie Hans Felber (Commander, German 7th Army, surprised and unable to contain the bridgehead); SS-Oberstgruppenführer Paul Hausser (Commander, Army Group G, forced to commit reserves piecemeal). **[VERIFIED]** `[S6, p. 254, 263]`
- **Terrain for the map:**
  - **The Rhine River at Oppenheim:** Located in the Upper Rhine Plain **12 miles south of Mainz** (49.99° N, 8.27° E) and **15 miles north of Worms** (49.63° N, 8.36° E). At Oppenheim (49.855° N, 8.360° E) and Nierstein (1.5 miles north, 49.87° N, 8.34° E), the Rhine River was **800 to 1,200 feet wide (250–365 meters)** with a current flowing at **3 to 4 miles per hour** `[S6, p. 256]`.
  - **Crucial Geographical Feature:** On the west bank, behind the town of Oppenheim, existed a small, sheltered canal/harbor basin (a quiet backwater off the main river channel) hidden by trees and buildings—allowing US engineers to unload and launch assault boats out of sight of the flat, open agricultural marshes and flood dikes of the Hessian Ried (*Hessisches Ried*) on the east bank toward **Leeheim** and **Gross-Gerau** (49.92° N, 8.48° E) `[S6, p. 257–260; S10]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S6, p. 256–260; S10]`
- **Phase-by-phase movement list:**
  1. **March 13–21, 1945 (The Saar–Palatinate Sweep & Pre-Positioning):** While Field Marshal Bernard Montgomery spends weeks stockpiling 60,000 tons of ammunition, 4,000 artillery pieces, and two airborne divisions on the lower Rhine for Operation Plunder/Varsity (scheduled for the night of March 23–24), Patton's Third Army sweeps the Saar-Palatinate west of the Rhine, capturing Mainz and Worms by March 21. Unknown to SHAEF press officers, Patton has quietly attached Navy landing craft (LCVPs), DUKW amphibious trucks, and 500 assault boats to his spearheads days in advance so he can "take the Rhine on the run." **[PLANNED strategic foresight by Patton]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 265–270; S6, p. 250–255]`
  2. **March 22, 1945 (Morning Order to Speed Up by 24 Hours):** Maj. Gen. Manton Eddy originally schedules XII Corps to cross on the night of March 23, but Patton insists on jumping the gun by 24 hours so Third Army crosses *before* Montgomery: *"We've got to get a bridgehead at once! Every day we save means the savings of hundreds of American lives! We can take the Rhine on the run!"* Patton orders the crossing for **22:00 hours (10:00 PM) that night** with **zero artillery preparation, zero smoke screens, and zero aerial bombardment** to preserve total tactical surprise. **[PLANNED operational audacity]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 271; S6, p. 256–257]`
  3. **March 22, 1945 (22:00 Hours: The Silent Paddle Across):** Under bright moonlight at 10:00 PM, troops of the **3rd Battalion (Co. K and Co. I) and 1st Battalion, 11th Infantry Regiment (5th Infantry Division)** quietly carry wooden plywood assault boats out of the sheltered canal basin at Oppenheim/Nierstein and paddle across the 1,000-foot-wide Rhine. Lt. Col. Kelley Lemmon and Capt.amen of Co. K reach the east bank in less than 10 minutes without a single German shot being fired until the boats scrape the mud on the eastern shore; the surprised seven-man German machine-gun post surrenders immediately and even helps carry American boats ashore. **[PLANNED stealth assault]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S6, p. 258–260; S7, p. 564]`
  4. **March 22–23, 1945 (Midnight to Dawn Build-Up):** One mile downstream at Nierstein, 1st Battalion, 11th Infantry encounters small-arms fire from German bunkers on the dike (where Sgt. Chilson silences two nests with white phosphorus grenades), losing only a handful of casualties. By midnight, all of the 11th and 10th Infantry Regiments are crossing via noisy motor-powered LCVPs and DUKWs; by **dawn on March 23 (06:00 AM)**, **six full infantry battalions** are across the Rhine with only **28 total casualties (8 killed, 20 wounded)**. **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 272; S6, p. 260–262; S7, p. 565]`
  5. **March 23, 1945 (Daylight Armor Ferrying & Pontoon Bridging):** At dawn, amphibious Duplex Drive (DD) Sherman tanks of the **748th Tank Battalion** swim across the Rhine to provide immediate armored support, while US Army combat engineers construct two heavy treadway and pontoon bridges across the river despite 150 desperate Luftwaffe fighter-bomber sorties (Third Army antiaircraft gunners shoot down up to 33 German planes). **[PLANNED]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 272; S6, p. 262–264]`
  6. **March 23–24, 1945 (Expanding the Bridgehead & 4th Armored Breakout):** By the afternoon of March 23—several hours *before* Montgomery's artillery barrage for Operation Plunder even begins at 9:00 PM—5th Infantry Division has carved out a bridgehead **5 miles deep and 7 miles wide**, capturing 1,900 German prisoners. On March 24, Patton walks across the pontoon bridge at Oppenheim, stops mid-span to urinate into the Rhine, and unleashes the **4th Armored Division** across the bridges toward Darmstadt and Frankfurt. **[PLANNED exploitation]** **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 273–274; S6, p. 264–266; S7, p. 566]`
- **What the enemy believed vs. What the general saw differently (Key Insight):**
  - *Enemy belief:* German High Command (and Allied commanders like Montgomery) believed that the Rhine—the widest water barrier in Western Europe—could only be forced after weeks of logistical build-up, massive artillery bombardments, smoke screens, and paratrooper drops (which was why German reserves were concentrated further north opposite Montgomery and Remagen) `[S1, p. 271; S6, p. 256]`.
  - *Key Insight:* Patton wrote in his diary on March 21: *"I feel sure of success, as the Germans will not expect an attempt without a build-up."* He realized that firing a multi-hour artillery barrage would only alert the Germans to rush reserves to the river bank, whereas rowing quietly across in the dark *immediately* upon reaching the river would catch a shattered, retreating enemy before they could even man their riverside bunkers `[S1, p. 271; S6, p. 257]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 271; S6, p. 256–257]`
- **Result as hard numbers:**
  - *Tactical & Strategic Result:* Complete US tactical and operational victory; Third Army secured the first deliberate assault crossing of the Rhine in WWII (after the intact bridge capture at Remagen on March 7), beating Montgomery's Operation Plunder by 24 hours and unhinging the entire German defense of southern and central Germany `[S6, p. 265; S7, p. 565]`.
  - *Casualties & Losses (Night of March 22–23, 1945 at Oppenheim):*
    - **US 5th Infantry Division:** **8 killed and 20 wounded (28 total casualties)** during the night assault crossing and initial bridgehead expansion (compared to **~1,600+ killed and wounded** suffered the very next day on March 24 by the Allied 17th and 6th Airborne Divisions alone in Montgomery's Operation Varsity) `[S1, p. 272; S7, p. 565, 572]`.
    - **German Forces:** **~1,900 prisoners** captured in the Oppenheim bridgehead in the first 36 hours, plus **~33 Luftwaffe aircraft** shot down attacking the bridges on March 23 `[S1, p. 272; S6, p. 264]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 272; S6, p. 264; S7, p. 565, 572]`
- **Vivid, sourced details:**
  1. On March 24, 1945, while walking across the floating treadway bridge at Oppenheim with his chief of staff Hap Gay and aide Col. Charles Codman, Patton paused in the exact center of the bridge, unbuttoned his trousers, and urinated directly into the Rhine River while announcing: *"I have been looking forward to this for a long time"*—as Codman snapped an official photograph of the act. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 273; S2, p. 661; S7, p. 566]`
  2. Immediately after crossing to the eastern shore at Oppenheim on March 24, Patton deliberately dropped to one knee and stumbled in the mud, then stood up clutching two handfuls of German dirt and told his grinning troops that William the Conqueror had fallen when landing in England in 1066 and shouted, *"See! I have taken the land in both my hands!"* **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 274; S2, p. 661]`
  3. So complete was the tactical surprise at 10:00 PM on March 22 that when the very first boatload of Company K, 11th Infantry touched the eastern bank of the Rhine at Oppenheim, the seven stunned German soldiers in the riverside dugout surrendered without firing a shot and volunteered to wade into the water to help pull the remaining American assault boats onto the shore. **[VERIFIED]** `[S6, p. 259; S7, p. 564]`

### 3. The general's method

**Patton's 3-Part Method:**
1. **Staff Preparation Before the Order:** Never wait for high command to react to a crisis; train your staff to draft coded contingency plans and pre-position bridging or transport assets days in advance so your army is already moving when the order arrives. `[S1, p. 191, 270; S9, p. 612]`
2. **"Hold Him by the Nose and Kick Him in the Pants":** Never fight the enemy's main line head-on if you can bypass it; pin the enemy's front with a small force, push armor through narrow bottlenecks, and wheel 90 degrees into his deep rear to sever his lifelines. `[S1, p. 104, 355]`
3. **Speed and Surprise Over Set-Piece Firepower:** Reject slow, methodical build-ups and long artillery preparations that warn the defender; strike violently "on the run" in the dark to seize river lines and road hubs before a retreating enemy can organize a defense, saving thousands of lives through sheer velocity. `[S1, p. 271; S6, p. 257]`

- *Alternative phrasing 1:* Plan the pivot before the crisis hits, wheel ninety degrees into the enemy's exposed flank, and trade slow artillery build-ups for immediate shock and velocity.
- *Alternative phrasing 2:* Out-think the enemy's timetable with pre-arranged staff drills, use tactical air power to cover exposed flanks during deep armored drives, and jump major barriers on the run before the defender can dig in.

### 4. Short Factual Section: The Controversies (The Honest Ledger)

- **The Sicily Slapping Incidents (August 3 & August 10, 1943):** While visiting the 15th Evacuation Hospital at Nicosia on August 3, 1943, and the 93rd Evacuation Hospital at Sant'Agata on August 10, 1943, Patton encountered two soldiers suffering from "battle fatigue" (PTSD) and malaria—Pvt. Charles H. Kuhl (August 3) and Pvt. Paul G. Bennett (August 10). Calling them cowards, Patton slapped both men across the face with his gloves, kicked Kuhl, and pulled out his pistol while threatening to have Bennett shot `[S2, p. 329–338]`. Eisenhower severely reprimanded Patton, forced him to apologize personally to the soldiers, doctors, and every division in Seventh Army, and sidelined him from commanding the D-Day invasion (assigning Omar Bradley to lead First Army and using Patton as the decoy commander of the fictional "First US Army Group" [FUSAG] for Operation Fortitude until August 1944) `[S2, p. 342–348, 410]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 329–348, 410]`
- **The Task Force Baum / Hammelburg Raid (March 26–28, 1945):** Just four days after crossing the Rhine at Oppenheim, Patton personally ordered a 314-man, 57-vehicle armored column from the 4th Armored Division (**Task Force Baum**, commanded by Capt. Abraham J. Baum) to drive 50 miles behind German lines to liberate **Oflag XIII-B at Hammelburg**—where Patton's own son-in-law, **Lt. Col. John K. Waters**, was held as a POW `[S2, p. 668–675; S7, p. 578]`. Operating without infantry support or an extraction plan, the entire task force was surrounded and annihilated by German forces on March 27–28: all 57 vehicles were lost, 32 Americans were killed or missing, Waters was seriously wounded in the buttocks by a German guard, and almost the entire force (including Baum) was captured `[S2, p. 673–675]`. Although Patton claimed in *War As I Knew It* that he did not know Waters was still at Hammelburg until nine days before and only wanted to deceive the Germans about his direction of attack, both his diary and aide Charles Codman confirmed Patton knew Waters was there, and Patton later admitted to Bradley it was the worst mistake of his war `[S1, p. 276; S2, p. 675; S7, p. 579]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 276; S2, p. 668–675; S7, p. 578–579]`
- **The Autumn 1944 Lorraine / Fort Driant Attrition Slog (September–November 1944):** Against fortified German positions around Metz in October 1944, Patton ignored the advice of XX Corps commander Walton Walker to bypass the concrete fortress of **Fort Driant** and ordered repeated frontal assaults by the 5th Infantry Division (Oct 3–12, 1944), resulting in Third Army's first tactical defeat and ~798 US casualties (over 50% of the assault force) before Patton called it off `[S4, p. 265–285]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S4, p. 265–285]`
- **Post-War Denazification Remarks and Relief from Third Army (September–October 1945):** As Military Governor of Bavaria in occupied Germany, Patton sparked outrage at a September 22, 1945 press conference by comparing the Nazi Party to the "Democratic and Republican election fight" in America and employing former Nazis in Bavarian civil administration, combined with harsh treatment and derogatory diary remarks regarding Jewish Holocaust survivors in Displaced Persons camps `[S2, p. 745–765]`. On October 2, 1945, Eisenhower relieved Patton of command of the Third Army and reassigned him to the paper-only Fifteenth Army collecting war history `[S2, p. 766]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S2, p. 745–766]`

### 7. Common myths and errors to avoid

- **Myth:** Patton personally designed and executed Operation Cobra (the Normandy breakout).
  - **Accurate Version:** Operation Cobra (July 25–30, 1944) was planned by Gen. Omar Bradley and executed by Maj. Gen. J. Lawton Collins's VII Corps (part of First Army). Patton's Third Army did not even become officially operational until **12:00 PM on August 1, 1944**—after Collins had already punched the hole to Avranches. Patton's genius was not creating the initial breach, but exploiting it by funneling seven divisions through a two-mile bottleneck and wheeling across France `[S5, p. 280, 338]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S5, p. 280, 338]`
- **Myth:** At the December 19, 1944 Verdun meeting, Patton was caught by surprise by Eisenhower's request, promised to attack in 48 hours from scratch, and then picked up the phone and said "Play ball" to start the planning.
  - **Accurate Version:** First, Patton had already anticipated the order on **December 18**—halting his 4th Armored Division's eastward attack and having his staff prepare three pre-coded plans ("Cent", "Nickel", "Dime") before he ever drove to Verdun `[S1, p. 191–192]`. Second, at Verdun on Dec 19, the agreed attack date with three divisions was the morning of **December 22** (roughly 67–72 hours away) `[S1, p. 193]`. Third, the telephone code word he gave Hap Gay from Verdun on Dec 19 was **"Nickel"** (for the Arlon–Bastogne axis), whereas the famous two-word code **"Play ball"** was actually used by Patton six weeks earlier on **November 7, 1944**, to launch his Lorraine offensive `[S1, p. 166, 193; S4, p. 350; S9, p. 613]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S1, p. 166, 191–193; S4, p. 350; S9, p. 613]`
- **Myth:** The 101st Airborne Division inside Bastogne refused to admit they needed rescuing and was angry at Patton for claiming credit.
  - **Accurate Version:** While generations of 101st Airborne paratroopers proudly joked after the war that *"they didn't need to be rescued,"* contemporary records and historian Hugh M. Cole's official US Army history prove that by December 25–26, Bastogne was down to its final artillery rounds, its hospital was overflowing with untreated wounded lacking plasma, and Gen. Anthony McAuliffe greeted Col. Creighton Abrams and Gen. Patton with profound relief, telling Abrams, *"Gee, I am mighty glad to see you!"* `[S3, p. 551–552]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S3, p. 551–552]`
- **Myth:** Patton was the first American commander to cross the Rhine River in World War II.
  - **Accurate Version:** Lt. Gen. Courtney Hodges's US First Army (specifically the 9th Armored Division) crossed the Rhine first on **March 7, 1945**, by seizing the intact Ludendorff railroad bridge at **Remagen** `[S6, p. 210]`. Patton's crossing at Oppenheim on the night of **March 22–23, 1945**, was the first **deliberate assault boat crossing** of the Rhine in WWII, and it beat Field Marshal Montgomery's massive Operation Plunder by 24 hours `[S6, p. 255–265]`. **[VERIFIED]** `[S6, p. 210, 255–265]`

### Fact-check list

1. **[VERIFIED]** At the December 19, 1944 Verdun conference, Patton declared of the German Ardennes offensive: *"Hell, let's have the guts to let the bastards go all the way to Paris. Then we'll really cut 'em off and chew 'em up!"* `[S1, p. 192; S8, p. 468]`
2. **[DISPUTED / POPULAR CONDENSATION OF ~72 HOURS]** The claim that Patton promised Eisenhower at Verdun on Dec 19 (11:00 AM) to counterattack with three divisions in "48 hours": Patton's and Bradley's memoirs both record that the agreed attack with three divisions (4th Armored, 26th, and 80th Infantry) was set for **06:00 AM on December 22** (~67–72 hours away), after Eisenhower rejected a weaker 48-hour attack on Dec 21. `[S1, p. 193; S3, p. 363; S8, p. 469]`
3. **[DISPUTED — CONFLATED WITH NOV 7, 1944]** The claim that Patton phoned his chief of staff Hap Gay from Verdun on Dec 19 and said **"Play ball"**: on Dec 19, Patton actually used the pre-arranged coin code words **"Cent, Nickel, and Dime"** (specifically **"Nickel"** for the Arlon–Bastogne axis); Patton used the exact two-word code **"Play ball"** six weeks earlier on **November 7, 1944**, to launch his Lorraine offensive. `[S1, p. 166, 192–193; S4, p. 350; S9, p. 613]`
4. **[VERIFIED]** Between December 18 and December 31, 1944, Patton rotated Third Army 90 degrees north across icy roads, moving **133,178 vehicles** (covering 4.5 million vehicle-miles) and **419,581 tons of supplies**. `[S1, p. 211]`
5. **[VERIFIED]** In early August 1944, Patton funneled **7 divisions (over 100,000 men and 20,000 vehicles)** through the narrow 2-mile coastal bottleneck at Avranches in 72 hours, attacking simultaneously into Brittany, south to the Loire, east to Le Mans (captured Aug 8), and wheeling 90 degrees north to Argentan (Aug 12). `[S1, p. 95–105; S5, p. 342, 495]`
6. **[VERIFIED]** On August 13, 1944, Gen. Omar Bradley ordered Patton to halt XV Corps at Argentan rather than closing the 15-mile gap to Falaise, prompting Patton to protest: *"Let me go on to Falaise and we'll drive the British back into the sea for another Dunkirk!"* `[S1, p. 107; S5, p. 509; S8, p. 377]`
7. **[VERIFIED]** In August 1944, US Third Army suffered **15,724 total casualties** (2,177 killed, 10,679 wounded, 2,868 missing), while the Falaise Pocket cost Germany ~10,000 killed and ~50,000 captured (and Third Army captured 68,500 POWs across its August campaign). `[S1, p. 122; S5, p. 558]`
8. **[VERIFIED]** Inside encircled Bastogne (Dec 20–26, 1944), ~11,000–11,840 US troops (101st Airborne and CCB 10th Armored) were surrounded by elements of 5th Panzer Army and 7th Army; the 101st Airborne suffered **341 killed, 1,691 wounded, and 516 missing** between Dec 19 and Jan 6. `[S3, p. 345, 475]`
9. **[VERIFIED]** After Third Army Chaplain Col. James H. O'Neill wrote the famous "Weather Prayer" (distributed on 250,000 cards on Dec 22) and the skies cleared on Dec 23, Patton awarded O'Neill a **Bronze Star Medal** on the spot. `[S1, p. 199; S7, p. 442]`
10. **[VERIFIED]** At **4:50 PM on December 26, 1944**, Lt. Col. Creighton Abrams's 37th Tank Battalion (4th Armored Division), led by 1st Lt. Charles Boggess in the Sherman Jumbo tank *Cobra King*, charged through falling friendly artillery fire at Assenois and broke the siege of Bastogne. `[S3, p. 547–551]`
11. **[VERIFIED]** At **10:00 PM on March 22, 1945**, Patton's **5th Infantry Division** (led by the 11th Infantry Regiment) paddled across the 1,000-foot-wide Rhine River at Oppenheim in darkness with **zero artillery preparation and zero air bombardment**, beating Montgomery's Operation Plunder by 24 hours. `[S1, p. 271–272; S6, p. 256–260]`
12. **[VERIFIED]** By dawn on March 23, 1945, six battalions of the 5th Infantry Division were across the Rhine at Oppenheim at the cost of just **8 killed and 20 wounded (28 total casualties)**, capturing 1,900 German prisoners in the first 36 hours. `[S1, p. 272; S6, p. 262–264; S7, p. 565]`
13. **[VERIFIED]** Quote: *"Brad, for God's sake, tell the world we're across! We knocked down thirty-three Krauts today when they came after our pontoon bridges. I want the world to know Third Army made it before Monty starts across!"* — Patton to Omar Bradley by telephone, March 23, 1945. `[S8, p. 519–520]`
14. **[VERIFIED]** On March 24, 1945, Patton paused in the middle of the pontoon bridge at Oppenheim to urinate into the Rhine River (photographed by Col. Charles Codman), then stumbled deliberately in the mud on the east bank to mimic William the Conqueror. `[S1, p. 273–274; S2, p. 661; S7, p. 566]`
15. **[VERIFIED]** Controversies: Patton slapped two hospitalized soldiers suffering from battle fatigue in Sicily (Aug 3 and Aug 10, 1943, Pvts. Kuhl and Bennett), resulting in his removal from D-Day command; and on March 26–28, 1945, he sent the 314-man **Task Force Baum** 50 miles behind German lines to Hammelburg (where his son-in-law Lt. Col. John Waters was a POW), resulting in the loss of all 57 vehicles and capture/death of nearly the entire force. `[S2, p. 329–348, 668–675]`
