In the winter of eighteen sixty-two, two rivers ran like open doors into the heart of the Confederacy, and two forts held them shut. One of them stood above the Cumberland River, at a place called Fort Donelson.

## BACKGROUND (~1:05 – ~1:30)

[ARCHIVE: ~20 s: headline date card "JANUARY 1862 / CAIRO, ILLINOIS"; LoC Civil War photo of Foote's gunboats / Fort Henry engraving (3D depth); one short Grant portrait (~3 s, label BRIG. GEN. ULYSSES S. GRANT); cuts every 2.5-3 s; no birth/career cards]
The Confederates held a line across Kentucky and Tennessee, and the key to it was the water. In January, Grant went to St. Louis to propose a river campaign, and his superior, General Halleck, cut him off and sent him back to Cairo, Illinois, crestfallen. But Flag Officer Andrew Foote, who commanded the Union gunboats, backed him. On the twenty-eighth Grant wired Halleck that if permitted, he could take Fort Henry on the Tennessee River. This time, Halleck said yes.

## MOVE 1 — FORT HENRY AND FORT DONELSON (~1:30 – ~5:30)

[MAP: m1-1 | `henry` (option 1 territory): two blue ironclads and a gray fort in floodwater; title card MOVE 1 · TWO RIVERS · FEBRUARY 1862 (the date scroll runs 6 FEB); gunboats fire on the fort (visible, large)]
On the sixth of February, Foote's gunboats steamed up the Tennessee and opened fire on Fort Henry. The river was in flood, and parts of the fort stood two feet deep in water. The Confederate commander, General Lloyd Tilghman, had already marched most of his men out. He surrendered to the navy with about ninety men. Grant had not needed to storm the fort.

[MAP: m1-2 | `donelson` wide, option 1: Grant's columns march overland from Fort Henry to Fort Donelson, about a dozen miles; the fort on its bluff above the Cumberland; Confederate numbers move inside the lines; the date scroll runs 12 FEB]
Grant wanted to hit the next fort before the Confederates could reinforce it. On the twelfth, fifteen thousand men marched across country to Fort Donelson. Inside were Confederate generals Floyd, Pillow and Buckner. Grant knew Pillow from the Mexican War, and judged that with any force, however small, he could march right up to within gunshot of any works Pillow was given to hold. And he thought fifteen thousand men on the eighth would do more than fifty thousand a month later.

[MAP: m1-3 | `donelson` close-up, option 2 (two glowing front lines): 14 FEB Foote's ironclads come in close, the fort's batteries fire back (large, visible), the gunboat `St. Louis` hit again and again, a boat drifts back helpless; snow falls; blue soldiers throw down blankets]
On the fourteenth, Foote brought his ironclads up the river to batter the fort. The Confederate guns hit his flagship about sixty times. Two of the ironclads drifted back down the river, helpless, and Foote himself was wounded. That night the weather turned bitter, with rain and snow, freezing and thawing, and many of Grant's men had thrown away their blankets on the march.

[MAP: m1-4 | the breakout again from the Union side (hook-3 reused as a quick echo is NOT allowed: new angle): 15 FEB, red columns press the blue right; date scroll 15 FEB; the Confederates carry three days' food: the haversack card on that word]
The Confederates saw their chance. At dawn on the fifteenth they struck the Union right with most of their garrison, and their men marched out with three days of rations in their haversacks. They were not planning a last stand. They were planning to leave, if the road opened. Grant did not know that yet. He knew only that his right wing was breaking, and that its men had their muskets but no cartridges.

[MAP: m1-5 | Grant's badge rides back along the river road; the right wing re-forms; one quote card on the spoken words]
Grant rode along the line, and he did not panic. He told an officer that whoever attacked first now would win, and that the enemy would have to be in a hurry if he meant to get ahead of him. He ordered the men to fill their cartridge boxes and get back into line, and, as he later wrote, it acted like a charm. In his official report he added that the confusion on the Confederate side was as great as his own, and possibly greater. The Confederates had gone out to break the ring, and then had pulled back inside it. Grant ordered counterattacks, and the first would come on the left.

[MAP: m1-6 | `donelson` close-up, option 2: C. F. Smith's division storms the abatis on the Union left, Smith himself in front (badge on his name), blue line takes the outer works; red line recoils into the fort; night; date scroll 15 FEB]
General C. F. Smith led his division up the hill on the Union left, going in front himself, through the felled trees, and took the outer works. That night the Confederates held a council, and the choice was to give up.

[MAP: m1-7 | Floyd and Pillow steamers leave the Dover landing upriver on the Cumberland (badges only after their names are spoken); Forrest's cavalry slips out through the flooded creek; Buckner's red units alone in the fort; letter cards, dawn 16 FEB]
Generals Floyd and Pillow, the two senior Confederate officers, left on river steamers in the night. Floyd had been President Buchanan's secretary of war, and he feared what the North might do to him. A colonel named Nathan Bedford Forrest led several hundred cavalry out through the flooded backwater. The command fell to Simon Buckner. At dawn he wrote to Grant asking for terms.

[MAP: m1-8 | letter card, Grant's reply on the words, hit + shake on "immediate"; surrender: blue flag over the fort; PRISONERS counter ticks 12,000+; 48 field guns + 17 heavy guns card; Union dead: "about 2,800 casualties" small caption]
Grant answered the same day. "No terms except an unconditional and immediate surrender can be accepted. I propose to move immediately upon your works." Buckner called the terms ungenerous and unchivalrous, but he accepted. Grant telegraphed Halleck that he had taken Fort Donelson, twelve to fifteen thousand prisoners, forty-eight field guns and seventeen heavy guns. The North turned the letters of his name, U.S., into a joke, and then a nickname. Unconditional Surrender Grant. His own side's losses were nearly twenty-eight hundred men.

[MAP: m1-9 | zoom-out: the two rivers now blue; the Confederate line in Kentucky and Tennessee collapses (territory colour change, smooth dissolve); method card METHOD · ATTACK WHILE HE IS STILL DECIDING on the spoken words]
Two river forts had fallen in ten days. Grant had gone from a snubbed brigadier to the man the whole North was talking about. The Union gunboats could now steam far into Tennessee. The Confederates had to fall back from Kentucky and much of middle Tennessee. Grant had not won by waiting for a perfect plan. He moved fast, hit the second fort before help could come, and attacked the moment the enemy hesitated.

[MAP: move1-sub | woven subscribe line, `E.subscribe` button bottom-right with cursor click on the clause; Vicksburg bluffs and the Mississippi appear on the last sentence; NEXT: MOVE 2 label]
Donelson was only the beginning. So how do you take a fortress built on a high bluff, when the river that carries your supplies runs right under its guns? That's the kind of question we answer here, and subscribing is how you catch the next one. Here's how Grant did it.

## MOVE 2 — THE VICKSBURG CAMPAIGN (~5:30 – ~11:30)

[MAP: m2-1 | `mississippi` wide (option 1, territory 1 APR 1863, 35-star US flag from June; for April still 34-star: use 34 until June, flag change on the date scroll at 1 JUN); title card MOVE 2 · THE MISSISSIPPI · 1863; Vicksburg on the bluff, red batteries along the river; blue forces on the opposite bank in swamp; the Confederacy split in two by the river on the words "cut in two"]
Vicksburg, Mississippi, sat on a bluff high above a bend of the Mississippi River. As long as the Confederates held it, the Union could not use the river freely, and the South stayed linked to its western states. All winter, every attempt to reach the city from the north had failed in the swamps. Canals dug in the mud, bayous cleared for gunboats, boats stuck among the trees, and nothing worked. Many in the North believed Grant had run out of ideas.

[MAP: m2-2 | `vicksburg` wide, option 1: Grant's decision: the army marches down the WEST bank through the Louisiana bayous from Milliken's Bend to Hard Times (arrows, bridges over bayous), the river on the left; date scroll 20 APR]
Grant had a different idea. He would march his army down the west bank, through the Louisiana swamps, and cross the river below the city. But for that, he needed boats and transports on the south side, and the only way to get them there was to run them past the guns of Vicksburg.

[MAP: m2-3 | night mode close-up, option 2: 16 APR 10 PM: seven gunboats and three transports slide along the bluffs, batteries fire (large, visible), bonfires and burning houses light the river, the Henry Clay burns and drifts; badge Porter on his name; stamp ONE TRANSPORT LOST on the spoken words]
On the night of the sixteenth of April, at about ten o'clock, Admiral David Porter led seven gunboats and three transports downriver past the Vicksburg batteries. The Confederates set houses on fire and lit bonfires on the shore so they could see the river. Shells hit every gunboat, and Sherman had even hauled yawl boats across the swamp, manned by soldiers, to rescue anyone who went into the water. One transport, the Henry Clay, was set on fire and burned to the water's edge. But no one on the transports was killed, and on the night of the twenty-second, six more supply steamers ran the guns, manned by army volunteers, with enough men offering to take five times the number. Five got through.

[MAP: m2-4 | diversions on the `mississippi` overview: Grierson's blue horsemen ride from La Grange down through Mississippi to Baton Rouge (date scroll 17 APR - 2 MAY, about 1,700 men); Sherman's corps moves up the Yazoo to Haines' Bluff and stops (badge on his name; Sherman only in this shot)]
Meanwhile Grant made the Confederates look the wrong way. Colonel Benjamin Grierson led about seventeen hundred cavalrymen from La Grange, Tennessee, on a long raid through Mississippi that ended at Baton Rouge. And Grant ordered General Sherman to take part of his corps up the Yazoo River and threaten the bluffs north of Vicksburg, to keep Pemberton's army tied down there.

[MAP: m2-5 | `grandgulf` close-up, option 2: 29 APR eight gunboats pound Grand Gulf's batteries (large and visible), nothing silenced; the 10,000 men on transports waiting; night: a dark figure (silhouette, no badge) comes to Grant's camp; arrow shifts south to Bruinsburg; the date scroll 29 APR]
The first place Grant picked to land was Grand Gulf. On the twenty-ninth of April, Porter's eight gunboats attacked its batteries for nearly five and a half hours, and did not silence a single gun. Ten thousand soldiers waited on transports and watched. That night, a local Black man told Grant that there was a good landing at a place called Bruinsburg, and a good road from it to Port Gibson.

[MAP: m2-6 | `bruinsburg` close-up: 30 APR dawn, blue transports unload on the Mississippi shore unopposed (arrow ticks 17,000); red units absent; the river crossed; Grant's badge; caption "a degree of relief" only if spoken]
The landing at Bruinsburg was not defended. On the thirtieth of April, about seventeen thousand Union soldiers stepped ashore on the east bank of the Mississippi. Grant later wrote that he felt a degree of relief scarcely ever equalled since.

[MAP: m2-7 | DECIDE card on the `mississippi` map at Port Gibson: E.decide({ sub: "Banks cannot reach Port Hudson before May 10, with 15,000 men. Pemberton and Johnston are close.", a: "WAIT FOR BANKS", b: "CUT LOOSE", real: "b" }); map dimmed behind; the reveal lights CUT LOOSE]
Now stop for a moment, and put yourself in Grant's place. His army is across the river and deep in enemy country. General Banks, who might have joined him, cannot get to Port Hudson before the tenth of May, and he'll have only fifteen thousand men. Do you wait for Banks, or do you cut loose and go inland alone? [MAP: m2-8 | the real choice: supply wagons and a thin line back to Grand Gulf fade; the army's arrow turns inland; Sherman badge and his warning card on his name ("this road will be jammed"), Grant's reply card: "make the country furnish the balance"; foraging icons: corn mills, cattle]
Grant decided to move independently of Banks and cut loose from his base. Sherman warned that one road could not feed the army. Grant answered that he did not expect to supply it with full rations, only to bring up hard bread, coffee and salt, and make the country furnish the balance. The army set out with about two days of food, and found beef and corn along the way.

[MAP: m2-9 | `jackson` overview, option 1 (territory): the single blue arrow drives between red Pemberton at Vicksburg and red Johnston at Jackson; battle stamps one per spoken name with date: PORT GIBSON 1 MAY, RAYMOND 12 MAY, JACKSON 14 MAY, CHAMPION HILL 16 MAY, BIG BLACK 17 MAY (hit, small shake, red pulse); the date scroll runs; two red armies cannot join]
Grant marched inland and wedged his army between two Confederate forces: Pemberton's at Vicksburg, and Joseph Johnston's gathering at Jackson. He beat them one at a time. Port Gibson on the first of May. Raymond on the twelfth. He seized Jackson on the fourteenth, and turned back west. At Champion Hill on the sixteenth, he beat Pemberton in the biggest fight of the campaign. Grant had about fifteen thousand men fully engaged, against about twenty-five thousand Confederates. One Union division lost more than a third of its men. But a whole Confederate division was cut off, and never made it back into Vicksburg. The next day, at the Big Black River, he took eighteen guns and more than seventeen hundred prisoners.

[MAP: m2-10 | numbers card: GRANT ~33,000 vs CONFEDERATES 60,000+ (Grant's own words), then the five battles cost 4,400 (695 + 3,425 + 259): ticks; Pemberton driven into the Vicksburg lines, ring closes; the date scroll 18 MAY]
Grant himself counted over sixty thousand Confederates in Mississippi, against about thirty-three thousand of his own. In eighteen days he beat them in detail, and the five battles cost him fewer than forty-five hundred men. By the eighteenth of May, Pemberton's army was shut inside Vicksburg.

[MAP: m2-11 | `vicksburg` siege close-up, option 2: 19 and 22 MAY assaults bounce off (blue wave breaks, impacts visible); trenches, parallel lines creeping; 4 JULY white flag, stamp VICKSBURG FELL; counters: ABOUT 29,500 SURRENDERED · 172 CANNON; Pemberton badge on his name; 35-star flag appears on the date scroll June]
Two assaults, on the nineteenth and twenty-second of May, failed. Grant later wrote that he regretted the second one. So he settled in for a siege. On the fourth of July, Pemberton surrendered about twenty-nine and a half thousand men and a hundred and seventy-two cannon. Lincoln wrote that the Father of Waters again went unvexed to the sea.

[MAP: m2-12 | method card METHOD · GO WHERE HE SAYS YOU CANNOT on the spoken words; the Mississippi turns blue top to bottom; the Confederacy cut in two (territory dissolve)]
Everyone had said the army could not live without its supply line. Grant cut it, fed his men off the land, and was at the gates of Vicksburg before the Confederates understood where he was. And he did it with fewer men than the Confederates had in the state.

## BRIDGE (~11:30 – ~12:10)

[ARCHIVE: ~25 s: date card "MARCH 1864 / WASHINGTON"; LoC photo Grant (lieutenant general) + Lincoln period engraving; then Overland Campaign engraving; no film]
After Vicksburg, Grant rescued the army besieged at Chattanooga, and in March of eighteen sixty-four, Lincoln made him lieutenant general, in command of all the Union armies. He went east, to face Robert E. Lee. In six weeks, from the Wilderness to Cold Harbor, the Army of the Potomac lost about fifty-five thousand men. On the third of June, Grant ordered one more assault at Cold Harbor. It lasted less than an hour, and cost about thirty-five hundred men. He wrote afterwards that he had always regretted it, because no advantage whatever was gained to compensate for the heavy loss.

## MOVE 3 — ACROSS THE JAMES (~12:10 – ~16:30)

[MAP: m3-1 | `richmond` overview, option 1 (territory, 35-star flag, CSA first national): the two lines at Cold Harbor east of Richmond, trenches, blue on the left; title card MOVE 3 · THE JAMES RIVER · JUNE 1864; the James River south; Petersburg 25 miles south of Richmond; five railroads card ONLY if verified (NOT in script)]
By the second week of June, the armies sat in trenches outside Richmond. Grant could not break through Lee's lines. So he decided not to try. South of Richmond, across the wide James River, lay Petersburg, where the railroads that fed Lee's army and the capital came together. If Grant could take it, Richmond would starve.

[MAP: m3-2 | diversions: Sheridan's cavalry rides west (date scroll 7 JUN) toward Trevilian Station, draws Hampton's and Fitzhugh Lee's cavalry; Warren's V Corps and Wilson's cavalry cross the Chickahominy at Long Bridge and stand toward Richmond (night 12 JUN); badges only after names; Meade badge on his name, in one line only]
The plan was to disappear. Grant did not tell the enemy where he was going, and he did not need to. He only needed Lee to look in the wrong place for long enough. Grant sent Philip Sheridan's cavalry off to the west, and Lee sent his own cavalry after it. After dark on the twelfth of June, Gouverneur Warren's Fifth Corps crossed the Chickahominy River and faced toward Richmond, while the rest of the army slipped out of the trenches and marched south by back roads, with General Meade's Army of the Potomac in the lead.

[MAP: m3-3 | `jamesriver` close-up, option 2: the columns arrive at the James at Wilcox's Landing; II Corps boards ferry boats on the morning of 14 JUN; a pontoon bridge appears at Weyanoke (Fort Powhatan to Windmill Point): 101 pontoons, 2,100 feet; date scroll 14 JUN; the bridge builds from 4 PM to 11 PM (clock)]
On the morning of the fourteenth, the first troops were ferried across the James at Wilcox's Landing. Upriver, at Weyanoke, army engineers began building a bridge, a hundred and one pontoon boats, about twenty-one hundred feet long, across a tidal river with up to ninety feet of water in the channel. They started at four in the afternoon, and by about eleven o'clock that night it was ready.

[MAP: m3-4 | numbers card: ABOUT 115,000 MEN (Grant's own count) cross, wagons, cattle; stamp WITHOUT THE LOSS OF A SINGLE MAN (only if spoken; Robbins' claim hedged: "by one engineer's count") ; Lee's HQ at Drewry's Bluff: red cavalry gone, red troops stay near Richmond; Lee telegram card on the spoken words 17 JUN 4:30 PM]
Over the next two days, about a hundred and fifteen thousand men, by Grant's own count, crossed the river with their guns and wagons. Lee had guessed on the fourteenth that Grant might go for Petersburg, but he would not believe it, and kept most of his army around Richmond. On the sixteenth he was still asking whether Grant had been seen crossing the James. On the seventeenth, he wired Beauregard: "Have no information of Grant's crossing James River."

[MAP: m3-5 | `petersburg` close-up, option 2: 15 JUN dusk: about 14,000 blue men of Smith's XVIII Corps with Hinks's Black division in the lead roll over the Dimmock Line, a two-mile hole; the 2,200 red defenders under Beauregard thin; badges only on names; Smith halts, the night; date scroll 15 JUN; ghost arrows toward Petersburg's railroad that he did not take]
The first attack on Petersburg was made on the fifteenth of June by about fourteen thousand men of General William F. Smith's corps, with a division of Black soldiers under General Edward Hinks in the lead. They faced about twenty-two hundred Confederates under General P. G. T. Beauregard, and broke through a stretch of the defences about two miles wide. But Smith stopped for the night, and waited for the morning.

[MAP: m3-6 | `petersburg` overview option 1: Lee's army hurries south across the James to Petersburg (red arrows) over 16-18 JUN; blue assaults fail 15-18 JUN; counters ABOUT 11,400 UNION CASUALTIES; trenches freeze into a line; date scroll 18 JUN]
By the time Smith's men and Hancock's corps were ready to renew the attack, Lee's veterans were arriving. By the eighteenth, the Union had lost about eleven thousand four hundred men in four days of assaults, and the chance was gone. Grant wrote years later that he believed then, and still believed, that Petersburg could have been easily captured at that time.

[MAP: m3-7 | zoom-out: the blue pontoon line to the south, trench lines at Petersburg growing; text card: SIEGE · 9 MONTHS; the railroads blue-cut; method card METHOD · NEVER GIVE HIM TIME on the spoken words]
Even so, the move changed the war. The Army of the Potomac was across the James and Lee was pinned in a siege that would last more than nine months, until the Confederates had to abandon both Petersburg and Richmond. Grant had moved more than a hundred thousand men out of a stalemate, over a river, and given the Union the initiative again. He had stopped asking how to break Lee's lines, and asked instead where Lee could not follow.

## ENDING (~16:30 – ~17:15)

[MAP: end-1 | `appomattox` or the three maps in a row: Donelson, Vicksburg, the James; then Appomattox 9 APR 1865 (stamp only on its words); engagement final card E.final({ moves: [["FORT DONELSON","FEB 1862"],["VICKSBURG","1863"],["THE JAMES RIVER","JUN 1864"]] })]
Look at the three moves together. At Donelson, he struck while the enemy was still deciding. At Vicksburg, he went where the enemy said no army could go, and lived off the land to do it. On the James, he slipped a hundred thousand men away from a stalemate and gave Lee no time to catch his breath. The nickname the North gave him in eighteen sixty-two was a joke about his initials. By eighteen sixty-five it described a method.

Grant took the surrender of the Donelson garrison in eighteen sixty-two, of Pemberton's army at Vicksburg in eighteen sixty-three, and of Lee's army at Appomattox in eighteen sixty-five. Each time, he moved before the enemy was ready, and he went where the enemy said he could not go. So which was Grant's greatest move? One, the two river forts. Two, Vicksburg.
