# Stonewall Jackson video: raw fact check (2026-10-16)
Verdicts: OK / CORRECTED / DISPUTED / UNSUPPORTED. These are SUGGESTED verdicts from raw notes. No script was written. Quotes are copied from the fetched text (OCR hyphenation cleaned). Page numbers of Henderson are NOT given on purpose: the OCR page headers are unreliable (digits misread), so cite by chapter only.

## 0. SUMMARY (claim number, suggested verdict, one line)
1. OK (with wording fix). ~17,000 men with Ewell is solid (Henderson "nearly 17,000", NPS "17,000"). "646 miles in 48 days" appears ONLY in Wikipedia (no primary or NPS/ABT source found); the 48 is "days of marching", not elapsed time (23 Mar-9 Jun = 78 days). History.com says "more than 650", Osprey "over 600". Say "about 650 miles in 48 days of marching".
2. DISPUTED. Union total: 52,000 (Wikipedia infobox, three armies, June 1862), "60,000" (Wikipedia Jackson page), "no less than 50,000" (ABT). No source gives 64,000. Safe: "over 50,000".
3. OK, three forces CORRECTED. Dates and results fine (Kernstown 23 Mar Jackson tactical defeat; McDowell 8 May; Front Royal 23 May; Winchester 25 May; Cross Keys 8 Jun; Port Republic 9 Jun). Front Royal garrison is 900-1,063 (not flat 1,000). Winchester: Banks ~6,500 vs Jackson 16,000 (NPS/ABT), but EVA says 3,500 for Banks (outlier). Cross Keys 11,500 vs 5,800 OK. Port Republic Union 3,000-3,500 OK.
4. OK. Massanutten ~50 miles; the single road over it runs New Market to Luray; Banks's Strasburg works faced the wrong way. "New Market Gap" as a name was not seen in the sources read (they say "the road from New Market").
5. Lincoln/McDowell OK; Williamsport date CORRECTED; 40,000 vs 20,000 CORRECTED. Lincoln's 24 May order was to "put 20,000 men in motion" (Shields + Ord); the "40,000" is McDowell's whole corps held back from McClellan since 3 April. Banks fled 35 miles on 25 May and crossed the Potomac that night / 26 May (not clearly dated 26 May in any source).
6. OK with ranges. Confederate ~2,500-2,800 (Henderson 2,500 k+w; Wikipedia 2,677; sum of NPS battle boxes 2,775). Union 5,400-7,000 (NPS box sum 5,435; Wikipedia ~7,000). Mixed definitions, see section.
7. OK (caveat). Contemporary: press used "Jackson's Foot Cavalry" by 26 Jul 1862 (Evening Star). No single coiner known. Imboden (1887) ties it to the Front Royal march.
8. OK, exact wording found (Imboden, Battles and Leaders vol. 2, p. 297). It is Imboden's recollection of what Jackson told him early in the war, not a Valley order.
9. DISPUTED (ranges). Jackson's wing ~24,000 (NPS series), 25,000 (NPS article); "23,500" not found. Pope 47,000 (Henderson, July), "barely 55,000" (NPS series), 51,000 AoV + 26,000 AoP = 77,000 (Wikipedia; 62,000 engaged), 70,000 (ABT). Lee total ~55,000 (ABT, NPS series), 50,000 (Wikipedia).
10. OK route, DISPUTED hours. Jeffersonton - Amissville - Hinson's Mill - Orlean - Salem (night 25 Aug) - Thoroughfare Gap - Haymarket - Gainesville - Bristoe Station at sunset 26 Aug. 55 miles/2 days (NPS article, ABT), 56 miles/48 h (NPS series), 54 miles/36 h (HistoryNet only), "over 50 miles in 34 h" (NPS series).
11. OK. Trimble's two regiments + Stuart's cavalry took Manassas Junction early 27 Aug; ~300 prisoners; lobster salad / Rhine wine is an unnamed eyewitness quoted by NPS; "million dollars" is one chaplain's guess (Sheeran), not an audited value. Burned the night of 27 Aug.
12. OK. Jackson attacked King's column at Brawner Farm on purpose (NPS says so). Casualties: ~1,150 Union / 1,250 Confederate (Wikipedia); NPS "nearly one-third of the 7,000 engaged". Ewell lost his left leg; Taliaferro wounded.
13. OK belief, CORRECTED wording. Two different "bag the whole crowd" texts exist (NPS vs Henderson). Verbatim Aug 30 dawn dispatch to Halleck found.
14. OK with nuance. Longstreet deployed by about noon on 29 Aug (NPS series), "late morning" (ABT), "afternoon" (NPS overview, loose). Pope was not told, but Porter and McDowell KNEW (Buford's report); "unnoticed by Pope" is right, "unnoticed by the Union" is wrong.
15. OK. Rocks thrown at the Deep Cut by Stafford's (Louisiana) and Bradley T. Johnson's brigades. Longstreet: 28,000 (NPS), 25,000 (Wikipedia, NPS series), "nearly 30,000" (NPS panel). "Largest simultaneous mass assault of the war" is NPS's own wording (not independent).
16. CORRECTED. Union 14,462 and Confederate 7,387 (ABT) / 7,298 (Wikipedia, killed+wounded only). "8,350" is UNSUPPORTED in everything read. The Union 14,462 is an OR return for 16 Aug-2 Sep, so it is larger than the 28-30 Aug fighting alone.
17. OK. Hooker ~130,000 (NPS, ABT) to 133,868 (OR via Wikipedia); Lee ~60,000 (60,298 OR; "slightly fewer than 61,000" NPS; ABT engaged 57,352). Longstreet with Hood and Pickett was near Suffolk.
18. Mostly OK; two CORRECTED. Cracker-box scene with Hotchkiss is ~3:30 am on 2 May (not the evening); the route stayed on the BROCK Road to the Orange Turnpike (did not turn onto the Plank Road). Column 28,000 (NPS series, Wikipedia) to "about 30,000" (NPS FRSP, ABT). Length "more than 12 miles" (NPS, EVA, Wikipedia) vs 14 (Hotchkiss in Henderson). Start 7-8 am; deployed ~3 pm; attack 5:15-6:00 pm.
19. OK. Hooker's 9:30 am wire to Howard verbatim; 4:10 pm dispatch verbatim (Henderson), but it was signed by Van Alen for Hooker, addressed to Butterfield for Sedgwick.
20. OK. XI Corps ~11,000 (marker, Stone Sentinels); 12,000-13,000 not found. Only two small regiments (153rd Pa, 54th NY) + 2 guns faced west.
21. OK as interpretive text, UNSUPPORTED as eyewitness. "Rabbits and deer" (NPS Civil War Series), "deer, rabbits and turkeys" (battlefield marker text), "rabbits and foxes" (Wikipedia). No named eyewitness found.
22. OK. More than two miles (ABT, Jackson's report via Henderson); Rodes halted ~7:15 pm about a mile west of Chancellorsville.
23. OK, litter count DISPUTED. 9-9:30 pm; 18th North Carolina (HistoryNet; NPS says "a North Carolina regiment of Lane's brigade"); two bullets left arm, one right hand. Litter: dropped once (Henderson, McGuire, Mary Anna Jackson, NPS) vs twice (HistoryNet).
24. OK, wording DISPUTED. Best-sourced: "He has lost his left arm; but I have lost my right arm." Reported through chaplain B. T. Lacy (Dabney 1866; Mary Anna Jackson 1892). Several variants exist. Lee's note found verbatim (variants).
25. OK. Died Sun 10 May 1863, 3:15 pm (EVA) at Fairfield (Chandler plantation), Guinea Station; pneumonia (McGuire: pleuro-pneumonia). Last words wording varies by source (see section).
26. OK. Union 17,304 (ABT, EVA); Confederate 13,460 (ABT) or 12,764 (Wikipedia); ~22% of Lee's army.
27. OK. Born 21 Jan 1824 Clarksburg; orphan by 1831 (father d. 1826, mother d. 1831); West Point 17th of 59, class of 1846; brevets; VMI 1851. "Old Blue Light" origin is contested.
28. DISPUTED. First print: Charleston Mercury 25 Jul 1861. Wording varies in every source; Bee died the next day.
29. Adoption date OK (Act of 1 May 1863); use in the field on 2 May UNSUPPORTED; "coffin draped in the first one made" CORRECTED to "one of the first examples" (Richmond Clothing Depot flag, 12 May lying in state).
Artillery icons: not researched (optional item skipped).

## 2. VALLEY CAMPAIGN 1862

### 1. Army size and "646 miles in 48 days"
- HEN-I (ch. "The Valley Campaign"): "The Army of the Valley numbered nearly 17,000 officers and men. Ewell's effective strength was 7,500 ; Johnson's 2,500 ; Jackson's 6,000 ; and there were eleven batteries." (before Front Royal)
- NPS-timeline: "By late May Jackson had 17,000 men in his ranks." ABT "Stonewall Marches...": "Their forces together numbered 17,000 men."
- WP (Valley campaign, lead): "Jackson's 17,000 men marched 646 miles (1,040 km) in 48 days and won several minor battles as they successfully engaged three Union armies (52,000 men)". Later in the same article: "he pressed his army to travel 646 miles (1,040 km) in 48 days of marching and won five significant victories with a force of about 17,000 against a combined force of over 50,000". WP Jackson page: "646 miles (1,040 km) in 48 days of marching ... against a combined force of 60,000."
- Search snippets (not fetched pages): History.com "more than 650 miles", Osprey "over 600".
- Nowhere found: the actual dates of the "48 days", or an original source for 646. Elapsed time 23 Mar (Kernstown) to 9 Jun (Port Republic) is 78 days; Jackson left the Valley 17 Jun.
- Note: 17,000 was the peak only for ~22 May to 25 May. Jackson fought Kernstown with ~3,500-3,800 and McDowell with ~6,000 (with Johnson's 3,000).
- Suggest: OK for 17,000; reword the miles/days to "about 650 miles in 48 days of marching".

### 2. Union forces
- WP infobox: "Strength 52,000 (June 1862)", commanders Banks, Frémont, McDowell (Shields served under McDowell).
- ABT "Stonewall Marches": "With McDowell and Fremont at his rear and Banks in front, Jackson would be surrounded by no less than 50,000 troops."
- HEN-I: "Banks and Fremont united had 40,000 men, McDowell over 80,000." (Henderson's own table in the chapter "Comments" lists "Strategic" strength opposing Jackson at Winchester as 60,000 and at Cross Keys 28,000; the OCR of that table is garbled, so do not quote it.)
- Verdict: DISPUTED, say "over 50,000" (ABT) or "three Union armies". Do not use 64,000.

### 3. Battles, dates, forces (NPS-timeline unless stated)
- Kernstown, 23 Mar: "Forces Engaged: 12,300 total (US 8,500; CS 3,800). Estimated Casualties: 1,308 total (US 590; CS 718). Result: Federal Victory." NPS text: "this battle was a tactical loss for Jackson, he scored a strategic win". EVA: Jackson "threw his small command of 3,500 men against Shields at Kernstown", Shields's division "just over 10,000 strong" in the area.
- McDowell, 8 May: "12,500 total (US 6,500; CS 3,000)... Confederate Victory"; ABT list: Union 256, Confederate 498 (754 total). EVA: "a Union tactical victory, as four Union regiments inflicted heavier casualties than they endured ... But Jackson drove the Union troops from McDowell to Franklin".
- Front Royal, 23 May: NPS "4,063 total (US 1,063; CS 3,000) ... Estimated Casualties 960 total (US 904; CS 56)". EVA: "the 900-man 1st Maryland Infantry (Union)". HEN-I: Front Royal "was committed to the charge of Colonel Kenly, of the 1st Maryland Regiment in the Federal service, and 1,000 rifles and 2 guns were placed at his disposal." WP: "about 1,000 men of the 1st Maryland Infantry under Col. John R. Kenly"; "Union casualties were 773, of which 691 were captured. Confederate losses were 36 killed and wounded." B&L-2 editors: "The captures at Front Royal were : 1 piece of artillery, 3 heavily laden trains with stores, and 8 wagons, with teams, retreating with commissary stores, and 160 prisoners, including Miss Belle Boyd". CORRECTED: garrison 900-1,063; casualties 773 (WP) to 904 (NPS) depending on source.
- First Winchester, 25 May: ABT "Forces Engaged 22,500: Union 6,500, Confed. 16,000. Estimated Casualties 2,416: Union 2,019 (62 killed, 243 wounded, 1,714 missing & captured), Confederate 397 (68 killed, 329 wounded)". NPS box says Confederate 400. EVA: "Banks deployed his 3,500-man command south of Winchester ... Jackson's 16,000 troops" (outlier). WP: Banks's "4,476 infantry, 1,600 cavalry, and 16 artillery pieces". Henderson fn: Confederate "68 killed ; 386 wounded ; 8 missing" (OCR 386 vs ABT 329: OCR unreliable).
- Cross Keys, 8 Jun: ABT "Forces Engaged 17,300: Union 11,500, Confed. 5,800. Estimated Casualties 972: Union 684, Confed. 288". NPS box: 664/287. (Do not use "Frémont ~16,000": Henderson's position table has Frémont at 16,000 in late May, but ABT/NPS engaged = 11,500.)
- Port Republic, 9 Jun: ABT "Forces Engaged 9,500: Union 3,500, Confed. 6,000. Estimated Casualties 1,818: Union 1,002, Confed. 816". Union = brigades of Erastus B. Tyler and Samuel S. Carroll (Shields's division, Kimball in command; Shields himself absent). HEN-I (Port Republic): "it was his [Tyler's] five regiments, over 3,000 strong". ABT: "Initial Confederate assaults were repulsed with heavy casualties. A Confederate flank attack, including a brigade of the Louisiana Tigers, overtook an artillery emplacement on the Union left at The Coaling."
- Result labels: ABT calls Kernstown "Union Victory"; ABT/NPS list McDowell, Front Royal, Winchester, Cross Keys, Port Republic as Confederate victories.

### 4. Massanutten, the one road, Strasburg
- HEN-I (ch. "Front Royal"): "a gigantic mass of mountain ridges, parallel throughout their length of fifty miles to the Blue Ridge and the Alleghanies. These are the famous Massanuttons"; "save at one point only the Massanuttons are practically impassable. From New Market, in the western valley, a good road climbs the heights, and crossing the lofty plateau, sinks sharply down to Luray, the principal village on the South Fork."
- WP: "Massanutten Mountain soared 2,900 feet and separated the Valley into two halves for about 50 miles, from Strasburg to Harrisonburg. During the 19th century, there was but a single road that crossed over the mountain, from New Market to Luray."
- EVA: "Jackson had intended to assault Banks head-on at Strasburg, but when he learned of the garrison at Front Royal he and Ewell crossed into Page Valley." Banks, at Strasburg, "asked but was refused permission to abandon Strasburg".
- B&L-2 (Imboden): "News of this disaster reached Banks at Strasburg ... The works Banks had constructed had not been made for defense in that direction, so he abandoned them and set out with all haste for Winchester".
- WP: Jackson "marched his command east from New Market over Massanutten Mountain, combining with Ewell on May 22, and proceeded down the Luray Valley ... He sent Ashby's cavalry directly north to make Banks think that he was going to attack Strasburg".
- Suggest OK. Call the crossing "the New Market road" unless a source for "New Market Gap" is found.

### 5. Banks, Washington, Lincoln, McDowell
- Banks's retreat: B&L-2 (Imboden): "on the 25th he fled again, not halting till he had crossed the Potomac". ABT: "Banks' army reached Williamsport on the Potomac River in safety, covering 35 miles in a single day." WP: "The Federals fled relatively unimpeded for 35 miles in 14 hours, crossing the Potomac River into Williamsport, Maryland." HEN-I quotes a Zouave captain: "my men marched 141 miles in 47 hours". No source gives "26 May" for the crossing; the retreat day was 25 May. CORRECTED to 25-26 May.
- Panic: HEN-I: "Lincoln and Stanton were electrified even more effectually than Banks. They issued an urgent call for more troops. 'There is no doubt,' wrote Stanton to the Governor of Massachusetts, 'that the enemy in great force are marching on Washington.' In the cities of the North the panic was indescribable. ... the Governors of thirteen States were calling on their militia to march to the defence of Washington."
- Lincoln's order (as printed in the Seventh Ohio regimental history, 24 May 1862, ~5 pm, matches OR Series I vol. 12 pt 3): "GENERAL MCDOWELL: You are instructed, laying aside for the present the movement on Richmond, to put 20,000 men in motion at once for the Shenandoah, moving on the line or in advance of the line of the Manassas Gap Railroad. Your object will be to capture the forces of Jackson and Ewell, either in cooperation with General Fremont or ..."
- Lincoln to McClellan, 24 May 4 pm (via WP, OR cited): "In consequence of General Banks's critical position I have been compelled to suspend General McDowell's movements to you. The enemy are making a desperate push upon Harper's Ferry, and we are trying to throw Frémont's force and part of McDowell's in their rear."
- Numbers: WP: "Lincoln to redirect 20,000 men from the corps of Maj. Gen. Irvin McDowell". EVA: "it did cause Lincoln to divert McDowell's 40,000-man command from Fredericksburg to the Valley" and (earlier) "On April 3 ... Lincoln countermanded the embarkation of 40,000 troops under Union general Irvin McDowell for the Peninsula". WP concluding paragraph: "causing Washington to withhold over 40,000 troops from McClellan's offensive". HEN-I: McDowell "was ordered to lay aside the movement, and to send half his army to ..." Henderson's position table: Shields 10,200 + Ord 9,000 (+ Bayard 2,000) near Front Royal/Manassas Gap, i.e. the ~20,000.
- Verdict: 20,000 = what Lincoln sent into the Valley on 24 May; 40,000 = McDowell's corps withheld from McClellan. Say "Lincoln pulled 20,000 men off the march on Richmond" and "about 40,000 troops never reached McClellan" (EVA/WP).

### 6. Campaign casualties, prisoners, supplies
- Confederate: HEN-I (ch. "Comments"): "8,500 prisoners were either paroled or sent to Richmond. 3,500 Federals were killed or wounded. ... 9 guns were taken and over 10,000 rifles, while the loss of the Confederates was no more than 2,500 killed and wounded, 600 prisoners, and 8 guns." (The 8,500 prisoners is far above other counts; B&L-2 editors: "Jackson reports the capture in all of 3050 of Banks's men." Treat Henderson's 8,500 as DISPUTED.) WP infobox: Union "~7,000", Confederate "2,677". Sum of NPS-timeline battle boxes: Confederate 718+498+56+400+287+816 = 2,775; Union 590+256+904+2,019+664+1,002 = 5,435. Killed+wounded+missing in all these battle boxes (Winchester Union includes 1,714 missing/captured).
- Banks's force: B&L-2 editors: "Banks reports on April 30th, as present for duty, 9178; and on June 16th, 7113,— being a reduction of 2065."
- Supplies: ABT Winchester: "Banks left behind tons of military supplies, including 500,000 rounds of ammunition and 15,000 pounds of bacon, leading the Confederates to give Banks the nickname 'Commissary Banks.'" and (Q&A) "over 500,000 rounds of ammunition, 100 heads of cattle, and 15,000 pounds of bacon". WP: Front Royal "Jackson's men captured about $300,000 of Federal supplies; Banks soon became known as 'Commissary Banks' to the Confederates". HEN-I fn: "Quartermaster's stores, to the value of 25,000L, were captured at Winchester alone, and 9,354 small arms, besides two guns" (OCR "25,000L" = £25,000). No ABT/NPS figure for total prisoners at Winchester beyond the 1,714 missing/captured.

### 7. "Foot cavalry"
- B&L-2 (Imboden, 1887): "He sometimes made thirty miles in twenty-four hours with his entire army, thus gaining for his infantry the sobriquet of 'Jackson's foot cavalry.' Very early in the afternoon of May 23d he struck Front Royal."
- WP "Foot cavalry": "an oxymoron coined by the media"; earliest dated uses: The Evening Star, 26 Jul 1862: "Stonewall Jackson is now the idol of the army and the people. His soldiers are proud of the name 'Jackson's Foot Cavalry.'"; The National Republican, 10 Sep 1862 (LOC link in WP). (That WP article is itself flagged as needing citations; the Evening Star line is checkable on Chronicling America.)
- NPS article "Confederates Take August": "Never did the 'foot cavalry' better deserve its name."
- HEN-I: the phrase was not found by search (OCR variants tried).
- Verdict: OK as a nickname by mid-1862; do not say a named person coined it at Front Royal.

### 8. Quote (exact)
B&L-2, Imboden, "Stonewall Jackson in the Shenandoah", p. 297 (page header 298 follows the quote): "Jackson's military operations were always unexpected and mysterious. In my personal intercourse with him in the early part of the war, before he had become famous, he often said there were two things never to be lost sight of by a military commander: "Always mystify, mislead, and surprise the enemy, if possible ; and when you strike and overcome him, never let up in the pursuit so long as your men have strength to follow; for an army routed, if hotly pursued, becomes panic-stricken, and can then be destroyed by half their number. The other rule is, never fight against heavy odds, if by any possible manoeuvring you can hurl your own force on only a part, and that the weakest part, of your enemy and crush it. Such tactics will win every time, and a small army may thus destroy a large one in detail, and repeated victory will make it invincible.""
Verdict OK. Note: it is Imboden's memory, written ~25 years later. Source URL: https://archive.org/download/battlesleadersof21john/battlesleadersof21john_djvu.txt (search "mystify").

## 3. SECOND MANASSAS, AUG 1862

### 9. Strengths
- NPS-2M sec 2: "Lee's intention, to send Jackson's entire wing, some 24,000 men, on an ambitious march around Pope's right flank"; sec 1: "Achieving all these goals with a dispirited army of barely 55,000 troops presented Pope"; "Jackson and Longstreet brought some 55,000 soldiers to the task".
- NPS article "Confederates Take August": "Jackson with Stuart's cavalry, comprising about 25,000 men".
- ABT: "Forces Engaged 125,000: Union 70,000, Confed. 55,000."
- WP infobox: "77,000 (estimated): 51,000 (Army of Virginia); 26,000 (Army of the Potomac ...); 62,000 engaged (estimated)" vs Confederate "50,000" (note: "75,000 according to Ballard-Arthur").
- HEN-II: July: "Pope's army consisted of 47,000 men"; "Jackson with 22,000" at Cedar Mountain (Aug 9) stage; for the flank march he gives the wing no flat number in the passages found.
- "23,500" not found anywhere. Verdict: DISPUTED, present as ranges.

### 10. The march
- NPS article (https://www.nps.gov/articles/the-confederates-take-august.htm): "Jackson began his march from Jeffersonton on the morning of August 25th. Passing through Amissville and crossing the upper Rappahannock at Hinson's Mill, Jackson's column proceeded to Orlean and bivouacked for the night at Salem. The next day Jackson pushed eastward through Thoroughfare Gap, Haymarket and Gainesville, reaching Bristoe Station on the Orange & Alexandria Railroad about sunset. ... In two days it had covered approximately 55 miles."
- NPS-2M sec 2: "Forty-eight hours later after covering fifty-six miles in the blistering August heat, Jackson lighted in the rear of Pope's army at Manassas Junction"; "Jackson had covered more than fifty miles in the thirty-four hours since his departure".
- HistoryNet (fetched): "The Confederates had marched 54 miles in 36 hours."
- ABT: "At sunset on August 26, Jackson's forces complete a remarkable 55-mile march". NPS-2M intro page: "a march of over 50 miles". HEN-II: "Jackson would march fifty miles in two days".
- Verdict: route OK; distance ~50-56 miles over 25-26 Aug; hours disputed (34/36/48); "54 in 36" rests on HistoryNet alone.

### 11. Manassas Junction
- NPS-2M sec 3: "Jackson quickly selected two regiments under the command of Issac Trimble to capture the junction. After moving forward, the two regiments quickly seized the depot and 300 prisoners." WP: Trimble's brigade "with cavalry support"; "capturing over 300 prisoners and eight cannons". NPS article: "accomplished with little effort in the early morning hours of August 27th".
- Eyewitnesses (NPS-2M sec 3): Maj. W. Roy Mason: "Jackson's first order was to knock out the heads of hundreds of barrels of whiskey, wine, and brandy. I shall never forget the scene. Streams of spirits ran like water through the sands of Manassas". Pvt John H. Worsham, 21st Va.: "all the delicacies, potted ham, lobster, tongue, candy, cakes, nuts, oranges, lemons, pickles, catsup, mustard, etc." Chaplain James B. Sheeran, 14th La.: "Just imagine about 6000 men hungry and almost naked, let loose on some million dollars worth of biscuit, cheese, ham, bacon, messpork, coffee, sugar, tea, fruit, brandy, wine, whiskey, oysters, coats, pants, shirts, caps, boots, shoes, socks, blankets, tents, etc."
- NPS "August 28, 1862" page: "An eyewitness wrote, 'To see a starving man eating lobster salad & drinking rhine wine, barefooted & in tatters was curious; the whole thing is indescribable.'" (eyewitness not named on that page).
- HEN-II: "another, whose butternut homespun hung round him in tatters, crammed himself with lobster salad, sardines, potted game and sweetmeats, and washed them down with Rhenish wine."
- Burning: NPS-2M: "That night, with Pope's army closing in on Manassas Junction, Jackson's men set fire to the remaining supplies." (27 Aug night). NPS article: "What could not be eaten or carried away was finally put to the torch."
- Value: only Sheeran's "some million dollars worth". Verdict OK; present as "an eyewitness guessed a million dollars".

### 12. Position, Brawner Farm
- NPS-2M sec 3: Stony Ridge, "the cuts and fills of an unfinished railroad running along the base of Stony Ridge formed a ready-made entrenchment"; Taliaferro's division began the march 9 pm 27 Aug.
- Deliberate attack: NPS "August 28" page: "To allow them to pass might well forestall the main objective of the campaign, which was to bring Pope to battle before McClellan's reinforcements could reach him. ... Jackson hesitated only a moment. Then with the softly spoken order, 'Bring out your men, gentlemen,' he hurled units of Gen. William Taliaferro's division, later reinforced by Ewell's troops, at the 'Black Hat Brigade' of Gen. John Gibbon." NPS Second Manassas page: "to prevent the Federal commander's efforts to concentrate at Centreville and bring Pope to battle, Jackson ordered his troops to attack". ABT: "Jackson strikes a lone Union division on the Warrenton Turnpike".
- Units: King's division; Gibbon's "Black Hat" Brigade (2nd, 6th, 7th Wis., 19th Ind.); the "Iron Brigade" name came after South Mountain (14 Sep 1862), so "later Iron Brigade" is right. ("later" is my knowledge; NPS calls it the "Black Hat Brigade".) Time: shots ~5:30-6:30 pm.
- Casualties: NPS Brawner page: "Nearly one-third of the 7,000 troops engaged became casualties". WP: "over 1,150 Union and 1,250 Confederate casualties. The 2nd Wisconsin lost 276 of 430 engaged. The Stonewall Brigade lost 340 out of 800 ... one of every three men engaged in the fight was shot." NPS-2M sec 4: Ewell "wound at the Brawner farm required that his left leg be amputated"; "Three Northern bullets found Taliaferro"; one of Lawton's regiments 72 percent casualties.

### 13. Pope
- NPS article (27 Aug evening): "Pope, now convinced that Jackson's entire command was at Manassas, issued new orders ... He then boasted, 'If you will march promptly and rapidly at the earliest dawn of day upon Manassas Junction we shall bag the whole crowd.'"
- HEN-II: "'We shall bag the whole crowd, if they are prompt and expeditious,' said Pope" (Henderson footnote OR vol. XII pt II p. 72). The two printed texts differ; the OR original is the dispatch to McDowell/Sigel/others, 27 Aug night. Use only "bag the whole crowd" unless the OR page is read.
- NPS-2M sec 4: "Worst of all, Pope's presumption that Jackson was attempting to retreat could not have been further from the truth."
- Pope to Halleck, dawn 30 Aug (HEN-II, ch. "Second Manassas"; OR not read): "We fought a terrific battle here yesterday with the combined forces of the enemy, which lasted with continuous fury from daylight until dark, by which time the enemy was driven from the field, which we now occupy. The enemy is still in our front, but badly used up. We lost not less than 8,000 men killed and wounded, but from the appearance of the field the enemy lost at least two to one. The news has just reached me from the front that the enemy is retreating towards the mountains."
- ABT: Pope "Mistakenly convinced that the Confederates are retreating ... orders a pursuit near midday" on 30 Aug.

### 14. 29 Aug
- NPS-2M sec 4: Longstreet's "25,000 men, began their march from Thoroughfare Gap at 6:00 A.M. August 29"; sec 5: "By noon the Confederates completed their arrangements." "Porter and McDowell knew full well that Longstreet had arrived ... But for some inexplicable reason McDowell failed to forward Buford's report to Pope". ABT: "Late in the morning, Lee arrives on the field with Longstreet's command, taking position on Jackson's right". NPS overview: "During the afternoon, Longstreet's troops arrived on the battlefield and, unknown to Pope, deployed on Jackson's right". NPS-2M: Pope's Joint Order assumed Longstreet was "thirty-six to forty-eight hours away".
- Union attacks: Sigel late morning, Grover 3 pm, Kearny 5 pm, Hood's clash 7 pm (NPS-2M contents). ABT: "the attackers are repulsed each time"; Lee wanted Longstreet to attack, Longstreet "convinces the Confederate commander to hold off."

### 15. 30 Aug
- Deep Cut (NPS marker page): "On the afternoon of 30 August, 1862, Union General John Pope ordered an attack ... Some 8,000 soldiers moved out of the woods along Featherbed Lane"; inscription: "3 p.m. ... More than 6,000 Union soldiers under General Fitz John Porter ... Opposing troops, separated only by the width of the protective embankment, held their muskets horizontally above their heads and fired blindly. Many soldiers resorted to throwing rocks. ... In less than an hour, the largest Federal attack of Second Manassas had failed." Colonel Bradley T. Johnson, CSA: "The whole field was covered with a confused mass of struggling, running, routed Yankees."
- Rocks, who: NPS-2M sec 5: "Stafford's and Johnson's brigades had completely expended their ammunition and relied on hurling large rocks across the embankment". WP: "Confederates in Col. Bradley T. Johnson's and Col. Leroy A. Stafford's brigades fired so much that they ran out of ammunition and resorted to throwing large rocks at the 24th New York". NPS overview: Porter's corps "struck Confederates under General Starke division at the 'Deep Cut'" (Stafford's Louisiana brigade was in Starke's division).
- Counterattack: NPS Lincoln Home page: "Longstreet's wing of 28,000 men counterattacked in the largest, simultaneous mass assault of the war." NPS panel (4 p.m.): "Longstreet's wing of the army- nearly 30,000 troops". WP: "Longstreet's wing of 25,000 men in five divisions counterattacked in the largest simultaneous mass assault of the war." ABT: "nearly 30,000 strong". NPS panel: "Only three Federal brigades- fewer than 5,000 men- stood in their path." So the superlative is NPS/WP wording, attribute it.
- End: ABT: "After dark, Pope pulls his beaten army off the field and retreats across Bull Run" (Stone Bridge; Centreville next).

### 16. Casualties (28-30 Aug)
- ABT: Union 14,462 (1,747 killed, 8,452 wounded, 4,263 missing & captured); Confederate 7,387 (1,096 killed, 6,202 wounded, 89 missing); "Estimated Casualties 21,849".
- WP: Union 14,462 (same split; its citation is the OR return "operations August 16 – September 2, 1862"); Confederate 7,298 = 1,096 killed + 6,202 wounded only (no missing).
- NPS-2M sec 7: "Lee lost 1,300 killed and more than 7,000 wounded during the three days of major fighting, while Pope suffered nearly 10,000 casualties, not counting those captured or missing."
- Not found: 13,800 (Union), 8,350 (Confederate). Verdict CORRECTED: Union ~14,500 (incl. ~4,300 missing/captured), Confederate ~7,300-7,400. Killed+wounded+missing: Union yes; Confederate ABT figure includes 89 missing.

## 4. CHANCELLORSVILLE, MAY 1863

### 17. Strengths
- NPS-CV sec 1: Union "numbered nearly 134,000 men of all arms and could carry 413 artillery pieces"; "The Army of Northern Virginia could muster slightly fewer than 61,000 men in all"; Longstreet "had been detached from the army with the divisions of Major General George E. Pickett and Major General John Bell Hood. Posted in Southside Virginia near Suffolk".
- NPS FRSP: "Lee's 60,000 veterans at Fredericksburg would guard their long river line against 130,000 well-equipped Yankees." ABT: "leaving Lee with only 60,000 troops to confront Hooker's 130,000 men"; ABT infobox "Forces Engaged 154,734: Union 97,382, Confed. 57,352". WP: 133,868 vs 60,298 (OR, "present for duty equipped"). EVA: Lee's army "numbered 61,000".
- Verdict OK. Say "about 130,000 vs 60,000".

### 18. Plan, column, route
- Cracker boxes: HEN-II quoting Hotchkiss: "When I reached those I found Generals Lee and Jackson in conference, each seated on a cracker box, from a pile which had been left there by the Federals the day before." (margin note "3.30 a.m.", 2 May). Dialogue: Lee: "General Jackson, what do you propose to do?" Jackson: "Go around here" ... "With my whole corps" ... "What will you leave me?" "The divisions of Anderson and McLaws." Lee: "Well, go on". NPS FRSP: the generals sat on cracker boxes on the evening of 1 May, "Then, about midnight, Lee's cavalry chief, 'Jeb' Stuart, galloped up" with news that the flank was "in the air"; "Before dawn, Lee and Jackson studied a hastily drawn map and decided to undertake one of the biggest gambles". So the evening camp-stool talk and the pre-dawn map decision are two moments.
- Column: NPS-CV sec 4: "sending 28,000 men of Jackson's Second Corps ... With the brigades under Anderson and McLaws—roughly 13,000 men supported by 24 guns". NPS FRSP: "Jackson's corps, about 30,000 troops ... Lee, with the remaining 14,000 infantry". ABT: "nearly 30,000 men ... Lee with only about 15,000". WP: "Second Corps of 28,000 men ... about 13,000 men and 24 guns". ABT "10 Facts": "Jackson's 30,000 men". So 26,000-33,000 is wider than any source; 28,000-30,000 is the supported range.
- Route: NPS FRSP: "Jackson led his column ... down the Furnace Road ... After about one mile ... Union scouts perched in treetops at Hazel Grove spotted the marchers"; "twice turning away from the Union line - first at Catharine Furnace, then again at the Brock Road"; "Jackson kept his column northbound on the Brock Road to the Orange Turnpike"; "The exhausting march, which altogether traversed more, than 12 miles, ended about 3 p.m." EVA: "Jackson would march his entire corps twelve miles". HEN-II: "There were three halts during the march of fourteen miles" (fn, letter from Major Hotchkiss). WP: "a 12-mile (19 km) march" and "started between 7 and 8 a.m. and lasted until midafternoon". WP says Jackson was prepared to turn right on the Plank Road but it led "to the wrong place"; he continued on the Brock Road (Fitzhugh Lee's reconnaissance). CORRECTED: not via the Orange Plank Road to the Turnpike; via Brock Road to the Turnpike.
- Attack time: NPS-CV sec 5: "between 5:15 and 6:00 P.M. Jackson checked his watch, then looked at Robert Rodes ... 'Are you ready, General Rodes?' ... 'Yes, sir' ... 'You can go forward then.'" EVA: "at about five thirty in the afternoon"; NPS FRSP: waited ~2 hours after deploying at ~3 pm.

### 19. Union reaction
- NPS FRSP: Hazel Grove scouts; "'Fighting Joe' ... advised the area commander, Major General Oliver O. Howard, to be on the lookout for an attack from the west. As the morning progressed, however, the Union chief grew to believe that Lee was actually withdrawing". Sickles: "In mid-afternoon the Federals overwhelmed Jackson's rearguard beyond the furnace along the cut of an unfinished railroad, capturing nearly an entire Georgia regiment"; "attracted some 20,000 Bluecoats onto the scene thus effectively isolating Howard's Eleventh Corps". WP names the 23rd Georgia, "overwhelmed by 5 p.m. and most were captured".
- Hooker 9:30 am (WP, OR cited): "We have good reason to suppose the enemy is moving to our right. Please advance your pickets for purposes of observation as far as may be safe in order to obtain timely information of their approach." Howard replied 10:50 am he was "taking measures". NPS-CV sec 4: "Shortly after nine o'clock, a courier from Birney found Hooker".
- 4:10 pm (HEN-II, ch. "Chancellorsville"; Henderson footnotes OR vol. XXV pt II, page not confirmed here): "Chancellorsville, May 2, 1863, 4.10 p.m. General Butterfield, — The Major-General Commanding directs that General Sedgwick cross the river as soon as indications will permit, capture Fredericksburg with everything in it, and vigorously pursue the enemy. We know that the enemy is fleeing, trying to save his trains. Two of Sickles' divisions are among them. J. H. Van Alen, Brigadier-General and Aide-de-Camp." Henderson adds: "(Copy from Butterfield, at Falmouth, to Sedgwick, 5.50 P.M.)". Not Hooker's own signature. Verify the OR page before quoting on screen.

### 20. Howard's corps
- Stone Sentinels (marker text): "Its 11,000 men included a large percentage of German immigrants"; "Union pickets had warned Howard of the enemy's approach, but he had ignored their reports. Headquarters had assured him the Confederate army was in retreat." NPS-CV sec 5: "Nearly 2,500 casualties (roughly 25 percent of its strength)", i.e. ~10,000. 12,000-13,000 not found.
- NPS FRSP: "Only two small regiments and half a New York battery faced west in the direction of Jackson's corps." WP: Col. Leopold von Gilsa's brigade (Devens's division) put "the 153rd Pennsylvania and 54th New York ... up as a heavy skirmish line"; "The pair of artillery pieces at the end of the XI Corps line were captured". NPS-CV: "Only five regiments faced the oncoming Rebels."

### 21. Animals
- NPS-CV sec 5: "a wave of terrified animals rolled eastward in front of the advancing human tide. Startled Federals at first pointed and laughed as rabbits and deer scattered through their lines—then frantically sought to form when they realized why the beasts had burst from the woods."
- Marker text on Stone Sentinels: "As his men struggled through the tangled woods, they drove deer, rabbits, and turkeys ahead of them. The frightened animals bounded into the Union camps, causing the Northern soldiers to cheer."
- WP: "Most of the men of the XI Corps were encamped and sitting down for supper and had their rifles unloaded and stacked. Their first clue ... was the observation of numerous animals, such as rabbits and foxes, fleeing in their direction". No named witness in any of these. Searched B&L-3 and Henderson: nothing. Verdict: usable as "park historians say"; UNSUPPORTED as a quoted eyewitness. Howard's 1880s B&L article was not checked for this.

### 22. Advance
- ABT: "push the Northern army back more than two miles". HEN-II quotes Jackson's report: "westward along the Germanna Ford road (the pike) more than two miles. ... As the nature of the country rendered it hazardous to attack by night, our troops were halted". NPS-CV: "Rodes's division, badly disorganized in victory, halted at 7:15 near a set of abandoned works ... roughly a mile west of Chancellorsville"; NPS FRSP: "Sunset and the inevitable intermingling of 'Stonewall's' brigades compelled Jackson to call a reluctant halt to the advance about 7:15."

### 23. The wounding
- Time: NPS-CV sec 6, Lt J. G. Morrison (Confederate Veteran): "It was now nine o'clock". EVA timeline: "May 2, 1863, 9:30 p.m." HistoryNet: "At 9:30 on the night of May 2, 1863 ... nervous soldiers of the 18th North Carolina Infantry ... struck almost simultaneously by three shots, twice in the left arm and once in the right hand." NPS-CV: "A colonel commanding a North Carolina regiment in Lane's Brigade cried out: 'Who gave that order? It's a lie! Pour it into them.'" NPS FRSP: "Two volleys".
- Litter: HEN-II: "one of the stretcher-bearers, struck by a shot in the arm, let go the handle. Jackson fell violently to the ground on his wounded side." NPS-CV (Morrison): the canister "struck down one of the litter bearers, by which the General received a severe fall." Mary Anna Jackson (McGuire's account): "One of the bearers was shot in the arm, and, letting go his hold, the general fell violently to the ground, upon his wounded side, causing such pain that for the first time he was heard to utter a groan." HistoryNet: "he was dropped twice during transport." Verdict: once in the four older/primary-type sources; twice only in HistoryNet; say "dropped".
- Surgery: HEN-II quoting McGuire: "at two o'clock on Sunday morning I informed him that chloroform would be given him ... Chloroform was then administered, and the left arm amputated about two inches below the shoulder." HistoryNet: "By 3 a.m., the surgeries had been completed". EVA timeline: 3 May, taken to Wilderness Tavern where McGuire amputated.
- Hill and Stuart: NPS FRSP: "Shortly thereafter a Federal shell struck Hill, incapacitating him, and direction of the corps devolved upon Stuart."

### 24. Lee's words
- Dabney (1866): "Mr. Lacy, while seeking him, called on General Lee, and told him that the General's condition was more threatening. ... 'Give him,' he added, 'my affectionate regards, and tell him to make haste and get well, and come back to me as soon as he can. He has lost his left arm; but I have lost my right arm.'"
- Mary Anna Jackson (Memoirs/Life and Letters): "Shortly after the general's fall, and before his situation had grown so critical, General Lee sent him, by a friend, the following message: 'Give him my affectionate regards, and tell him to make haste and get well, and come back to me as soon as he can. He has lost his left arm, but I have lost my right arm.'" (Mary Anna's text is next to her remarks about Lacy.)
- HEN-II: "'Give him my affectionate regards,' he said to an aide-de-camp who was riding to the hospital ; 'tell him to make haste and get well, and come back to me as soon as he can. He has lost his left arm, but I have lost my right.' 'Any victory,' he wrote privately, 'would be dear at such a price. I know not how to replace him.'"
- Variants seen: "You are better off than I am, for while you have only lost your LEFT, I have lost my RIGHT arm." (historion.iovan.net, Lee-biography text, to Jackson); to the cook "William ... I have lost my right arm" (HNN, via search snippet only); EVA: Lee to the young officer who brought the news: "Captain, any victory is dearly bought that deprives us of the services of Jackson even temporarily."
- Lee's note to Jackson (HistoryNet, Joe Haines, "Stonewall Jackson's Death"): "'I have just received your note, informing me that you were wounded,' Lee wrote. 'Could I have directed events, I should have chosen, for the good of the country, to have been disabled in your stead. I congratulate you upon the victory, which is due to your skill and energy.'" Variant (historion.iovan.net): "'Could I have directed events,' he wrote the wounded General, 'I should have chosen to have been disabled in your stead. I congratulate you on the victory which is due to your skill and energy.'" The note was sent 3 May (secondary sources only; original not read).
- Verdict: DISPUTED wording. The Lacy link and the "left ... right arm" wording are the best-documented; keep the quote in the Dabney/Mary Anna form or paraphrase.

### 25. Death
- EVA timeline: "May 10, 1863, 3:15 p.m. Thomas J. 'Stonewall' Jackson dies of pneumonia at Fairfield, the home of Thomas and Mary Chandler, having spoken as his last recorded words: 'Let us cross over the river and rest in the shade of the trees.'" ABT: "He died on May 10 at the plantation of Thomas Chandler at Guinea Station of pneumonia". NPS FRSP: "at a small frame building near Guinea, Jackson would be dead." Moved there 4 May (EVA timeline).
- McGuire diagnosis (Mary Anna Memoirs): "An examination disclosed pleuro-pneumonia of the right side. I believed, and the consulting physicians concurred in the opinion, that it was attributable to the fall from the litter the night he was wounded." (OCR "pleuropneumonia"). A modern doctor in HistoryNet (editor's note) argues pulmonary embolism, not pneumonia (opinion).
- Last words wording: Mary Anna Jackson (McGuire's text): "all at once he spoke out very cheerfully and distinctly the beautiful sentence which has become immortal as his last: 'Let us cross over the river, and rest under the shade of the trees.'" HEN-II: "'Let us cross over the river, and rest under the shade of the trees,' and the soul of the great captain passed into the peace of God." HistoryNet quotes McGuire: "'Let us cross over the river and rest under the shade of the trees'". Dabney: "'Let us pass over the river, and rest under the shade of the trees.' These were the last words he uttered." EVA: "in the shade". Before that, delirium: "Order A. P. Hill to prepare for action! Pass the infantry to the front rapidly! Tell Major Hawks--" (HEN-II). Final canonical text (Henderson / Mary Anna / McGuire): "Let us cross over the river, and rest under the shade of the trees."
- McGuire's own 1866 article (Richmond Medical Journal, May 1866, "Last Wound of the Late General Jackson") not found online; Dabney's "pass over" shows the wording was already varying in 1866.

### 26. Casualties
- ABT: Union 17,304 (1,694 killed, 9,672 wounded, 5,938 missing & captured); Confederate 13,460 (1,724 killed, 9,233 wounded, 2,503 missing & captured). EVA: Union "lost 17,304 men"; Lee's "casualty total ... more than 13,000". WP: campaign Union 17,287 (1,606/9,762/6,919); Confederate 12,764 (1,665/9,081/2,018). Percent of Lee's army: 13,460/61,000 = 22%; 12,764/60,298 = 21%.
- All include killed + wounded + missing/captured; the figures cover the whole campaign including Second Fredericksburg and Salem Church.

## 5. BIOGRAPHY / MYTHS

### 27. Biography
- Born 21 Jan 1824 Clarksburg, (West) Virginia, third child of Jonathan Jackson and Julia Beckwith Neale Jackson (EVA timeline; ABT; HEN-I). WP note: the physician recalled delivering him just before midnight on the 20th, "but the family has insisted since then that he was born in the first minutes of January 21" (the WP footnote prints the year as 1809, an obvious typo).
- Orphan: HEN-I: "at a very early age our little Virginian found himself a penniless orphan" (mother died 1831); WP: father Jonathan died 1826 (I did not read the passage fully; confirm).
- West Point: EVA: "He graduated seventeenth in rank of fifty-nine cadets in the class of 1846"; McClellan 2nd, Pickett last. HEN-I: "In the final examination he came out seventeenth".
- Mexican War: EVA: "winning renown and promotion to brevet major for his fearless conduct at the Battle of Chapultepec on September 13, 1847". WP: "eventually earning two brevet promotions". ABT: "received brevets to the rank of major".
- VMI: EVA: "professor of natural and experimental philosophy (akin to modern physics) and artillery instructor at the Virginia Military Institute in Lexington"; EVA timeline: June 1851 begins the job. ABT says he resigned his commission and began teaching "In 1852" (ABT and EVA differ by a year: appointment 1851, army resignation effective 1852).
- "Tom Fool": EVA: "More than a few cadets made fun of him and some labeled him 'Tom Fool', but others noted his kindness". "Old Jack": EVA: "Jackson's own soldiers most often called the general 'Old Jack,' a sobriquet dating from West Point".
- "Old Blue Light": WP: "a term applied to a military man whose evangelical zeal burned with the intensity of the blue light used for night-time display"; also the story that soldiers believed his eyes glowed blue when battle began. A New Criterion reply (Tillinghast, citing Kenneth E. Hall 2005) argues the phrase first mocked his Presbyterian piety (search snippet, not read in full). Not in EVA or Henderson. Verdict: origin DISPUTED; avoid asserting one.

### 28. "Stonewall"
- First print (Civil War News, quoting the Charleston Mercury of 25 Jul 1861, attributed to correspondent Leonidas W. Spratt): "There is JACKSON standing like a stone-wall. Let us determine to die here and we will conquer. Follow me!" Same article: other versions include "Yonder stands Jackson like a stone wall" (1891) and Bee's gravestone wording; Cooke (1863) differs from the Mercury.
- HEN-I: "'Look ! there is Jackson standing like a stone wall ! Rally behind the Virginians !'"
- EVA: "Look, men, there is Jackson standing like a stone wall!"
- WP: "There is Jackson standing like a stone wall. Let us determine to die here, and we will conquer. Rally behind the Virginians!"; "There is some controversy over Bee's statement and intent, which could not be clarified because he was mortally wounded almost immediately after speaking and none of his subordinate officers wrote reports of the battle. Major Burnett Rhett, chief of staff to General Joseph E. Johnston, claimed that Bee was angry at Jackson's failure to come immediately to the relief of Bee's and Francis S. Bartow's brigades". WP also cites Freeman, Lee's Lieutenants vol. 1 pp. 733-34 for "additional controversies about what Bee said and whether he said anything at all", and McPherson's "Rally around the Virginians".
- Richmond Dispatch, 8 Feb 1862 (via WebFetch of Civil War Richmond): credits the comparison to Beauregard, not Bee: "See Jackson! He stands firm as a stone-wall!"
- Bee died 22 Jul 1861 (the day after) (my knowledge; not re-sourced here). Verdict DISPUTED: safe line is "Gen. Barnard Bee is said to have shouted..." and show the Mercury 25 Jul 1861 text as the first printed version.

### 29. Second National flag
- confederateflags.org (D. D. Cannon Jr.): "The change in the flag law that lead to the adoption of the 'STAINLESS BANNER' was initiated on 22 April 1863 ... the Act adopted on 1 May 1863". "One of the first examples of the 'STAINLESS BANNER' to be made by the Richmond Depot covered the casket of Lt. Gen. Thomas J. 'Stonewall' Jackson, as his body lay in state at the capitol in Richmond on 12 May 1863." Also: "Shortly after the adoption of the Confederate 2nd national flag on 1 May 1863, the Richmond Examiner (of 15 May 1863) reported on one that was then nearing completion for the capitol". Flags actually made at the depot were 2:3 proportion, not the 1:2 of the Act.
- ECW: "The second national flag was first unveiled May 1, 1863"; the MOC flag "first flew over the Virginia State House ... Then it covered Jackson's casket ... through Jackson's burial in Lexington on May 15."
- Field use on 2 May: no evidence; the first depot flags were made after 1 May and the report of the first display is 14-15 May. The Army of Northern Virginia continued to use the battle flag. Verdict: adoption date OK; "used at Chancellorsville" UNSUPPORTED; "first one made" CORRECTED to "one of the first".
