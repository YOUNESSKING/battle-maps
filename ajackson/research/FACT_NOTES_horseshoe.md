## A. Jackson in 20 seconds

1. Born 15 March 1767 in the Waxhaws region on the Carolina border (exact birthplace uncertain). Source: WP Andrew Jackson (Hermitage/NPS agree on date).
2. As a boy he served the Patriot side in the Revolution as a courier; captured with his brother Robert by the British in April 1781. When he refused to polish a British officer's boots the officer slashed him with a sword, leaving scars on his left hand and head. AGE: he was 14 (born March 1767, event April 1781); popular accounts say 13, one NPG page says 15. Safest: "as a teenager" or "at 14". The story comes from later retellings, not from Jackson himself. Sources: WP; Britannica; NPG; rootsweb Waxhaw page (all via search). Brother Robert died soon after, their mother also died in 1781 [verify, general knowledge].
3. Admitted to the North Carolina bar in September 1787; practised law on the Tennessee frontier. Source: WP.
4. Elected major general of the Tennessee militia in 1802 (decided by a tie-break by Gov. Archibald Roane). Source: WP.
5. "Old Hickory": won in 1813 when he led about 2,000 Tennessee volunteers to Natchez to guard against a British threat to New Orleans, was ordered to disband them with no pay or transport, refused to abandon them, spent his own money, gave up his horses for the sick and walked back along the Natchez Trace to Tennessee beside his men; they said he was tough as hickory. Exact date of the nickname not given (1813 expedition). Sources: NPS Natchez Trace "Andrew Jackson Gains His Nicknames" https://home.nps.gov/natr/learn/historyculture/andrew-jackson-gains-his-nicknames.htm ; NCpedia; World History Encyclopedia. The Creek nickname "Sharp Knife" came later, from the harsh peace (NPS Natchez page).
6. Jackson was recovering from gunshot wounds from a September 1813 brawl with the Benton brothers when the Creek War landed on him [verify, general knowledge; Remini].

## B. Creek War origins (1811-Feb 1814)

7. A civil war inside the Muscogee (Creek) Nation first. Historian John K. Mahon: "as much a civil war among Creeks as between red and white". The Red Sticks (Upper Creeks, militant, resisting US encroachment and agent Benjamin Hawkins's "civilization" programme) vs the Lower Creeks who were pro-American. Tecumseh's 1811 visit to the Tukabatchee council encouraged the Red Sticks. Name: many scholars say from red-painted war clubs. Sources: WP Creek War; Battlefields Trust; NPS.
8. Burnt Corn, 27 Jul 1813: territorial militia (Col. James Caller, Maj. Daniel Beasley) ambushed a Red Stick pack train returning from Spanish Florida (Pensacola) with supplies; the warriors rallied and drove them off. Source: WP Fort Mims, Creek War; Battlefields Trust.
9. Fort Mims, 30 Aug 1813: Red Sticks under Peter McQueen and William Weatherford (Red Eagle), 700-1,000 warriors, stormed a fortified homestead 35-40 mi north of Mobile held by ~265 militia plus civilians (~517-553 people inside, including mixed-heritage Creek families and enslaved people). Deaths: DISPUTED. Encyclopedia of Alabama ~250 defenders and civilians dead plus ~100 captives; WP says estimates of 400-500 killed in the Creek War article and 265 militia + 252 civilians "killed or captured" in the Fort Mims article; relief column counted 247 bodies; Red Stick losses 50-300. Safest on screen: "hundreds killed" or "over 250". The Red Sticks also took 100+ enslaved people captive. Sources: WP Fort Mims; EoA Creek War; Battlefields Trust; AHC. The attack made the war a war with the United States.
10. Tallushatchee, 3 Nov 1813: Gen. John Coffee's mounted Tennesseans destroyed a Red Stick band (186 Red Sticks killed per Battlefields infobox; US 46 casualties). Talladega, 9 Nov 1813: Jackson relieved friendly Creeks besieged at Lashley's/Leslie's Fort; ~300 Red Sticks killed; US 15 killed ~85 wounded (WP, conflicting 17). Sources: WP; EoA; Battlefields.
11. Winter crisis: supplies failed, enlistments expired, troops mutinous. East Tennessee militia and volunteers went home in Dec 1813; a draft of raw recruits in January 1814 left a small cadre. Jackson warned that "any officer or soldier who flies before the enemy... shall suffer death" (NPS-CLR p.19). Source: NPS-CLR; WP.
12. Emuckfaw Creek, 22 Jan 1814 (Jackson routed a band of Red Sticks but could not take the fortified town; he also noted the Tohopeka breastwork was too strong to break in January) and Enitachopco (Enotachopo) Creek, 24 Jan 1814 (a mauling in retreat; indecisive). Sources: NPS-CLR p.19-20 (dates), WP Creek War.
13. The 39th US Infantry (regulars, Col. John Williams), previously headed for New Orleans, arrived at Fort Strother 6 Feb 1814 at the urging of Gov. Blount and Judge Hugh Lawson White; fresh Tennessee levies followed; Jackson now had a disciplined core. Sources: WP Creek War (6 Feb); NPS-CLR p.20.
14. Fort Williams (Coosa River, Cedar Creek) built ~22 Mar 1814 as the staging depot; army staged from 24 Mar; 52-mile march through wilderness from Fort Williams began 26 Mar. Sources: WP Fort Williams; NPS-CLR p.20.

## C. Horseshoe Bend, 27 March 1814

15. The place: Tohopeka ("Tehopeka", "Tohopiska"), a bend of the Tallapoosa River; Jackson: "This bend resembles in its curvature that of a horse shoe". Peninsula about 80-100 acres by Jackson's conjecture (J-BLOUNT; NR-1975 "about 100 acres" - note NHD geometry suggests the whole loop is larger). Refugee village of ~300 log huts housing Red Sticks from Okfuskee, Oakchaya, Newyaucau, Hillabee, Fish Pond and Eufaula; they did not evacuate women and children (NPS-CLR p.19; NR-1975; EoA). Faction names: both sides' stories need "Red Stick Creeks / Muscogee" and "Cherokee and Lower Creek allies".
16. The breastwork: logs laid horizontally (several large logs high) with clay/earth chinking, "from five to eight feet high, and prepared with double rows of port-holes very artfully arranged" (J-BLOUNT quoted in NPS-CLR p.19). Zig-zag/angled so attackers faced a double and cross fire ("an army could not approach it without being exposed to a double and cross fire"). Period maps show it angled and concave to the south, "five large logs with two ranges of port holes", fraised, with a trench and an earth bank on the southern side (Waselkov, in NPS-CLR p.20 n.76). Length: NR-1975 "some 250 yards"; NPS-CLR "300-400 yards" "across the narrowest portion"; EoA and WP "nearly 400 yards" ("eighty poles" = 440 yd in Jackson). SAFEST: "about 350 metres / nearly 400 yards". Built from December 1813 (NPS-CLR p.19). It stood in the north neck; Tohopeka lay at the southernmost tip, behind it. Located by archaeology in 1964 and 1973 (Dickens); the park marks it with white posts.
17. Red Stick defenders: Jackson's prisoners said ~1,000 warriors in the bend (J-BLOUNT); NPS-CLR says ~1,350 Red Sticks and families on the eve; EoA ~1,000 warriors plus at least 350 women and children, and only about a third had a gun. Leaders: Menawa (second chief of Okfuskee, "Hothlepoya"; born c.1765, a mixed-heritage Creek) with the prophet Monahee (killed, per J-BLOUNT) and Peter McQueen (WP lists him; presence not confirmed). SAFEST: "about 1,000 warriors".
18. Jackson's force: SAFEST "about 3,000". Breakdown: Jackson led ~2,000 infantry (39th US Infantry, East and West Tennessee militia) and two guns; Coffee ~700 mounted riflemen plus ~600 Indian allies (500 Cherokee, 100 friendly Lower Creek) (EoA; NPS-CLR p.20). Total ~3,300 (EoA, Battlefields); WP gives 2,700 + 600. The ~500 Lower Creek of the brief is NOT supported: sources give ~100 (EoA, NPS-CLR). Jackson's allies were told to wear white deer tails to be told from Red Sticks (NPS "Courting victory").
19. Timeline (all 27 Mar 1814): army left camp ~6 mi north at 06:30; Coffee's column sent to cross "two miles below the bend" at an island ford and ring the loop so "none of them should escape by attempting to cross the river" (J-PINCKNEY); Bean with 40 men on the island due west of the peninsula (COFFEE). ~10:30 Jackson opened with one 6-pounder and one 3-pounder from a small eminence (Gun Hill), "distant from its nearest point about eighty yards, and from its farthest, about two hundred and fifty" (J-BLOUNT); J-PINCKNEY says 150-200 yd; Carroll map shows first line ~400 yd north for 2 h, second 300 yd for 1.5 h. The cannonade lasted ~2 h with little effect on the logs.
20. The Cherokee swim: three Cherokees led by Tuq-qua, "The Whale", swam the ~120-yard river, took Red Stick dugout canoes and ferried others; Capt. William Russell's spies, Col. Gideon Morgan's Cherokee, Maj. John Walker and ~30 others followed; about 200 Cherokee crossed (NPS-CLR p.21; NPS-KNIFE; EoA). Lower Creek and Cherokee burned Tohopeka from behind. "Charles Reese" is NOT supported by any source consulted here; do not use. Colonels Gideon Morgan and Richard Brown led the Cherokee; Maj. William McIntosh (Coweta) the Lower Creeks; the Cherokee John Ross served as an adjutant (NPS). Coffee: seeing ~100 warriors and the women and children among the huts "they could no longer remain silent spectators" (COFFEE).
21. The assault: Jackson saw smoke and heard firing at ~12:30 and ordered the charge on a drum roll; the militia and the 39th "hailed with acclamation" the order. "Muzzle to muzzle, through the port-holes, in which many of the enemy's balls were welded to the bayonets of our musquets" (J-BLOUNT in NPS-CLR p.21). Maj. Lemuel Purnell Montgomery (39th) was killed at the wall (NPS-CLR; his grave is on Gun Hill); Lts. Somerville and Moulton also killed; Col. Williams led the regulars (J-BLOUNT). 3rd Lt. Sam Houston, 39th Infantry, was among the first over, took an arrow in the thigh, and later two rifle balls in the shoulder (NPS-CLR p.21; WP).
22. Fighting lasted over five hours (J-PINCKNEY, WP) until dark, resumed next morning with searching; Jackson's men killed concealed warriors for hours. The Red Sticks fought "to the last with that kind of bravery desperation inspires" (NPS park page quote from Jackson). Jackson: "impossible to conceive a situation more eligible for defense" (Battlefields Trust).
23. Red Stick dead: 557 counted on the peninsula (J-BLOUNT; J-PINCKNEY) plus ~200-300 shot in the river (Coffee: "from two hundred and fifty to three hundred", NPS-CLR p.25 n.86); Jackson: "at least 800". Others: "more than 800" (EoA, NPS), 900 (NR-1975). SAFEST: "more than 800 killed (557 counted on the field)". Prisoners: ~350 women and children (EoA; J-BLOUNT "over 300", J-PINCKNEY ~250 early). The count was made by cutting off noses (WP); soldiers also took bridle reins and clothing as trophies (WP). Say at most: "the dead were counted by cutting off their noses". Do not dwell or show it.
24. Jackson's side (J-BLOUNT): white troops (militia and 39th) 26 killed 107 wounded; Cherokee 18 killed 36 wounded; friendly Creeks 5 killed 11 wounded; total 49 killed 154 wounded (matches EoA). The 39th: 17 killed 55 wounded, included in the 26/107. (J-PINCKNEY first estimate: 25 killed, 106 wounded among whites.) WP alt: 47 killed/159 wounded. SAFEST: "49 killed, 154 wounded among Jackson's army and allies".
25. Menawa: wounded seven times, lay among the dead until dark, crawled to the river and escaped by canoe with a small band toward the Seminole in Florida (EoA; Battlefields; WP says ~20 of 1,000 escaped). He returned and lived on the Tallapoosa until 1836; removed west (~1836-40) (WP Menawa; EoA). His birthplace Oakfuskee is now under Lake Martin.
26. Effect: it effectively ended organised Red Stick resistance; some hostile groups held out for months; survivors fled to Pensacola and the Seminole, others starved in the cane swamps (NPS-CLR p.26). Coffee withdrew and Jackson returned to Fort Williams, then moved to the Coosa-Tallapoosa confluence ("Hickory Ground"), where Fort Jackson was built on the old French Fort Toulouse site in April 1814 (WP Fort Toulouse; NPS-CLR).

## D. Aftermath and the honest lines

27. Promotion: commissioned major general in the US Army (a regular, May 1814; 28 May [verify]) and given command of the 7th Military District (Gulf coast: Mobile, Pensacola, New Orleans) after Pinckney's recommendation (WP; EoA).
28. Treaty of Fort Jackson, 9 Aug 1814: Jackson alone dictated the terms; the Creek National Council (28 towns represented) ceded nearly 22 million acres (NPS) / about 23 million (LoC, WP, NPS Natchez), roughly half of the Creek Nation's land, in central Alabama and southern Georgia. Jackson claimed it as payment for an "unprovoked, inhuman, and sanguinary" war and held the whole nation responsible; the LOWER CREEK ALLIES who fought for him also lost land (allied headmen could only claim one-square-mile reservations, few were granted; the Creek National Council protested and said only a faction attacked; 1962 Indian Claims Commission awarded ~$4M for a ~9 million-acre disputed tract). The Creeks also lost their links with Spanish Florida and Britain; the US gained rights to roads, posts and trading houses. Cherokee claimed 1.9 million acres of the land (WP). Red Sticks who fought received no compensation. Sources: NPS https://www.nps.gov/articles/treaty-of-fort-jackson.htm ; EoA; LoC; NPS Ocmulgee PDFs.
29. Why the Gulf mattered: Britain and Spain could use the coast (Pensacola, Mobile, Apalachicola, New Orleans); Red Sticks who fled to Pensacola were British-friendly; Jackson's 7th Military District was the shield for the lower Mississippi, setting up Mobile/Pensacola (Nov 1814) and New Orleans (8 Jan 1815) (WP; EoA). British landing at Pensacola Aug 1814 [verify].
30. Later: Jackson became president in 1828; he signed the Indian Removal Act in 1830 (28 May [verify date]), passed narrowly (Senate 28-19), which displaced tens of thousands of Native Americans; the Creeks ceded their remaining Alabama land by treaty on 24 Mar 1832 and were forcibly removed in 1836-37; the Cherokee were driven west on the Trail of Tears in 1838-39 (thousands died, ~4,000 estimated [verify]). Sources: NPS Ocmulgee https://www.nps.gov/ocmu/learn/historyculture/upload/Accessible-Muscogee-Creek-Removal.pdf ; WP Andrew Jackson; EoA.
31. Jackson was an enslaver: 9 enslaved people in 1804, over 100 by 1820, over 150 at his death in 1845, about 300 over his lifetime, at the Hermitage plantation near Nashville (WP Andrew Jackson). (Menawa's 1825 reprisals killed William McIntosh over the Indian Springs treaty, with ~100 enslaved people seized: WP Menawa. Not needed for the film.)

## On-screen numbers (safest)
- Born 1767; Old Hickory 1813; Creek War 1813-14.
- Fort Mims 30 Aug 1813: "hundreds killed" (or "over 250").
- Horseshoe Bend, 27 Mar 1814, Tohopeka bend of the Tallapoosa.
- About 3,000 under Jackson, including about 600 Cherokee and Lower Creek allies (500 + 100).
- About 1,000 Red Stick warriors under Menawa; breastwork nearly 400 yards, 5-8 feet high, double portholes.
- Two guns (6-pounder + 3-pounder) from 10:30 for about two hours; charge at about 12:30 on the drum.
- "557 counted on the field, more than 800 killed in all"; about 350 women and children captured.
- Jackson's side: 49 killed, 154 wounded (26/107 white troops, 18/36 Cherokee, 5/11 Lower Creek).
- 9 Aug 1814 Treaty of Fort Jackson: about 22 million acres (often cited as 23 million), roughly half of the Creek lands, including allies' land.

## Suggested sensitivity lines (military story first, one clause each)
- Name the sides: "Red Stick Creeks, one faction of a divided Muscogee Nation" vs "Jackson's army and its Cherokee and Lower Creek allies".
- At the aftermath beat (move end card): "Nearly 22 million acres were taken, including land of the Creeks who fought beside Jackson." Then "Jackson later signed the Indian Removal Act; he also enslaved people at the Hermitage." Never celebrate the killing; show the count as cost.

## Myths to avoid
- "Jackson's report of 28 March to Blount": the Blount letter is 31 March; 28 March is the Pinckney letter.
- "Charles Reese" as the Cherokee swimmer: unsupported; Tuq-qua ("The Whale") leads the three NPS names.
- "500 Lower Creeks": sources say ~100 (some secondary sources are higher).
- Casualty figures of 276/1,339/1,063 (Battlefields Trust infobox) or "23 killed, 47 wounded" Native allies (WP infobox): inconsistent aggregates; use Jackson's figures.
- The Congressional monument on Gun Hill carries the wrong date (29 March); the battle was 27 March (NPS-CLR p.49).
- The sword scar at "13" is a popular figure; he was 14; do not tell the boot dialogue as fact.
- Horseshoe Bend did not end the war with Britain or Spain; it ended the Creek War.
- "The Red Sticks' flag": none documented; do not invent one. Period flags: US 15-star/15-stripe flag (1795-1818); British Union Flag of 1801 (incorporating St Patrick's saltire); British Army regimental colours = king's colour (Union) + regimental colour (facing colour) [general knowledge]; allies wore white deer tails.
- Lake Martin / modern reservoirs: the bend is NOT flooded; draw the plain river (see geo_horseshoe.json notes).
- Do not call the battle a "massacre" without context, and do not use "savages" (that word is in Jackson's period letters; leave it out). Do not show the nose-count or trophy-taking.
